# HM3D-M 3: A Benchmark for Multi-Agent, Multi-Modal, Multi-Object Goal Navigation 

Hongyu Ding 1,4,∗, Kanyu Xu 2,∗, Mingjia Chen 3, Zixuan Chen 2,Jieqi Shi 2,†, Jing Huo 1,†, Yifan Zhang 4,†, Yang Gao 2

Abstract — Rapid progress in AI agents and robotics makes it a matter of the near future that humans are served by a team of robots. Such a team will receive several goals at once, each specified in whatever form is convenient for the human, such as a category name, a photo, or a spoken description. Existing benchmarks study multi-agent, multi-modal, and multi-object goal navigation in isolation , and a benchmark that places all three in one episode is missing. To fill this gap, we introduce HM3D-M 3. A team of K agents starts at distributed locations in a photorealistic indoor scene and searches for an unordered set of M object goals, each given as an open-vocabulary category, an instance image, or an instance description. HM3D-M 3 provides 1,000 validation episodes over 3,926 goals from 379 open-vocabulary categories, 100K observation–assignment training samples, and two team-level metrics, goal-level SR and makespan-normalized SPL. We further contribute two baselines. HiGA is a trained hierarchical goal assigner on top of a frozen open-vocabulary navigation policy, and LaViRA-M 3 is a training-free team of VLM agents with shared mapping and progress analysis. Adapted multi-robot and zero-shot methods reach 16–27% SR, while our baselines reach 38–41% with complementary strengths and distinct failure modes. Furthermore, we deploy LaViRA-M 3

on a heterogeneous three-robot team. Project page: https: //robo-lavira.github.io/hm3d-m3/ 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Consider a home with several robots and a resident who asks them to find a nightstand, the couch in a photo, and “the plant by the sliding glass door”. Nothing in this request is exotic. Rapid progress in AI agents and robot hardware places such teams within the near future, and the request has the shape everyday instructions take: several goals at once, each given in whatever form is at hand. What is missing is a way to measure how well a team handles it, since the goals have to be divided, recognized under three different specifications, and tracked jointly. Multi-agent, Multi-modal, Multi-object goal navigation in photorealistic scans of real buildings [15], [16], illustrated in Fig. 1. An episode places K agents at distributed, jointly reachable start positions and gives the team an unordered set of M goals from 379 open-vocabulary categories. Each goal is specified as a category (ON), an instance image (IIN), or an instance description (TN). A goal counts as found when an agent declares it while standing within 1 m of that instance, and every episode stores the oracle team makespan d∗ for budgeting and normalization. We further contribute two baselines, one trained and one training-free. HiGA, short for Hierarchical Goal Assigner, is a trained method in which a transformer reads the shared map, agent poses, and per-goal CLIP heat maps, assigns goals to agents, and hands each assignment to a frozen open-vocabulary navigation policy [6]. It is trained with behavior cloning and DAgger [17] from an oracle assigner. LaViRA-M3 is a training-free method that extends the vision-language model (VLM) navigation agent of LaViRA [18], [19] to a team coordinated only through a shared map, a shared goal board, and shared natural-language progress analysis. We also adapt four existing multi-robot and zero-shot meth-ods [11], [13], [20], [21] to the benchmark. The results expose a task that is far from solved, with adapted methods at 16–27% SR, HiGA at 37.9%, and LaViRA-M 3 at 41.0%, and two baselines with complemen-tary strengths. HiGA is the strongest on category goals but weak on image goals and inefficient, whereas LaViRA-M 3 is the strongest on image and text goals and the most efficient. Their failures differ, since LaViRA-M 3 mostly declares a goal from the wrong place, whereas HiGA mostly fails to detect or match it. Our contributions are:

Goal navigation benchmarks. Single-agent benchmarks have progressively enriched how a goal is specified, from a closed set of categories [1], [2] to an open vocabulary [6], and to a particular instance shown in an image [5] or described in language [7]. Multi-object variants ask one agent for several goals, in a fixed order [3] or as an unordered set [10], recently across several floors [9], and lifelong multi-modal navigation chains 5–10 goals of mixed modality into one episode with per-sub-task scoring [7], [22]. The one multi-agent benchmark among them, Habitat-MAS [8], targets language-specified household tasks for heterogeneous teams, with multi-object navigation limited to a closed vo-cabulary and a single modality. HM3D-M 3 inherits the open vocabulary and the three goal modalities of this line, and adds a team of agents, an unordered goal set searched jointly, and a per-episode team optimum. 

Multi-robot semantic navigation. After early work in syn-thetic scenes [4], recent systems on photorealistic scans let a VLM allocate frontiers to robots [11], [12], and LLM agents have been coordinated on unordered multi-object missions in a single modality on small custom episode sets [23]. Closest to our task are two very recent systems that run robot teams on multi-modal, multi-goal missions. DM 3-Nav [13] is decentralized, with agents that exchange local maps, task states, and intents within communication range and claim goals by broadcasting them, and it introduces a makespan-normalized SPL. AnyGoal [14] is a training-free team in which a VLM guides exploration toward category, image, and language goals presented sequentially, as in GOAT-Bench. Both are methods rather than benchmarks, evaluated on single-agent episodes with sub-task scoring, and neither defines a multi-agent episode distribution, a team budget, or team metrics, which HM3D-M 3 supplies. Trained and training-free navigation policies. Trained object-goal policies range from end-to-end RL and imita-tion [24] to the DAgger-trained open-vocabulary policy [6] that we use frozen as the lower level of HiGA. Training-free methods pair explicit maps with foundation models [20], [25], unify category, image, and text goals by graph match-ing [26], or let an LLM or VLM act as the navigation agent itself [18], [27]. LaViRA [18] and Uni-LaViRA [19] decompose each decision into a language, a vision, and a robot action. LaViRA-M 3 extends them to a team. III. T HE HM3D-M 3 BENCHMARK 

A. Task Definition 

An episode of HM3D-M 3 is a tuple (S, {sk}Kk=1 , {gj }Mj=1 )

with a scene S, start poses sk for K agents, and M goals. Each goal gj = ( oj , m j , x j ) refers to an object instance 

oj of a distinct category and is presented to the team in one modality mj with content xj . An ON goal gives the category name, e.g. “nightstand”. An IIN goal gives an image of the specific instance taken from a camera that is not the agent’s, and a TN goal gives a description of the specific instance, e.g. “the plant by the sliding glass door”. IIN and TN goals point to a specific instance, while an ON goal accepts any instance of the named category. All M goals are given at the start in no particular order. Each agent observes posed RGB-D images and its own pose in a frame shared by the team, as in prior multi-robot work [11], [13], and executes discrete actions, MOVE FORWARD by 0.25 m and 

TURN LEFT or TURN RIGHT by 30 ◦, in lockstep with its teammates. 

Success protocol. At any step an agent may declare 

FOUND (j) for a goal j that is still unresolved. The declara-tion succeeds if the agent stands within 1 m of a navigable viewpoint of an acceptable instance of gj , following the success criterion of GOAT-Bench [7], and is otherwise void, with the episode continuing without penalty. Declaring and scoring are separate: a declaration is the policy’s own belief, which the team may share, whereas the evaluator scores it against the ground truth offline and never reveals the verdict, so any verification before declaring must use the agent’s own perception. A goal counts for the team regardless of which agent found it. The episode ends when all M goals are found or when every agent has exhausted its budget of 500 actions or a path length of 3 d∗, where d∗ is the oracle team makespan defined below. 

B. Episode Generation 

Scenes and goals. We use the HM3DSem scenes [16] with the train/validation scene split of HM3D-OVON [6], and draw goal instances and their navigable viewpoints from its 379 open-vocabulary categories. For each episode we sample a floor that contains at least M distinct categories, then M instances of M distinct categories on that floor, each keeping up to eight navigable viewpoints as its success region. Modalities are balanced within the episode, with exactly one goal per modality for M =3 and as evenly as possible otherwise, as shown in Fig. 2b. Fig. 2d shows how many other instances of the same category share the floor with each IIN and TN goal. 

Image and text goals. IIN images follow the InstanceIma-geNav protocol [5] adopted by GOAT-Bench, with a camera placed 0.5–2 m from the instance at a height of 0.8–1.5 m and a field of view of 60 –120 ◦, keeping only viewpoints from which the instance is sufficiently visible. For TN descriptions we follow the LanguageNav pipeline of GOAT-Bench, rendering the instance from its best viewpoint and prompting a VLM, Qwen3.5-27B [28], for one sentence that distinguishes it from others of its category by color, material, and neighboring objects, and we spot-check the results. 

Starts and reachability. The K start poses are sampled on the same floor by rejection sampling. They must lie on navigable regions of sufficient size, be separated by a controlled geodesic distance so that the team starts dis-tributed rather than co-located, and jointly cover the goals, 

i.e. every goal must be geodesically reachable from at least one start. Each goal enters the routing geometry through a single representative viewpoint, and the start–goal and goal– goal geodesic matrices over these points are stored with the episode.

All methods receive the same inputs, the goal set with category names, reference images, and descriptions, posed RGB-D from every agent, and a shared top-down occupancy map built from the team’s depth observations. Goals that any agent has declared found are removed from every agent’s list. The evaluator’s verdict on those declarations is never revealed to the team. 

A. Adapted Methods 

To compare coordination strategies rather than perception stacks, all adapted methods and HiGA share one goal-recognition module, taken from the strongest HM3D-OVON baseline [6]. An open-vocabulary detector, OWLv2 [29], pro-poses candidates for every unresolved goal in every frame. A candidate is accepted for an ON goal by detector confidence, for a TN goal by CLIP [30] similarity between the crop and the description, and for an IIN goal by SuperGlue [31] keypoint matching against the reference image, after which a point-goal policy approaches the object and declares FOUND .Exploration and coordination follow each method’s original design. VLFM [20] scores frontiers with a BLIP-2 [32] value map, and each agent greedily heads to the highest-value frontier for the unresolved goals on the shared map. 

EGO-Swarm [21] is a purely geometric swarm in which each agent moves to the nearest frontier, with a repulsion term that keeps agents apart. Co-NavGPT2 [11] uses a centralized VLM planner that reads the shared semantic top-down map with agent positions and frontier candidates and assigns one frontier to every agent at each replanning step. We use Qwen3.5-27B in place of GPT-4o so that all VLM-based methods share the same model. DM 3-Nav [13] is decentralized, with agents exchanging local maps, task states, and navigation intents within communication range, claiming goals by broadcasting their intent, and selecting frontiers weighted by distance. We port the official implementation to our episodes. 

B. HiGA, a Hierarchical Goal Assigner 

HiGA splits the problem into two levels, shown in Fig. 3a. An upper level decides who pursues what , and a lower level decides how to get there .

Lower level. Each agent executes its current assignment with the frozen DAgRL+OD policy of HM3D-OVON [6], whose RGB policy explores toward the assigned category until the recognition module of Sec. IV-A takes over. The detector watches all unresolved goals rather than only the assigned one, so an agent that passes an unassigned goal will still claim it. 

Upper level. The assigner observes the occupancy map, the poses of the K agents, and one heat map per goal, all in the shared frame. The heat map is obtained by scoring every RGB frame of every agent against the goal text with CLIP [30] and back-projecting the score through depth onto the map, where image goals use their category name. Map, goal, and agent tokens from three encoders are processed jointly by a 3-layer transformer with d=256 , an MLP head scores every agent–goal pair, and the Hungarian algorithm decodes a one-to-one assignment, leaving surplus goals unassigned and surplus agents exploring. The assignment is recomputed whenever a goal is found, an agent becomes idle, or a new candidate is detected. 

Training. The teacher is an oracle assigner that uses ground-truth goal positions to compute the makespan-optimal assign-ment of Eq. (1) at every event. We collect 100K observation– assignment samples from the teacher on the training scenes, train with cross-entropy to the teacher’s assignment as be-havior cloning, and then run three rounds of DAgger [17], relabeling the states visited by the student.

The task is far from solved. Adapted methods find 16–27% of the goals, the best method, LaViRA-M 3, finds 41.0%, and the best SPL is 20.3%. Image goals are the hardest for every detector-based method, with 5.9–18.8%. 

Frontier allocation is not goal allocation. Among the adapted methods, spreading the team out matters more than semantic frontier scoring. The purely geometric EGO-Swarm at 23.7% outperforms independent VLFM agents at 16.1%, and the VLM- and intent-based frontier allocators of Co-NavGPT2 and DM 3-Nav add only a few points, 24.5% and 26.6%. Once the map is shared, deciding which goal each agent pursues is what separates the methods. HiGA, which shares the camera and the recognition module of the adapted methods, gains 11 points over the best of them. LaViRA-M 3

gains 14 points with its wider camera and 3 points with the sensor-matched 42 ◦ camera of Table III, and in both configurations its SPL is about twice that of any adapted method. The wider field of view thus accounts for a large part of the SR gap of LaViRA-M 3 but not for its efficiency. 

The two paradigms are complementary. HiGA is the strongest on category goals with 53.0% ON but weak on image goals with 18.8% IIN and the least efficient method with 7.1% SPL, since its detector finds categories reliably but keypoint matching rarely confirms an instance, and its policy, trained for single-goal episodes, travels far. LaViRA-M3 is the strongest on instance goals with 41.4% IIN and 40.9% TN and the most efficient with 20.3% SPL, since the VLM reasons over the reference image or description and shared progress analysis avoids redundant travel, but its category search trails HiGA by 14 points. With the matched camera, LaViRA-M 3 still leads HiGA on image goals, 27.8 against 18.8, and on SPL, 23.0 against 7.1. Neither paradigm dominates.

E. Failure Analysis 

Fig. 5 attributes every missed goal to one cause, for both baselines, and the first split is whether the method ever acted on the goal. LaViRA-M 3 misses 59.0% of all goals, and for 50.7% of all goals it acted and still missed. In 41.8% the agent declared FOUND but stood more than 1 m from a valid viewpoint, which we call a pose miss, and in 8.9% its own verification rejected the claim and it never returned. Only 8.3% of goals were never claimed, and only 2.9% were never approached within 3 m. The pose misses are not one failure but three. Only about a quarter were declared within 2 m of a viewpoint, the case in which the agent saw the object and stopped early, which the 2 m depth gate of Sec. IV-C permits. About 30% had passed within 1 m of the goal earlier but declared elsewhere, a failure of choosing where to stop, and about 40% were declared more than 5 m away, that is, the agent misidentified an object or a room. The looser gate therefore explains only part of the gap. Stop selection and identity verification at a distance are the larger parts, and all three are properties of the baseline rather than of the task. HiGA misses 62.1%, and for 37.6% it acted and still missed. In 25.7% the detector fired but the approach did not end within 1 m of the right instance, and in 11.9%, all of them image goals, the keypoint matcher never confirmed the instance. A further 16.4% of goals were within 3 m of an agent but never detected, and 8.1% were never approached. Perception rather than allocation limits the trained paradigm. The two profiles are complementary and suggest pairing a VLM that recognizes instances with a learned policy that approaches, selects the stop point, and confirms identity at close range. VI. R EAL -W ORLD DEPLOYMENT 

To test whether the setting transfers beyond simulation, we deploy LaViRA-M 3 on a heterogeneous team of three robots in an office, an Agilex Cobot Magic wheeled platform, a Unitree Go1, and a Unitree Go2 quadruped, shown in Fig. 6. Each robot carries an RGB-D camera, a Livox Mid-360 LiDAR for localization and mapping, and an onboard computer, an RTX 4090 on the Cobot Magic and a Jetson Orin NX on each quadruped, on which it runs its own LaViRA agent with a locally deployed Qwen3.8-27B [34], the successor of the Qwen3.5 model used in simulation, in 4-bit GGUF quantization, without any cloud API. The robots exchange the shared map as voxel records over Wi-Fi, together with the goal board and their progress analysis, through the same channels as in simulation, and each exe-cutes the resulting waypoints with its own velocity controller, the only platform-specific component. In the run of Fig. 6, the robots spread out through the office, each claims the goal it encounters, and a goal found by one robot is removed from the others’ lists, as also shown in the supplementary video. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced HM3D-M 3, a benchmark in which a team of agents searches for an unordered set of open-vocabulary goals given as categories, instance images, and descriptions, with a trained and a training-free baseline. The results show a task with ample headroom at 41% SR, two baselines with complementary strengths and distinct failures, and a difficulty that grows with the number of goals and is only partly relieved by adding agents. 

Limitations. We assume a shared coordinate frame and lock-step execution, without communication cost or localization drift.
