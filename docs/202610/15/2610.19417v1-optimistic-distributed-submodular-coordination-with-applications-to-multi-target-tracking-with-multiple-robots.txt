# Optimistic Distributed Submodular Coordination with Applications to Multi-Target Tracking with Multiple Robots 

Pranjal Sharma, Atharva Navsalkar, Zirui Xu, Vasileios Tzoumas †

Abstract — We provide a distributed online algorithm for multi-agent submodular coordination in bandit environments, 

i.e. , environments whose evolution is unknown a priori and may even adapt to the agents’ actions. We are motivated by multi-robot tasks such as target tracking, where the optimal actions change over time and the agents must track them rather than a single fixed optimum. To address such problems, we provide the Optimistic Distributed Online Greedy ( O-DOG )algorithm, that leverages predictions about the environment’s behavior and improves regret guarantees. The agents select actions simultaneously over arbitrary, possibly time-varying, network topologies, and each agent acts optimistically , i.e. ,leveraging predictions of its upcoming rewards, e.g. , from forecasts of the targets’ motion and of its neighbors’ actions. We bound O-DOG ’s tracking regret in terms of the agents’ prediction error and the bound never exceeds its prediction-free counterpart. Particularly, it is sublinear even when the optimal action changes at every time step, provided the predictions are accurate enough. We further quantify the suboptimality due to decentralization as a function of the network structure, and show that accurate predictions accelerate convergence. We validate O-DOG in simulated multi-robot target tracking. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Teams of robots are increasingly deployed to gather infor-mation in dynamic environments: tracking multiple moving targets [1], [2], exploring and mapping unknown spaces [3], [4], and monitoring environmental phenomena [5], [6]. At every time step t, the robots seek to maximize a shared utility via optimization problems of the form 

max

Although eq. (1) is generally NP-hard [7], near-optimal polynomial-time approximation algorithms have been proposed when ft is submodular , a diminishing returns property. For example, the Sequential Greedy ( SG ) algo-rithm [8], in which robots choose their actions sequentially to maximize theire marginal contribution, achieves a 1/2

approximation bound when ft is submodular. All afore-mentioned complex tasks can be modeled as submodular coordination problems. Consequently, ( SG ) and its variants have been commonly adopted in controls, machine learning, and robotics literature [1], [3], [5], [6], [9]–[18]. Three challenges stand between this approach and de-ployment. First, ft is unknown when the robots act: the utility of an action depends on where the targets will be, so the robots must therefore commit to actions before ft

is revealed and learn from the utilities of their actions in hindsight. Second, communication is a bottleneck: SG and its online counterpart [19] require each robot to wait for the decisions of all preceding robots in the sequence before it can select an action. Therefore, every action selection round incurs a chain of sequential communication rounds whose length grows quadratically with the team size [18]. Consensus-based simultaneous methods, in turn, require connected, undirected networks and operate on a continuous relaxation of the objective [20]. Third, the environment can be adversarial as targets may deliberately evade the robots, reacting to their motion [21], so that the optimal actions may change at nearly every time step. Additionally, the robots are seldom without foresight: motion models [22] and learned predictors [23], [24] forecast the targets’ future positions. But their accuracy cannot be guaranteed when the targets behave adversarially [25]. We therefore seek a bound on the 

tracking regret [26], which compares the robots’ cumulative utility against the near-optimal utility that can be attained by the best sequence of time-varying actions chosen with knowledge of the future. Moreover, we seek a bound that improves as the forecasts become more accurate. To our knowledge, existing methods address at most two of these challenges. Distributed submodular coordination with limited information [9], [10], [14], [16], [18] assumes 

ft is known, and model-based methods plan on forecasts but presume them correct [3], [12]. Online methods handle unknown ft, but some compete only with the best fixed ac-tion [27], [28], including DOG [29]. OSG [19] and its bandit variant [30] bound the tracking regret but decide sequentially, while MA-OSMA [20] decides simultaneously via consensus on a continuous relaxation but requires undirected connected networks and real-valued messages. None of these use fore-casts, and their tracking regret bounds grow linearly in time when the optimal actions change at a constant frequency, 

e.g. , when targets actively evade pursuers. Optimistic online learning makes the regret scale with the error of a forecast of the next loss [31]–[33], but its fast rates in games [34] assume that payoffs are bilinear in the players’ mixed strategies. In contrast, we consider problems where each robot’s utility depends on its teammates’ realized actions. In submodular maximization, optimism so far requires a centralized decision maker and has been limited to weighted threshold potentials, a subclass of submodular functions [35], while MetaBSG [25] accepts or rejects suggested actions as a whole, deciding sequentially. We propose the Optimistic Distributed Online Greedy (O-DOG ) algorithm for eq. (1) (Section III) as an extension of 

DOG [29], which addresses all three challenges. First, O-DOG ’s tracking regret is bounded in terms of the robots’ arXiv:2610.19417v1 [eess.SY] 14 Oct 2026 cumulative forecast error rather than the time horizon (Sec-tion IV). We define the forecast error or accuracy as the cumulative difference between the predicted and true rewards over the time horizon. We prove that accurate forecasts yield a better bound and faster convergence to near optimal actions as compared to the forecast free alternatives. Second, O-DOG applies to arbitrary, possibly directed and time-varying, communication networks, and its approximation guarantee improves as the network becomes more connected. When the network is strongly connected, it matches the bound with the 

1/(1+ κf ) factor of SG , whereas it degrades down to 1−κf

when the robots do not communicate at all (Theorem 2). Third, as in DOG , each robot evaluates its candidate actions against the actions of the robots it can receive information from, so the robots decide and communicate simultaneously rather than in a sequential chain. We evaluate O-DOG in simulated target tracking under 20 and 50 robot scenarios, where a quarter of the targets are active evaders (Section V). With about half of OSG ’s messages per round, O-DOG tracks the targets 27% (20 robots) and 39% (50 robots) closer than O-OSG , i.e., OSG 

equipped with O-DOG ’s optimism framework. Moreover, O-DOG tracks 70% closer than the published OSG . This is achieved by replacing OSG ’s N − 1 sequential communica-tion hops per round with a single simultaneous exchange, which lets O-DOG re-select actions up to 11 times faster. II. We presented the Optimistic Distributed Online Greedy (O-DOG ) algorithm for multi-agent submodular coordina-tion in unpredictable environments. Leveraging tools from optimistic online learning, O-DOG enables agents to make simultaneous online decisions over arbitrary, possibly time-varying, communication networks, while exploiting predic-tions of their upcoming rewards. We bounded O-DOG ’s tracking regret in terms of the agents’ prediction error rather than the time horizon: the bound never exceeds its prediction-free counterpart, and remains sublinear even when the optimal actions change at every time step, provided the environment changes predictably. In simulated multi-robot target tracking with a 20 agent network, O-DOG tracked the targets by up to 27% closer than the state-of-the-art sequential algorithm OSG equipped with optimism while transmitting approximately half the number of messages. On a 50 agent network that gap grew to 40%. Compared to OSG without any optimism, O-DOG tracks up to 70% closer, thereby achieving a scalable distributed submodular coordination architecture for tracking regret. 

Future work. We will extend O-DOG ’s guarantees to actions whose rewards are revealed only over multiple time steps, such as multi-step trajectories executed in the style of receding horizon, by using delayed feedback techniques [43].
