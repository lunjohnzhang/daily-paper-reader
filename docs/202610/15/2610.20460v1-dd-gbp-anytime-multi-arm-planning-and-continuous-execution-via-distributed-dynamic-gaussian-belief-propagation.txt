# DD-GBP: Anytime Multi-Arm Planning and Continuous Execution via Distributed Dynamic Gaussian Belief Propagation 

Zhao Jin, Fengyi Dai, Yuetong Yin, Yunan Wang, Wenjie Chen, Chuxiong Hu 

Abstract —Safely executing long-horizon multi-arm tasks online in complex dynamic environments remains challenging. We pro-pose a new distributed dynamic Gaussian belief propagation (DD-GBP) method that directly plans joint-space trajectories through parallel local inference. A unified Gaussian-process (GP) param-eterization connects adaptive planning horizons, interpolation-time factors, and continuous execution. Each robot maintains a local tree-structured factor graph and exchanges messages asynchronously over active inter-arm links. Dynamic subnet re-construction and event-triggered updates reduce communication and inference costs while responding to moving obstacles. We also provide theoretical analyses of local inference accuracy, anytime early stopping, triggered-replanning consistency, and GP-consistent execution. In 50 long-horizon, multi-target four-arm scenes with moving obstacles, DD-GBP achieves 92 .5% collision-free all-target com-pletion and the highest success rate among six representative baselines. Scenario ablations demonstrate long-horizon safety and robustness to communication impairment and extreme condi-tions; success remains 86 .0% under 90% packet loss. Execution results further show substantially suppressed extreme velocity and acceleration peaks relative to Syn-GBP. On one workstation, obstacle-free tests with 30 coexisting arms retain 99 .87% success and a 15 .80 ms mean warm solve, exceeding 60 Hz and outperforming the compared methods in scalability. Dual-arm transport experiments under dynamic interference demonstrate online response and practical deployability. To the best of our knowledge, these results establish a new state of the art for online long-horizon multi-arm planning in dynamic scenes. 

Index Terms —Online motion planning, Gaussian belief propa-gation, Autonomous cyber-physical systems, Collision avoidance, Distributed dynamic planning. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# MULTI-MANIPULATOR collaboration improves flexi-bility and productivity in cooperative handling [1], logistics [2], [3], and manufacturing [4]–[6]. In confined workspaces without physical separation, each arm’s motion changes the space available to its neighbors, so collision avoidance requires coordination throughout the task. The plan-ner must maintain this coordination while responding online to moving obstacles and disturbances, making fast solution updates and safety over long-horizon execution essential.

Centralized planners jointly optimize all robots’ trajectories and constraints [7]–[9], which allows interactions to be consid-ered within one planning problem. Their computational cost, however, grows with the coupled state space and can limit online multi-arm planning [10]. Distributed planners reduce this burden by assigning a local planning problem to each robot, while using inter-robot communication to coordinate the resulting trajectories [11], [12]. Insufficient coordination can still cause collisions in dense workspaces. To coordinate coupled trajectories, optimization-based dis-tributed methods decompose planning into per-robot subprob-lems. The nonlinear variant (NADMM) of the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) [13], [14] coordi-nates these subproblems through consensus constraints and repeated information exchange. For high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) manipulators, collision constraints couple many joint variables and make the local subproblems highly nonconvex, complicating convergence and exposing poor local minima. For online operation, model predictive control (MPC) repeat-edly optimizes a finite trajectory horizon, executes its first part, and updates the plan using new observations. Distributed MPC (DMPC) [14], [15] applies this procedure to coordi-nated per-robot problems, balancing horizon length against the cost of repeated solves. Short horizons reduce each update’s computation but can restrict coordination over longer motions, motivating solvers that exploit trajectory structure to make fre-quent distributed updates affordable. For manipulators, Gafur et al. [16] combine distributed nonlinear MPC (NMPC) with dynamic collision and deadlock avoidance, providing a directly relevant constrained-optimization baseline. A different way to reduce online computation is to learn planning policies from data. Learning-based planners have been demonstrated in grid-based multi-agent pathfinding [17] and dual-arm operation [18], [19], with a graph neural network (GNN)-based planner coordinating up to eight arms in static scenes [20]. Their performance in new task distributions depends on how well the training data and learned policies generalize to the new geometry. Model predictive path integral (MPPI) planning offers another route to frequent online up-dates. MR-STORM [21] uses graphics processing unit (GPU)-accelerated sampling to extend predictive control to decoupled multi-arm planning, evaluating candidate motions against other arms’ predicted trajectories. Efficient computational structures and mature optimizers also accelerate planning. GPMP2 [22] uses continuous GP trajectory parameterization to construct sparse Bayesian factor graphs for single-arm maximum a posteriori (MAP) opti-arXiv:2610.20460v1 [cs.RO] 14 Oct 2026 DD-GBP: ONLINE MULTI-ARM PLANNING 2

mization with GTSAM [23]. Centralized extensions explore robot formations [24] and 2D multi-robot scenarios [25]; high-dimensional coupling and local minima remain challenges for centralized optimization. We likewise use GP trajec-tory parameterization, but go beyond sparse representation by further exploiting its advantages for asynchronous multi-arm coordination, collision queries, adaptation, and consistent execution. This representation naturally matches our local Gaussian message passing. For continuously updated coordination, Gaussian Belief Propagation (GBP) provides a distributed inference formula-tion over continuous states through Gaussian graphical models [26]–[29]. Its local message structure allows neighboring vari-ables to exchange information without global consensus vari-ables or task-specific training, and has supported localization of planar automated guided vehicle (AGV) teams [30], [31] and collaborative planning [32]. For manipulators, GP-based replanning by Bari et al. [33] remains single-arm, whereas Prados et al. [34] coordinate learned short-horizon policies rather than joint-space trajectories. Direct multi-arm plan-ning further requires coupled joint motions, adaptive horizons and interaction neighborhoods, and a consistent inference– execution representation. Our Syn-GBP study [35] explored short-horizon dual-arm assembly; extending it to scalable multi-arm planning calls for a new architecture and accom-panying theoretical analysis. Building on but differing from that preliminary study, we propose DD-GBP as a more complete algorithmic and theoretical framework. Its tree-directed local solve, dynamic interaction subnets, adaptive horizons, and common GP ex-ecution model target scalable multi-arm coordination and safe long-horizon execution; the experiments verify substantial performance gains. The new contributions are as follows. 1) Adaptive factor-graph planning with replanning con-sistency. We formulate multi-arm trajectory planning as MAP inference over a changing factor graph, with geometry-aware collision factors and adaptive planning horizons. We prove that pruning inactive inter-arm fac-tors preserves the complete gated objective (Prop. 2) and establish a local bound on the change from the time-aligned previous trajectory after a replanning trigger (Prop. 3). This links online network adaptation to a well-defined objective and a quantitative measure of replanning consistency. 2) Distributed multi-arm network scheduling and any-time planning. We design a network update procedure that combines event-triggered subnet selection, local backward–forward inference, and asynchronous inter-arm message exchange. Each robot solves its fixed linearized local tree and communicates only on active links. We derive residual-based early-stopping bounds (Prop. 1), giving deadline-limited updates a quantitative inference-error criterion while keeping coordination lo-calized as the team grows. 3) Consistent continuous-trajectory optimization and execution. We optimize joint-space trajectories through GP support states, whose adaptive process scale reflects the acceleration-driven motion amplitude, and use the same parameterization for interpolation-time collision factors, timestamp-aligned inter-arm queries, and exe-cution references. We establish the connection between planning and execution by showing that, for the adopted prior, the conditional GP mean is the cubic Hermite trajectory sent to the tracking controller (Prop. 4). This makes continuous trajectory evaluation part of the planning model and preserves that model when robots exchange and execute their plans. To our knowledge, we provide the first matched evalu-ation spanning six representative multi-arm planning base-lines: NADMM [14], DMPC [14], [15], GPMP2 [22], Syn-GBP [35], MR-STORM [21], and Gafur’s NMPC [16]. DD-GBP establishes a new state of the art among these evaluated baselines, with the highest success rate in the matched four-arm benchmark and the strongest observed scalability. PLANNING METHOD 

This section presents the planning problem and the oper-ations of DD-GBP in the order shown in Fig. 2. We first formulate multi-arm motion planning as inference over GP trajectory parameters, then define the local and inter-arm factors that form the DD-GBP net. Finally, we describe how robots reconstruct their active subnets, schedule local inference and message exchange, and generate continuous execution ref-erences. Section IV analyzes the resulting inference accuracy, switching consistency, and connection between GP planning and execution. Unlike MPC methods that explicitly formulate planning as nonlinear programs [14], [15], DD-GBP uses an inference– execution–retriggering loop: GP support states represent the trajectory, weighted factors encode boundary, motion, and collision requirements, and local message exchange refines the trajectory through distributed Gaussian inference. C. Distributed and Dynamic Planning with GBP 

DD-GBP operates as an event-triggered receding-horizon loop. A new target, a predicted local collision, or a neigh-borhood change triggers reconstruction of the affected subnet 

Gtr . Each robot linearizes its active factors and performs backward–forward inference with the current remote messages held fixed during the sweep. The resulting support-state mean is passed to GP-consistent Hermite interpolation and the low-level tracking controller. Robots exchange updated messages only along active inter-arm couplings. The outer loop selects the active subnet, while repeated local sweeps and message exchanges refine its trajectory estimate. Alg. 1 summarizes the procedure.

A. Convergence and Anytime Planning 

For nonlinear GBP on loopy graphs, convergence under repeated relinearization and topology changes remains an open problem in general. For the fixed Gaussian models covered by Weiss and Freeman [37], converged belief means recover the Gaussian MAP solution even when the covariance esti-mates are approximate. Walk-summability and strict diagonal dominance provide sufficient convergence conditions [38], while analyses of different factorizations, schedules, damp-ing choices, and variable groupings further clarify how the inference design affects convergence [27], [28], [39], [40]. In dynamic multi-arm planning, we regard a timely, usable trajectory estimate as sometimes more important than waiting for full convergence while obstacles and neighboring arms continue to move. The iterative structure of GBP supports this choice, as each completed local sweep provides an estimate for its current Gaussian subproblem, which remains available if the distributed iteration is interrupted. We carry this estimate into the next planning cycle through the time-aligned GP warm start, allowing refinement to continue as new information arrives. We organize these updates through the robot-wise schedule in Fig. 2. Before each local solve, a robot collects updated neighbor beliefs or trajectories and obstacle observations as external information. It then holds this information fixed and linearizes its local factors about the current trajectory. By combining interpolation-time factors on each adjacent support pair with the corresponding motion-transition factor, we retain a chain on which one backward–forward sweep gives exact local Gaussian marginals [26]. The robot then publishes its updated belief or trajectory to its neighbors. These alternating local solves and message exchanges form block-wise Gaussian inference. Multivariate GaBP studies relate convergence to variable grouping [40], motivating our robot-wise blocks: each local solve groups a coupled trajectory chain, while neighbor messages convey cross-robot influence. Each completed sweep returns the MAP trajectory estimate for that robot’s current fixed Gaussian subproblem. When an iteration cap or deadline interrupts the coupled solve, the following proposition bounds the remaining error using the assembled residual and local linearization mismatch.

The assembled residual and δt then separate the remaining Gaussian inference error from the local approximation error, giving us a basis for choosing a stopping tolerance and assessing the estimate available at a planning deadline.

C. Consistency of GP Interpolation and Continuous Execution

DD-GBP combines switching factor-graph inference, adap-tive horizons, and localized backward–forward messages for online multi-arm planning. A common GP representation connects support-state inference, interpolation-time factors, timestamp alignment, and continuous execution, with local replanning-consistency and early-stopping bounds under stated assumptions. On 50 four-arm scenes with four dynamic obstacles, DD-GBP records 92 .5% collision-free all-target completion and 

16 .3 ms mean warm solves. Obstacle-free tests extend to 30 arms with 99 .17 –100% single-arm task success and average planning times of order 10 ms. Mechanism and scenario studies demonstrate robust long-horizon coordination under sensing and communication disturbances. Compact-workspace physical demonstrations further show the integrated architec-ture’s deployability. Tracking and execution quality 

The compact comparison retains aligned and unshifted joint-position root-mean-square (RMS) errors, velocity RMS/ peak, and accel-eration RMS/ peak. Lag alignment uses a configuration-wide lag fitted on separate scene folds; filtering in the impedance controller introduces an approximately 0.1 s response lag. Gafur uses its joint-trajectory controller’s desired reference. DD-GBP combines low aligned tracking error with the smallest extreme execution peaks among the compared methods: 0.900 rad/s and 53 .2 rad/s 2, versus Syn-GBP’s 7.40 rad/s and 760 .7 rad/s 2. Its adaptive planning and GP-consistent continuous execution suppress abrupt velocity and acceleration transients, producing tighter peak distributions while maintaining high task completion (manuscript Tab. I(a) and Fig. 6(a)). DMPC’s slow planning and response reduce its update frequency and typical motion speed, but large extreme peaks remain. Removing adaptation, localized connectivity, or inter-polation can reintroduce execution excursions; the full framework improves execution stability rather than merely lowering average motion speed.
