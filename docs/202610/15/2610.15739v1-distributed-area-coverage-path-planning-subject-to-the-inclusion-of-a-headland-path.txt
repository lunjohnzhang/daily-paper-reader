# Distributed area coverage path planning subject to the inclusion of a headland path 

Mogens Plessen ∗

Abstract 

A deterministic method for the problem of distributed area coverage path planning subject to the inclusion of a headland path is presented. Given a, in general, non-convex area to be covered that may, in general, include obstacle areas, multiple robots, at least one robot depot, and constraints on each machinery such as a working width, work capacity limits, travel capacity limits, nonholonomic dynamical constraints and, possibly, a robot start and end depot assignment, distributed paths for each machinery are calculated that collectively enable full area coverage. Variations are discussed, in particular, a scenario where unconstrained depots assignment can attain a global optimal solution to the problem. The importance of inclusion of a headland path for area coverage, as well as the benefits in comparison to an alternative Boustrophedon-pattern based approach are highlighted.

1. Introduction 

Distributed area coverage promises parallelization of work by distribution of partial area coverage work to di ff erent robots or vehicles. Parallelization can lead to reduction of total time required for the area coverage task. In contrast to paralleliza-tion, multiple machines can also cooperate to perform sequen-tially dependent tasks [1, 2, 3]. A common objective in distributed area coverage path plan-ning is to minimize the total pathlength collectively traveled by all robots, however other optimization criteria exist also, such as minimizing the number of turns made by robots [4]. Within an agricultural setting, robots are performing area coverage work typically ground-based. However, the broader topic is likewise relevant for aquatic and aerial applications, for example, for mapping. The vast majority of research for distributed area coverage path planning focuses (i) on Boustrophedon path planning-related methods, and (ii) under total absence of a headland path:

Methods that (i) output paths less structured (in comparison to Boustrophedon) or less intuitive coverage paths and which are more closely related to the multiple-Traveling Salesman Problem (m-TSP), but (ii) also lack headland paths consider-ation include [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. These obser-vations presents both a research gap and the motivation for this paper. A method is presented that explicitly includes a headland path in the distributed area coverage planning problem. In agriculture, headland paths are common for area coverage applications, especially for cereal crop production. However, the literature focused so far on single-robot area coverage plan-ning, or dismissed the headland path for the distributed area coverage planning problem. Headland paths imply a path traversed along the area bound-ary as visualised in Fig. 2. A robot moves along headland path segments and mainfield lanes while conducting area coverage work. An important parameter is the working width of the robot operating in the area, for example, to spray the area with pesti-cides, fertilizers, and so forth. Within this paper, the focus is (i) on an alternative path plan-ning method in comparison to the Boustrophedon method, and (ii) the explicit inclusion of a headland path in the distributed area coverage planning problem. In fact, it is argued that both aspects are not a limitation, but, on the contrary, features that improve distributed area coverage path planning. In this paper, a static and fully observable area is assumed, that can in general be non-convexly shaped and include obstacle areas such as tree islands, ponds and the like. The remaining paper is organised as follows: problem for-mulation, modeling and proposed solution, discussion and the conclusion are described in Sections 2-4. 

2. Materials and methods 

2.1. Problem formulation 

The problem addressed can be formulated as follows. 

Problem 1. Given an area to be covered that may in general be non-convexly shaped and may include obstacle areas, multiple robots, at least one robot depot, constraints on each robot such as a working width, work capacity limits, travel capacity lim-its, nonholonomic dynamical constraints, and a possibly given robot start and /or end depot assignment for individual robots, calculate distributed paths for each of the robots such that col-lectively full area coverage is achieved. 2.2. Discussion of constraints

The avoidance of area coverage gaps and overlaps is a main challenge. This motivates to (i) re-use existing tractor traces when covering an area or paths multiple times, and (ii) avoid any new transitions between mainfield lanes and headland path that would create additional compacted areas that can then not be further used for crop growth. 

2.3. Proposed solution 

The proposed distributed area coverage path planning solu-tion is summarised in Algorithm 1. Several comments can be made. Each individual robot is la-beled by index i ∈ { 1, . . . , N}.Each robot is initially located at a start depot from where it will begin its partial area coverage work. Depending on a given application, this assignment can (i) be assumed as being given, or (ii) can be assigned by the method. The notion of a principal depot is introduced. The principal depot denotes a depot, which is in general not di ff erent from other depots, but which plays an important role for the calcula-tion of a full area coverage path plan (FACP) in Step 3 of Alg. 1. FACP starts at the principal depot. Here, the method from [24] is used for the calculation of FACP. 

D1 describes the set of all depots which are admissible to be selected as principal depot and at which there is at least one robot initially located. For a typical application, D1 would de-scribe the set of all depots. In Step 4 of Alg. 1 both the FACP plan, PFACP , and its inverse (the path given by the inversion of the sequence of position co-ordinates), here denotes as PFACP inverse , are considered. Note that this inversion does not change the the FACP path length. Then, remaining Steps 5-18 are based on a specific FACP plan candidate. In Step 6 at the principal depot candidate D1, a robot i is se-lected according to a criterion. Depending on the problem con-straints, a robot may, for example, be selected as (i) the one with maximal work capacity and minimial activation cost in case there are multiple robots at D1, or (ii) the one with maximal work capacity and minimial activation cost among all available robots in case of a free assignment of robots to depots is per-mitted.

3. Discussion and limitations 

3.1. Unconstrained depot assignment and global optimality 

Fig. 6 illustrates two examples to highlight the di ff erence when depot locations are fixed or can be freely assigned. In the latter case, a global optimal solution for the distributed area coverage path planning problem can be constructed. This can be seen as follows. By non-negativity of pathlengths a global optimal FACP solution exists. It can be partitioned into partial paths, whereby each partial path is served by a corre-sponding robot. are then assigned as the depot locations, whereby each depot serves as the start depot to one robot and as the exit depot of another robot. It remains that each robot starts each partial area coverage path at its designated start depot. Two comments can be made. First, all partial area coverage paths can be worked in parallel by the robots, which is the key advantage over a single robot working the FACP. The disadvan-tage is that robot depots (an infrastructure) is needed. Second, the free depot assignment permits to eliminate initial and final non-working transition phases for all robots. This in consequence enables the optimality of the solution. Finally, an even stronger scenario can be constructed. If ad-ditionally it is assumed that (i) all depots permit to recharge (both work as well as travel capacacity) at all depots, and (ii) each partial path is selected such that it can be worked by even the robot with least work capacity, then area coverage in contin-uum is possible, where a continuous work pattern of robots can be achieved. This implies that each robot traverses each depot and is recharged at each depot. 

3.2. Discussion of heuristic Step 6 and 10 

Step 6 and 10 of Alg. 1 imply a heuristic selection step. If there are multiple robots at a depot one robot has to be selected according a criterion. Such a criterion may be, for example, to prioritize a robot with maximal work capacity, cwork  

> i

, out of the options at the depot. This heuristic may be sensible, but it is noted that it does not guarantee to yield a global optimal solution to Problem 1. In contrast, the global optimal solution (which exists by non-negativity of path lengths) can be achieved by exhaustively test-ing all robot selection options and calculating all correspond-ing downstream solution dependencies, thereby implicitly con-structing a solution tree. Unfortunately, the size of this tree grows exponentially with the number of robots involved. There-fore a heuristic is more appropriate. 

3.3. Collision avoidance 

Since all robots are coordinated via the FACP-plan in Step 2 of Alg. 1, frontal collision scenarios of robots are avoided by construction. Because of the FACP-coordination all robots are always driving in the same direction along headland path edges. Therefore, any heuristic priority scheme (e.g., the robot closest to a shared headland path segment has priority to pass that segment first) is su ffi cient for local collision avoidance. 

3.4. Discussion of counterexamples for alternative methods 

Several counterexamples and arguments are provided to mo-tivate why (i) Boustrophedon-based planning, and (ii) the ab-sence of a headland path is not a viable alternative to the pre-sented method. First, in case of absence of a headland path coverage possibly very large area coverage gaps can result. This is illustrated in Fig. 3. Therefore, using only a Boustrophedon pattern for area coverage is suboptimal. This statement holds for both, single-robot area coverage and distributed multi-robot area coverage. Second and following up on the previous argument, if a head-land path coverage is sought to be included, then the combina-tion of Boustrohpedon-pattern with headland path coverage is suboptimal as demonstrated in [26, 24, 27]. Third, the Boustrophedon pattern in combination with short-est path transitions, in particular, within agricultural fields are problematic. This was discussed in [26] and is further illus-trated by Fig. 2, 7 and 8. For example, for robots with ground-contact tyre-traces in the ground repress or compact area that is then not further eligible for crop growth (cf. 2). This moti-vates to (i) re-use existing tractor traces when covering an area or paths multiple times, and (ii) avoid any new transitions be-tween mainfield lanes and headland path that would create addi-tional compacted areas that can the no longer be used for crop growth. For distributed area coverage multiple shortest paths between depots and LWL-locations are needed. Subject to the constraint of re-using only existing tractor traces for movement and avoiding new repressed area transitions, scenarios such as illustrated in Fig. 8 typically result, which imply very long de-tours and path lengths accordingly. Fourth, while the previous first two argument remain valid the last third argument about repressed area transitions is not relevant for aquatic or aerial applications. In such applications the combination of Boustrophedon-pattern with headland path coverage for distributed area coverage may be shorter than the proposed solution in Sect. 2.3. However, no generalizing state-ment can be made (examples can be constructed or selected in favor of one or another solution). Shortest path contributions to the overall path length now become shorter because the transi-tion graph is less constrained, but the overall path length may still be larger since the combination of Boustrophedon-pattern and headland path coverage is suboptimal for area coverage as shown in [26, 24]. This suboptimality may, in general, out-weight the savings from the shorter path contributions. 7Note that Alg. 1 can likewise be used for distributed area cov-erage path planning based on the Boustrophedon-pattern with headland path coverage. Therefore, only Step 3 in Alg. 1 needs to be adapted to account for Boustrophedon-based planning. Similarly, note that freeform mainfield lanes [28] or 3D data [29, 30] only changed the transition graph coordinates, but do not require a change of the structure of Alg. 1. 

4. Conclusion 

A deterministic algorithm for distributed area coverage path planning subject to the inclusion of a headland path was pre-sented. Various variations were discussed. A scenario was pre-sented where unconstrained depot locations assignment along the headland path can attain a global optimal solution. The importance of inclusion of a headland path for area cov-erage, as well as the benefits in comparison to an alternative Boustrophedon-pattern based approach were highlighted.
