Abstract 

As robotic teams tackle increasingly complex tasks in dynamic and unstruc-tured environments, effective coordination requires agents to maintain accurate, aligned representations of their environment, teammates, and mission state. We argue that Mutual Awareness, Shared Situational Awareness, and Team Situa-tional Awareness — three concepts widely invoked in the multi-robot systems literature — are not interchangeable: they form a containment hierarchy in which each type subsumes the previous in scope, and span a distribution spectrum from fully individualized understanding (MA) to fully uniform understanding (SSA), with TSA occupying a mixed position. We establish this through a two-dimensional taxonomy organized along the object of awareness and awareness distribution axes. Treating these terms as synonyms obscures the precise coor-dination requirements each imposes on a robotic system. A Search and Rescue case study with heterogeneous aerial and ground robots grounds each taxonomy 

1arXiv:2610.06427v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 position in concrete coordination requirements. These findings provide a concep-tual foundation for principled specification, design, and comparison of collective awareness in multi-robot systems.

# 1 Introduction 

Multi-agent systems in robotics represent an innovative approach to solving complex problems through the coordination and collaboration of multiple robotic units exhibit-ing emergent behaviors. These systems offer several advantages, including increased robustness, scalability, and flexibility in tackling diverse challenges across various domains, such as area mapping, collective construction, and target search and track-ing. By leveraging collaboration and cooperation between its members, these robotic systems can achieve tasks that would be difficult or impossible for individual robots to accomplish alone, making them particularly useful in many applications ranging from automated construction to environmental monitoring Wooldridge (2009). More formally, a Multi-Agent System (MAS) is a collection of agents that concur-rently sense and act upon an environment, transforming it through their combined activities, with emergent behavior arising from agent interactions rather than from any individual agent in isolation Weiss (2016). A multi-robot system (MRS) consti-tutes a specific instantiation of this paradigm in which all agents are embodied robots interacting within a shared physical environment to accomplish tasks that exceed the capabilities of any individual unit Rizk, Awad, and Tunstel (2019). MRS vary along several dimensions: they may be homogeneous or heterogeneous in robot composition, communicating or non-communicating in information exchange, and cooperative or competitive in their interaction objectives Verma and Ranga (2021). Enabling robotic teams to pursue shared objectives without unnecessary overhead, such as resolving conflicts, redundantly synchronizing actions, or correcting misalignments, requires coordination Wooldridge (2009) Bond and Gasser (1989). Awareness plays a central role in enabling this coordination, as agents must maintain accurate representations of their environment, their teammates, and the evolving mission state to anticipate and complement each other’s actions. A key element of agent autonomy is the theoretical foundation laid by Ends-ley (1995) about Situation Awareness (SA) in the form of a model divided into three layers: perception of the environmental elements, comprehension of their mean-ing, and projection of their future status. In recent theoretical work, awareness has been proposed as the real-time understanding of sensory flows, where understanding is achieved when an agent successfully derives valid inferences about objects in its environment Sanz, Rodr´ ıguez, Molina, Aguado, and G´ omez (2024). This framework enables individual agents to make informed decisions and act appropriately within their environment. Building on this foundation, concepts such as Mutual Awareness, 2Shared Situational Awareness, and Team Situational Awareness emerged to charac-terize how team members align their understanding to enable effective coordination in multi-agent contexts. These concepts have been widely adopted by the multi-robot systems community, where researchers frequently invoke awareness as a fundamental notion in coordina-tion capabilities Fernandez-Cortizas, Perez-Saura, Sanz, Molina, and Campoy (2024). However, the three terms are often used interchangeably, without distinguishing the distinct requirements each imposes on a robotic system. We argue this conflation is consequential: MA, SSA, and TSA differ both in the scope of what agents must know and in whether that knowledge must be uniform across the team. Clarifying these distinctions raises two questions that remain underspecified in the literature: 1. What are the distinct requirements of the different awareness types, and how do they relate to each other? 2. What precise definitions can be established for each awareness type that charac-terize their distinct requirements and roles in multi-robot coordination? Addressing these questions is essential for enabling principled system design and technical progress in multi-robot coordination. Drawing from cognitive science, multi-agent systems, and robotics, this article examines these three awareness types and develops a conceptual framework to clarify their distinct requirements and relation-ships. Section 2 reviews the three main forms of awareness identified in the literature, Mutual Awareness, Shared Situational Awareness, and Team Situational Aware-ness, analyzing their definitions and key distinctions. Section 3 presents a taxonomy that characterizes these awareness types along two axes, awareness distribution and object of awareness, and illustrates each classification using a Search and Rescue sce-nario involving a heterogeneous team of aerial and ground robots. Finally, Section 4 synthesizes the main findings and identifies directions for future research. 

# 2 Awareness in Multi-Robot Systems 

The notion of Situation Awareness (SA), originally formulated by Endsley Endsley (1995) as a three-layer model encompassing the perception of environmental elements, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their future status, has been progressively extended to the domain of multi-robot systems. In this collective context, individual SA provides the foundation upon which distributed forms of awareness are built, as each agent must maintain its own understanding of the environment and its role within the team. Researchers across cognitive science, human factors, and multi-agent systems have proposed a range of overlapping concepts to characterize how awareness emerges and is maintained across a robotic collective. This section reviews the three forms that have received the widest attention in the multi-robot systems literature: Mutual Awareness, Shared Situational Awareness, and Team Situational Awareness. 1 A survey of representative definitions for each is provided in Table 1.

42.1 Mutual Awareness 

Mutual Awareness (MA) is described as the mutual understanding of each other’s intentions, beliefs and activities She and Li (2017). Schmidt Schmidt (1998) charac-terizes it more precisely as the perception and understanding that member A has of the activity, intention, status, possible results, and influence of member B. This defi-nition highlights that MA is inherently directional and may be asymmetric: as noted by Villa et al. Villa et al. (2008), if A can observe B but B cannot observe A, then A holds a watching awareness , whereas if A knows B is observing them but cannot reciprocate, A holds a watched awareness . Some theories propose that MA is essen-tial for any cooperative activity Shu and Furuta (2005), and several authors reduce MA to individual activity and mutual beliefs Grosz and Sidner (1990); Tuomela and Miller (1988), which influence the thoughts and actions of team members. Similarly, Bratman Bratman (1992) states that mutual responsiveness, defined as responsiveness to the intentions and actions of others, which emerges when participants are com-mitted to the same goal, is essential for achieving a shared cooperative activity. This matches the idea proposed in Grosz and Sidner (1990) regarding mutual beliefs about the intentions of other individuals being considered in the definition of a shared plan. Complementary to individual SA, mutual awareness is also mentioned in She and Li (2017) as a component of TSA, where authors describe MA as the understanding of each other’s activities, beliefs and intentions. 

## 2.2 Shared Situational Awareness 

Shared Situational Awareness (SSA) refers to the overlap between the SA requirements of different team members, or the degree to which team members possess the same SA across shared requirements Endsley Mica R. (2001). Shared SA is also described as the overlap in situation awareness that is necessary among team members Endsley Mica R. (2001). Complementing this view, Salas et al. Salas, Stout, and Cannon-Bowers (1995) describe Shared SA as a continuously updated and integrated mental representation of a mission built from shared information among team members, emphasizing its dynamic and constructive character. Endsley also suggests that teams can develop internal mechanisms to facilitate the achievement of shared SA. Furthermore, Ends-ley and Jones state that the information to be shared across teams should be defined by examining the areas where SA requirements of each team overlap, to avoid exces-sive communication overload. This shared information is specifically characterized as critical Sepp¨ anen, M¨ akel¨ a, Luokkala, and Virrantaus (2013). 

## 2.3 Team Situational Awareness 

Team Situational Awareness (TSA) has been the subject of varied interpretations, reflecting differing assumptions about how awareness is formed and maintained in teams. Some definitions of TSA describe it primarily as the combination or sharing of individual team members’ situational awareness (SA). From this perspective, TSA corresponds to the overlap of individual SA knowledge states McNeese, Demir, Cooke, and She (2021). A common definition captures TSA as the degree to which every team member possesses the SA necessary for fulfilling their responsibilities Endsley and M. 5Robertson (2000) including not only the environment but also each individual’s inter-action with the cooperative task Shu and Furuta (2005). Salas et al. Salas, Prince, et al. (1995a) further identify two critical abstractions underlying TSA: individual SA and team processes, where the latter encompasses the teamwork behaviors and cog-nitive processes that facilitate team performance and that cannot be reduced to the sum of individual awareness states. In contrast, Salas et al. describe TSA as a shared understanding of the situation among team members at a given moment Salas, Prince, Baker, and Shrestha (1995b). Similarly, another viewpoint characterizes it as a com-mon perspective among two or more individuals regarding environmental events, their implications, and likely developments She and Li (2017). Artman and Garbin Art-man and Garbis (1998) expand on this, defining TSA as a collaboratively constructed model that is partly shared and partly distributed, enabling anticipation of future states. Finally, Stanton et al. describe TSA as knowledge of a dynamic organizational structure and environment, actively formed and sustained through interactions with both the external world and other team members Stanton, Salmon, Walker, Salas, and and (2017). 

# 3 Taxonomy of Collective Awareness 

To provide a structured characterization of the awareness types discussed in the previ-ous section, this section presents a taxonomy organized along two key axes: the object of awareness and awareness distribution. Before examining how each awareness type is positioned along these axes, we introduce the illustrative example that will be used throughout this section to ground the abstract classifications in a concrete setting. The example concerns a heterogeneous robotic team cooperating in a Search and Rescue (SaR) operation. The team is composed of aerial robots (UAVs) equipped with cameras and image analysis capabilities, and ground robots (UGVs) equipped with manipulation capabilities. The mission unfolds across four interdependent phases: deployment, in which robots are dispatched to the affected area; reconnaissance, in which robots survey the environment, build maps, and search for survivors; rescuing, in which heterogeneous robots collaborate to remove debris and assist survivors; and monitoring, in which robots track survivors’ conditions and environmental hazards. These phases are not strictly sequential and often overlap, requiring the team to maintain awareness at multiple levels simultaneously under conditions of unreliable communication, dynamic environmental changes, and role-based task specialization. Concretely, during reconnaissance, UGVs must navigate debris-filled corridors without interfering with each other; UAVs detect and localize survivors and broadcast their location and assessed status to the whole team; and UAVs identify safe landing zones using aerodynamic models specific to aerial platforms and share these assessments with other UAVs for mission replanning. This scenario provides a concrete reference for illustrating how each awareness type is positioned along the taxonomy axes. The following figures illustrate how each awareness type manifests concretely within this setting. Figure 1 illustrates mutual awareness during the reconnaissance phase. The object of awareness is the planned trajectory of a peer agent, and each robot maintains its own localized interpretation of that trajectory rather than converging on a common representation, which is the defining characteristic of mutual awareness.

## 3.1 Object of Awareness 

Beyond the definitions discussed in Section 2, it is important to examine what agents or team members can be aware of within a collaborative system. Identifying the ele-ments in the environment or within the team that constitute the content of awareness is essential because the breadth and nature of these objects evolve alongside the sys-tem’s increasing awareness capabilities. As the awareness of the team becomes more sophisticated, agents transition from being aware of isolated features, such as the tra-jectory or state of neighboring agents, to developing an integrated understanding of the entire operational context, including tasks, roles, goals, and dynamic environmental conditions. A parallel effort to enumerate objects of awareness in human–robot teams, orga-nized along a complementary directional axis, was independently developed by Drury, Scholtz, and Yanco (2003). This subsection categorizes the three awareness types based on the scope and nature of the elements they concern. Figure 4 illustrates the hierarchical relationship among their respective objects of awareness. When examining the concept of mutual awareness, its primary objects are the other members of the team and their internal states, such as intentions, beliefs, goals, and plans. Mutual awareness emphasizes the reciprocal understanding that agents have of one another, which is fundamental to effective teamwork. This form of awareness is not only about knowing that other agents exist or recognizing their presence in the environment, but also involves awareness of what other agents are trying to achieve, how they interpret the current situation, and how they are likely to act based on that interpretation. Leveraging this awareness of other team members, agents can better anticipate the behavior of their teammates, adapt their own actions accordingly, and proactively coordinate in pursuit of shared objectives. This means that Shared SA involves not only an understanding of each agent’s internal states, such as their goals, inten-tions, and actions, but also a collectively held awareness of critical external factors in the environment. Shared situational awareness goes beyond mutual aware-ness by also encompassing elements external to the team, such as mission objectives, environmental constraints, or dynamic threats, that all agents must align on to oper-ate cohesively, making decisions based on a shared and synchronized understanding of the operational context. In the SaR example, the object of shared situational aware-ness is the location and assessed status of survivors detected during reconnaissance: mission-critical elements that all agents must integrate uniformly regardless of their individual spatial representations (Figure 2). Expanding to an even broader domain, Team Situation Awareness builds upon the foundations of both mutual and shared situational awareness by incorporating the structure and functional diversity within a team. As emphasized by Endsley and Jones in Endsley Mica R. (2001), one of the defining characteristics of a team is the presence of distinct roles, each associated with specific responsibilities and subgoals that contribute to the achievement of a common team objective. These role-based subgoals naturally give rise to varying information needs among agents, meaning that the situation awareness required by each team member is shaped not only by what is commonly shared across the team, but also by the unique demands of their individual roles. Consequently, team situational awareness encompasses both the shared aware-ness necessary for coordinated collaboration, captured by SSA, and the individualized awareness each agent must maintain to effectively perform their own functions. ## 3.2 Awareness Distribution 

A second dimension along which the three awareness types can be differentiated con-cerns how awareness is spread across agents in the team. At one end of the spectrum, awareness may be shared: all agents reach the same conclusions and maintain the same understanding of a given element, eliminating the need for ongoing coordination about those elements. At the other end, awareness may be distributed: agents form individ-ualized understandings of the same element, shaped by their own roles, perspectives, and available information Endsley Mica R. (2001) Stanton et al. (2006). We define awareness distribution for a multi-agent system as the degree to which awareness of a given element is uniform across agents in the system. This distribution can manifest in different forms depending on the nature of the awareness being studied and the coordination demands of the task at hand. Mutual awareness belongs to the distributed end of the spectrum, as it simply requires agents to be aware of each other’s presence, actions, or intentions without enforcing any alignment in their awareness of specific elements in the environment. 11 There is no requirement for agents to share the same understanding or inferences, allowing each to maintain individualized awareness shaped by their roles and perspec-tives. In the SaR example, this distributed character is evident in how two UGVs track each other’s planned trajectories during reconnaissance. One robot may represent its environment as a 2D occupancy grid while the other uses a costmap; each forms its own localized understanding of the peer’s intended path without requiring identical internal representations (Figure 1). Shared situational awareness, in contrast, is placed at the shared end of the spec-trum, as it demands that all agents maintain the same awareness regarding particular critical elements, supporting seamless coordination through identical interpretations of key aspects of the environment. In the SaR example, when a UAV detects and localizes a survivor, it broadcasts the location and assessed status to the entire team. Although each robot integrates this data into its own internal representation, the logi-cal content, that is, the identity, location, and status of the survivor, is identical across all agents, reflecting the shared character of this awareness type (Figure 2). Team situational awareness lies between these two extremes: it requires shared awareness of certain task-relevant elements necessary for coordinated action, while allowing individual agents to maintain divergent or role-specific awareness of other ele-ments not critical to joint decision-making. This partial alignment enables flexibility and task specialization while still supporting cohesive team behavior. In the SaR exam-ple, all robots share awareness of survivor locations and mission progress, while UAVs additionally maintain their own role-specific assessments of airspace conditions and safe landing zones. This combination of shared and individualized awareness allows the team to coordinate effectively while each platform contributes specialized knowledge that the other cannot produce (Figure 3). 

## 3.3 Synthesized Definitions 

Drawing on the two axes established above, we state a precise definition for each awareness type that captures both its object and its distribution.  

> Definition 1 (Mutual Awareness) The awareness each agent holds of its teammates’ inter-nal states, including their intentions, beliefs, goals, and plans, where each agent forms an individualized understanding shaped by its own perspective and role.

In the SaR scenario, mutual awareness governs how UGVs track each other’s planned trajectories during reconnaissance. When UGV A communicates its path, UGV B integrates that information into its own costmap, forming an understanding of A’s intentions adapted to its own internal architecture (Figure 1). The object of awareness is the peer’s planned path; the distribution is individualized, as B’s repre-sentation need not match A’s internal encoding, only capture the same spatial intent in B’s own terms. 12 Definition 2 (Shared Situational Awareness) The awareness all agents in a team must maintain of the environmental and mission elements that fall within every agent’s require-ments, specifically their intersection, such that all agents reach an aligned interpretation of the information essential for coordinated action. 

When a UAV detects a survivor and broadcasts their location and assessed status, every agent must integrate the same conclusion regardless of its internal representation: a survivor is present at a specific location with a given urgency (Figure 2). The object of awareness is the survivor’s location and status; the distribution is shared, as no agent may hold a divergent interpretation of this element without risking conflicting rescue priorities.  

> Definition 3 (Team Situational Awareness) The complete awareness a robotic team must maintain, encompassing both the shared understanding of mission-critical elements common to all agents (SSA) and elements of the environment that are relevant to the mission yet specific to certain roles, such as platform-dependent terrain or airspace conditions, for which divergent, role-shaped understanding is appropriate.

The full awareness landscape of the SaR mission integrates the shared elements above with role-specific knowledge that is mission-relevant but not required by every platform. UAVs must assess wind conditions and identify safe landing zones; UGVs must track debris properties and terrain traversability (Figure 3). Neither is captured by MA or SSA alone, yet both are necessary for effective mission execution. TSA encompasses all three layers: peer-state awareness, shared mission-critical awareness, and role-specific environmental knowledge. 

# 4 Conclusions 

This article has addressed two fundamental questions about collective awareness in multi-robot systems, contributing a conceptual framework that clarifies the theory-practice gap surrounding awareness terminology in multi-robot coordination. 1. What are the distinct requirements of the different awareness types, and how do they relate to each other? 

Our structured taxonomy categorizes collective awareness along two dimensions, awareness distribution and object of awareness, establishing that mutual aware-ness, shared situational awareness, and team situational awareness form a layered hierarchy with distinct requirements. MA requires that agents maintain aware-ness of each other’s beliefs, plans, and states to enable basic coordination. SSA builds upon MA by demanding shared semantic understanding of mission-critical environmental elements, ensuring aligned interpretation across the team. TSA integrates both shared awareness and role-specific knowledge, enabling specialized coordination while maintaining collective coherence. The Search and Rescue case study illustrates how mission requirements decompose into these specific aware-ness specifications, showing that each phase demands different combinations of 13 awareness types. 2. What precise definitions can be established for each awareness type that character-ize their distinct requirements and roles in multi-robot coordination? 

The taxonomy yields the three definitions stated in Section 3, each grounded in both axes. Together they establish that MA, SSA, and TSA form a layered structure with distinct distribution and content requirements, rather than inter-changeable labels for the same phenomenon. This taxonomy provides a conceptual foundation for more principled design and meaningful comparison of multi-robot coordination approaches. Despite the concep-tual clarity this taxonomy provides, several open challenges remain. Future work should formalize the requirements identified by the taxonomy as verifiable system spec-ifications, develop architectural components that instantiate these requirements, and empirically evaluate their effect on coordination performance in deployed systems.
