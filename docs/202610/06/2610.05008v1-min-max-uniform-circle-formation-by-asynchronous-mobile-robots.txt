Abstract 

Given a set of point robots R in the Euclidean plane and a target circle C enclosing all robot positions, the Min-Max Uniform Circle Formation (MMUCF) problem requires the robots to move to distinct positions on C such that the final configuration forms a regular n-gon while minimizing the maximum distance traveled by any robot. Uniform circle formation is a fundamental coordination task in swarm robotics with applications in perimeter monitoring, surveillance, boundary coverage, and pattern formation. The literature does not address the optimization of the maximum individual displacement during the formation process. In this work, we study the min–max versions of the circle formation and uniform circle formation problems, where the goal is to minimize the maximum distance traveled by any robot. We consider these problems under the ASYN C model, where robots are autonomous, anonymous, identical, homogeneous, oblivious, and silent, and operate under the Look–Compute–Move model with non-rigid motion. We first give necessary conditions for a deterministic solution and then present deterministic, distributed, and collision-free algorithms that form a circle and a uniform circle in finite time while minimizing the maximum movement. The algorithms ensure that robots reach distinct positions on the circle and, in the uniform case, equally spaced positions on C under the considered model. 

Keywords: Uniform Circle Formation, Autonomous Mobile Robots, Asynchronous Model, Non-Rigid Movements, Persistent Lights, Min–Max Travel-Distance Optimization, Optimal Assignment. 

# 1 Introduction 

In the past two decades, multi-robot systems have become an important research focus because of their robustness, scalability, and versatility in diverse applications [1, 8, 21, 24, 28]. A swarm of mobile robots is a distributed multi-agent system consisting of numerous small, autonomous , homogeneous , and anonymous 

robots that cooperate to accomplish a common task. Each robot operates as an independent computational unit with limited sensing, processing, and actuation capabilities, and coordinated interactions among robots lead to complex collective behavior. Inspired by the self-organizing mechanisms observed in social insects, swarm-based systems are particularly suited for hazardous or inaccessible environments where human intervention is impractical. Their fully decentralized nature enables deployment in unknown and dynamic scenarios and provides robustness against individual failures. In addition, the relatively simple

In distributed computing, theoretical research on multi-robot systems has focused on coordination and complexity issues arising in systems of autonomous mobile entities operating without centralized control. Several computational models emphasizing different capabilities have been proposed, including models with explicit communication [17], memory [20], or identifiers [30]. 

Our Model. In this paper, we consider the oblivious (no memory of past actions) mobile robots model. In this model, robots are simple computational entities represented as points in the Euclidean plane and operate in repeated Look-Compute-Move cycles. During each cycle, an active robot observes the positions of other robots in its local coordinate system (Look), computes a destination based on the observed configuration (Compute), and moves toward that destination (Move). Movements may be rigid ,meaning the robot always reaches its destination, or non-rigid , meaning the robot may be stopped before reaching the destination but is guaranteed to move at least a minimum distance δ toward it. The robots are autonomous (operate without centralized coordination), homogeneous (identical in capabilities and behavior), anonymous (without unique identifiers), oblivious (no memory of past actions), and silent (no explicit communication). Robots do not retain any memory between computational cycles. In extended models, they may be equipped with a constant amount of persistent memory [20], usually implemented through externally visible lights, commonly known as the luminous model , with a finite set of colors [27]. Such persistent memory can be used for communication and state retention (FALL model) [13], only for remembering the robot’s internal state (FSTATE model) [12], or only for communication without storing past states (FCOMM model) [20]. Each robot uses its own local Cartesian coordinate system centreed at its current position, with its own unit of length and orientation of the coordinate axes. Depending on the model [29], different levels of agreement among robots may be assumed. In the strongest case, robots agree on the directions and orientations of all axes, forming a common global reference frame. In weaker settings, robots may agree only on the direction and orientation of one axis. Robots may also share chirality, meaning they have a common notion of clockwise direction even if their axes are not aligned. In the weakest case, robots may have no agreement on axis directions or orientations, and their coordinate systems are completely independent. In this paper, we consider robots with one axis of agreement and equipped with visible lights. Robot activations are controlled by an adversarial but fair scheduler under one of the standard models. In the fully synchronous model ( FSYN C ), all robots execute their cycles simultaneously in discrete rounds. In the semi-synchronous model ( SSYN C ), an arbitrary subset of robots is activated in each round. In the asynchronous model ( ASYN C ), there is no common notion of time, and the duration of each phase is finite but unpredictable. Fairness guarantees that each robot is activated infinitely often, allowing progress to be measured in rounds or epochs depending on the synchronization model. 

Problem Setting. Let C be a circle of finite radius h, and let R = {r1, . . . , r n} be a set of n robots initially placed at distinct positions inside C. Let P = {p1, . . . , p n} be a set of n target points on the boundary of C. The goal is to move the robots so that each robot occupies a distinct point of P while minimizing the maximum distance traveled by any robot. In the MMUCF problem, P consists of the vertices of a regular n-gon inscribed in C, i.e., the points are equally spaced along the circumference. Bhattacharya et al. [4] studied a centralized version of this problem in which robot movements are computed centrally. In contrast, we consider a distributed setting where robots act autonomously and make decisions based only on their local observations. 2Our Contributions. In this problem, we prove three impossibility results in Section 3 (Theorems 1–3), showing that it cannot be solved without additional assumptions about the robots. In Section 4, we then design a deterministic algorithm MinMaxUniformCircle() for robots equipped with visible lights and one-axis agreement that achieves uniform circle formation under the min–max objective. These results indicate that the uniform variant is strictly more difficult than the non-uniform variant and requires additional capabilities to be solvable. 

Related Works. Geometric formation problems for autonomous mobile robots have been extensively studied in the distributed computing literature since the seminal work of Suzuki and Yamashita [29], which introduced the framework for pattern formation by anonymous robots operating without centralized control. Subsequent research by Flocchini et al. [19], Défago and Konagaya [10], Défago and Souissi [11], and Flocchini et al. [16] established feasibility and impossibility results for circle formation and related tasks under different synchronization models and visibility constraints. In the circle formation problem, robots must move to the boundary of a common circle, and the problem has been studied under asynchronous settings, limited visibility, and weak agreement on coordinate systems. A stricter variant, Uniform Circle Formation ( UCF ), requires robots to occupy equally spaced positions on the circle and has received significant attention due to its symmetry requirements. Deterministic solutions for UCF 

have been obtained even under the fully asynchronous model by Flocchini et al. [18] and Viglietta [22], resolving long-standing open cases. Several extensions also consider additional capabilities such as persistent visible lights, obstructed visibility, fat robots, and rigid movements; for example, UCF with persistent lights and rigid motion has been solved by Pattanayak et al. [25]. Other works study trade-offs between time, memory, and communication complexity, including results by Feletti et al. [14, 15] and Mondal et al. [23]. However, most existing works focus on feasibility and symmetry breaking rather than optimizing movement costs, and relatively little attention has been given to minimizing the maximum distance traveled by any robot in fully distributed and oblivious settings. Bhagat et al. [3] study this objective in a distributed setting for circle formation, whereas Bhattacharya et al. [4] consider a centralized formulation. # 3 Impossibility Results for MMUCF Problem 

In this section we present two impossibility results for the MMUCF problem. These impossibility results are with respect to the minimal capabilities of the robots required to solve the problem. According to the Theorem 2, during the execution of each algorithm A ∈ fG∗, first robots require to compute their final destination points on the circle C and then accordingly move the towards respective destination points. A robot can use the algorithm presented in [4] or in [7] to compute its final destination point. However, this simple strategy can not be adopted directly to a swarm of robots. There are many factors that forbid the direct adaptation of this strategy, like the activation schedule of the robots, the non-rigid movements of the robots, the lack of a global coordinate system, the obliviousness of the robots, etc.

# 4.1 High-level overview of the algorithm 

We present MinMaxUniformCircle() , a deterministic distributed algorithm for solving MMUCF 

under the luminous, oblivious, non-rigid ASYN C model. From the initial configuration R(t0), every robot computes the optimal min–max value λ∗

> C

, a regular target-point set P, and an optimal assignment 

fopt (t0) : R(t0) → P using the algorithm in [4]. The main challenge under obliviousness and non-rigid movements is to preserve the initially selected target-point set P and optimal assignment fopt throughout the execution, since robots may observe intermediate configurations after partially completed moves. The preservation mechanism depends on the critical set R∗(t0). In a unique-critical configuration, the critical robot determines a radial reference for P; if the critical robot is located at the centre O, a short deterministic movement first establishes the radial reference. Multiple-critical configurations are handled according to the positions of the non-critical robots and unreserved targets relative to the collective critical region D∗(t0). Stationary critical robots normally preserve the target-point set. In the special configuration Kct mcr , with exactly two critical robots, a virtual dummy point is used to reconstruct the same target-point set during the movement of candidate robot (see Definition 3). # 4.5 Computation of the Assignment 

There may exist more than one optimal assignment of the robot positions to the points in P. We have the following observation. To solve the MMUCF problem, robots need to agree on a common optimal assignment fopt to the points in P. Moreover, in the asynchronous setting with non-rigid movements, robots may observe intermediate configurations. Therefore, the initially computed assignment fopt must be preserved throughout the execution; in particular, no robot may change its assigned target on the basis of an intermediate snapshot. 13 In this section, we describe an algorithm to output such an assignment. We assume that, in the initial configuration, no robot lies on the circle C. However, during the execution of the algorithm, robots move to the points on C. Our algorithm uses axis-only agreement to compute such an assignment fopt (as stated in Theorem 1), particularly in symmetric configurations or when a candidate robot has two target points at the same minimum distance. The computation of the optimal assignment fopt depends on the structure of the critical robot set in the initial configuration R(t0). The configuration may contain either a unique critical robot or multiple critical robots. The unique-critical case is further divided according to whether the critical robot is located at the centre O of the target circle C, since a robot located at O does not initially determine a unique radial target point. Accordingly, the computation of fopt is divided into the following three cases: (1) the configuration contains a unique critical robot that is not located at O; (2) the configuration contains a unique critical robot located at O; and (3) the configuration contains multiple critical robots. The algorithms presented in [4] and [7] are used to compute λ∗

> C

(R(t0)) and identify the corresponding critical set, which determines the assignment procedure to be applied.

# 4.6 Correctness of MinMaxUniformCircle() 

The correctness of the algorithm follows from the invariance of the selected optimal solution and the finite progress of robots toward their assigned targets. We first prove that the target-point set and the corresponding assignment remain unchanged during the execution. We then show that a selected candidate preserves its assigned target until reaching it, and that simultaneously selected candidates have distinct reserved targets. These properties ensure collision-free movements. Finally, the fairness of the scheduler and the non-rigid movement assumption guarantee that every robot reaches its target in finite time. Hence, the final configuration realizes the initial optimal assignment and achieves the optimal min–max travel distance.

4.6.5 Collision Freedom and Finite Progress Theorem 4 (Collision-Free Execution of MinMaxUniformCircle() ). If all robots initially occupy pairwise distinct positions, then every execution of MinMaxUniformCircle() preserves this property, provided that every authorized movement phase obeys the clear-path rule. Proof. By Lemma 8, the targets assigned to the robots in S are pairwise distinct. By the clear-path rule, their movement paths satisfy the required disjointedness conditions. Consider an arbitrary movement phase starting at time ts. Let S denote the set of robots authorized to move during this phase, and let 

pi be the target assigned to each robot ri ∈ S. By the clear-path rule, the following conditions hold: 1. the assigned targets {pi : ri ∈ S} are pairwise distinct and unoccupied at time ts;2. for every ri ∈ S, (ri(ts), p i] ∩ { rk(ts) : rk /∈ S} = ∅;

3. for any two distinct robots ri, r j ∈ S, [ri(ts), p i] ∩ [rj (ts), p j ] = ∅.

By Lemma 7, every robot in S preserves its assigned target throughout the phase. Consequently, for every time t during this phase, ri(t) ∈ [ri(ts), p i], r i ∈ S. Under non-rigid motion, an adversarial interruption may stop a robot at an intermediate point of this segment, but cannot alter its prescribed trajectory. Moreover, while robots display TargetTrace or ReferenceTrace , no robot outside S is allowed to initiate a movement. Robots displaying λAnchor or λ− 

> Anchor

also remain stationary. Hence, during the considered phase, only robots in S may change their positions. Assume, for contradiction, that a collision occurs during this phase, and consider the first such collision. There are only two possibilities. If a moving robot ri ∈ S collides with a stationary robot rk /∈ S, then rk(ts) ∈ (ri(ts), p i], contradicting Condition 2. Otherwise, two robots ri, r j ∈ S collide. Their common collision point must then belong to both prescribed movement segments, implying [ri(ts), p i] ∩ [rj (ts), p j ]̸ = ∅, which contradicts Condition 3. Thus, no collision can occur during the phase. In particular, when a robot ri reaches pi, it changes its light to TargetReached and remains stationary there; since pi is included in its movement segment, Condition 3 already prevents any simultaneously moving robot from reaching the same position. In every subsequent movement phase, this stationary robot is again taken into account by the clear-path rule. The preliminary displacement of a centre-critical robot is also collision-free, since it is performed entirely inside an empty sector and within its prescribed safe distance ρc. Therefore, every movement phase of MinMaxUniformCircle() is collision-free. Since the robots occupy pairwise distinct positions initially, induction over successive movement phases implies that no two robots ever occupy the same position. Hence, every execution of MinMaxUniformCircle() is collision-free.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we studied the min–max uniform circle formation problem for autonomous mobile robots under the asynchronous and non-rigid movement model. We established three impossibility results and proposed a distributed algorithm for robots equipped with externally visible lights and one-axis agreement. The algorithm preserves the optimal target-point set and assignment despite asynchronous 30 activations and non-rigid interruptions, guarantees collision-free execution, and achieves the optimal min–max travel distance in finite time. Several directions remain open, including weakening the robot capabilities, considering the case where the target circle is not given in advance, studying limited visibility settings, and developing fault-tolerant variants in the presence of obstacles.
