Abstract 

Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) increasingly perform material transport in production logistics, where their operation is governed by job generation, dispatching and robot control. We present MoRoOp, a dataset of AMR operations recorded in a laboratory kit preparation and supply scenario over nine eight-hour shifts. During each shift, an AMR executed stochastically generated kit supply, empty-box refill and charging jobs. The dataset links job specifications, the operations constituting each job, dispatch events documenting operation state transitions and outcomes, and robot-state observations comprising po-sition, orientation, velocity, per-wheel state of charge and diagnostics. It contains 1,382 jobs, 4,815 operations, 19,352 dispatch events and 140,386 robot-state observations together with the kit specifications used during job generation. The dataset was recorded in an operating laboratory environment, and technically valid observations of delays, obstructed navigation and unsuccessful operations were retained. Both raw and cleaned robot-state tables are provided. Documented reuse directions include the evaluation of AI agents on operational decision records, disturbance detection, operation prediction, data-driven sim-ulation and event-log analysis. 

## Background & Summary 

Production logistics comprises the storage, handling and transport activities that supply and connect production processes. Increasing product variety, smaller batch sizes and more frequently reconfigured production structures 1arXiv:2610.02428v1 [cs.RO] 1 Oct 2026 raise the complexity of internal material flows and the effort required to man-age them [1]. Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) have become a widely used resource in this context. In contrast to automated guided vehicles, which fol-low predetermined paths and are coordinated by a central controller, AMRs localize and plan their motion autonomously, and transport tasks can be assigned to them dynamically during execution [2, 3]. The associated plan-ning and control decisions, including task allocation, dispatching, routing and charging, have been studied extensively in intralogistics and warehous-ing research [2, 4, 5]. Operating an AMR in such a system generates data at several levels. At the planning level, transport jobs are specified with attributes such as job type, requested content, release time and due date. At the execution level, a dispatching component decomposes jobs into executable operations, resolves their dependencies, and records state transitions, execution outcomes and termination reasons as discrete events. At the resource level, the AMR re-ports its position and orientation, velocity, navigation targets, battery state and diagnostic information as a time series. These levels differ in struc-ture, temporal resolution and semantics, and they are typically produced and stored by different software components. They are consequently also analysed separately. Order and event data are treated in planning, control and process analysis, while robot telemetry is treated in navigation, diag-nostics and maintenance. Making such heterogeneous sources jointly usable is a recurring requirement in production logistics [6, 7]. The motivation for this dataset follows from that separation. A job record states what was requested and when it was due. The associated op-eration and dispatch-event records state how the request was decomposed and what happened to each part of it, including timeouts and unsuccessful executions. The robot-state observations describe the physical behaviour of the resource during the same period. When these records are available together, the executed behaviour of the system can be reconstructed and related to what was planned, for instance whether a late job followed from a delayed release, an extended waiting period at a station, a navigation dis-turbance or an intermediate charging decision. The same correspondence between plan and execution is the input required by digital shadows and digital twins of material handling systems [8, 9] and by methods that con-struct simulation models directly from recorded operational data [10]. Arecent review of publicly available manufacturing datasets groups them into machine-level sensor recordings on one side and management-level produc-tion records on the other, and notes that documentation of the monitored processes is often incomplete [11]. 2The dataset was recorded in the laboratory demonstrator associated with the concept of Self-Organizing Production Logistics (SOPL) [12]. SOPL ad-dresses production logistics systems that are exposed to variability, dynamic interdependencies and frequent disturbances, and proposes multi-agent ar-chitectures in which embodied and non-embodied agents coordinate through event-driven interaction, shared semantic knowledge structures and digital twins. The order-driven kit preparation and supply scenario introduced in that work is the scenario used here, although no data from the present cam-paign were reported there. During the data-collection campaign, jobs were generated by fixed stochastic rules and executed under a rule-based dis-patching policy rather than by agent-based decision-making. The released records therefore document the operation of the demonstrator under a rule-based control policy and provide a reference for later configurations of the same physical system. Publicly available datasets involving mobile robots in shared indoor envi-ronments are largely oriented towards perception and navigation. Datasets such as TH ¨OR-MAGNI provide human and robot trajectories recorded with motion capture and on-board sensors [13], and comparable recordings have been published for a mobile robot performing retail tasks alongside human operators [14]. A second group of datasets releases machine-level signals without the work that produced them, ranging from high-frequency signals of a stationary manipulator recorded per motion cycle [15] to long sensor recordings of a single industrial asset annotated with reported failures [16]. Event logs published for process mining research provide case and activity structure with administrative timestamps, but contain no physical state of the executing resources [17]. Datasets combining both perspectives are less common. Examples include a multiphase flow facility benchmark that pairs an operation log with process measurements [18] and a smart-factory dataset that pairs OPC UA logs with sensor measurements of stationary stations [19]. In research on AMR dispatching and routing, evaluation is commonly based on generated benchmark instances [20]. The released dataset comprises nine eight-hour shifts recorded on five consecutive days and contains 1,382 jobs, 4,815 operations, 19,352 dispatch events and 140,386 robot-state observations. One further shift was recorded but excluded from the release because of an implementation error in the job generator, as documented in the Technical Validation section. Three characteristics of the released data are relevant for reuse. First, the data were recorded in an operating laboratory in which personnel could cross or temporarily obstruct the planned trajectory of the AMR. Waiting, delayed execution, obstructed navigation, operation timeouts and unsuccessful op-3erations occurred during normal operation and were retained. The quality-screening procedure removed only technically invalid robot-state records, such as invalid localization states or physically implausible values, and the raw records are released alongside the cleaned ones. These records were retained deliberately, since they document the operating conditions under which the campaign took place. Second, the robot state was polled at a fixed frequency but stored only when a reported value changed, so the robot-state tables form an irregularly sampled time series. Third, station processing times were assigned by the job generator and represent waiting periods of the AMR rather than measurements of physical human work. The recording documents the operation of a single AMR. This reflects the configuration of the demonstrator at the time of data collection and keeps the correspondence between dispatching decisions, executed operations and robot-state observations unambiguous, because every state observation be-longs to the resource that executed the operation under consideration. Ef-fects arising from the interaction of several vehicles, such as congestion, right-of-way conflicts and fleet-level task allocation, are consequently not represented. The demonstration roadmap defined for SOPL foresees in-creasingly distributed configurations [12], for which the present recording provides a single-resource reference. The combination of job specifications, operation records, dispatch events and robot-state observations from the same system allows several forms of reuse. Agents built on foundation models are increasingly proposed to plan and supervise the operation of physical logistics resources, which is also the direction taken by SOPL [12]. Assessing whether such an agent decides well requires a record of what was requested, of the executable plan that followed from the request, of what the resource physically did while carrying it out, and of what happened when execution was disturbed. Work on grounding language model plans in the actions a robot can perform [21], on replanning from execution feedback [22] and on explaining failures from recorded robot experience [23] rests on records of this kind, and applications of such models in manufacturing and supply chain operations are being surveyed [24, 25]. In this dataset each job is present as the original request with its type, requested content and timing, as the ordered set of operations into which it was decomposed together with the dependencies between them, as the sequence of dispatcher events that queued, started and closed those opera-tions, and as the robot-state record of the execution that followed, including the periods in which the AMR reported an obstruction. The decisions of an agent placed in the same situation can therefore be compared with the operations that the rule-based dispatcher produced and with the execution 4that resulted. The robot-state tables constitute multivariate time series recorded dur-ing operation that included periods of obstructed navigation, and are there-fore applicable to time-series anomaly detection and to fault detection and diagnosis for industrial robots [26, 27]. The reported stuck conditions, navi-gation states and per-wheel states of charge allow disturbances observed by the AMR itself to be located in time and, through the recorded timestamps, related to the operation that was being executed. The recorded release, start and finish timestamps, together with the due dates and execution outcomes of the jobs, support the prediction of operation durations and job lateness in analogy to predictive process monitoring on business process event logs [28]. Job and operation records also provide the arrival, routing and process-ing information required by methods that generate discrete-event simulation models and digital twins directly from operational data [10, 29, 30], subject to the qualification noted above that station processing times originate from the job generator rather than from measured physical work. Dispatch events can be converted into event logs in which either jobs or operations serve as cases, which makes the data accessible to process mining techniques that have been applied to internal logistics event data in automotive produc-tion [31] and surveyed for Industry 4.0 settings more broadly [32]. Because jobs, operations, kits and stations are interrelated object types, the records can equally be prepared as object-centric event data. Finally, the scenario is small, physically interpretable and completely specified, which makes the dataset usable in teaching on production logistics, discrete-event simulation, process mining and industrial data analysis. The dataset is released following the FAIR principles [33]. Version 1.0.1 is archived in the KTH Data Repository [34] and mirrored on Hugging Face [35], and example code for loading and analysing the released tables is archived with the dataset toolkit [36]. 

## Methods 

The dataset was collected at the joint IPU laboratory facility of the De-partment of Production Engineering at KTH Royal Institute of Technology. Data acquisition was conducted over five consecutive days, comprising two eight-hour shifts per day. During each shift, an AMR executed dynami-cally generated material-transport jobs within a laboratory production lo-gistics scenario. The job generator created transport requests according to 5predefined stochastic rules, while the operation dispatcher translated these requests into executable operations and coordinated their execution on the AMR. The resulting dataset combines job specifications, operation records, dispatch events and robot-state observations. Scenario description The dataset was collected in a production logistics scenario previously introduced in [12]. The scenario comprises an AMR and four stations representing activities commonly encountered in production logistics environments, see Figure 1. It implements a kit preparation and supply process in which an assembly station dynamically requests predefined kits. Each kit is represented by a Eurobox containing a specified combination of components. In the laboratory implementation, the components are toy building bricks available in four colors and two sizes. Euroboxes are used as load carriers for both individual component supplies and completed kits. To distinguish these two uses, they are referred to as component boxes and kit boxes , respectively. During data collection, the AMR state was queried at a frequency of 1 Hz. A new robot-state message was published to the robot states Kafka topic only when at least one reported state value differed from the previously retrieved state. Consequently, the robot state was evaluated at a fixed polling frequency, whereas the recorded observations form an event-triggered, irregularly sampled time series. The operations instantiated by the job generator were managed by the OperationDispatcher software module. The dispatcher maintained a queue of pending operations, evaluated operation dependencies, and coordinated their execution on the AMR. Information about generated jobs was pub-lished to the jobs topic, while operation state transitions and execution 12 outcomes were published to the dispatch events topic. The resulting data collection pipeline therefore comprised three Kafka streams: jobs ,

robot states , and dispatch events .Node-RED was used as the data-ingestion component. It consumed mes-sages from the three Kafka streams, transformed the messages into SQL statements, and inserted the resulting records into a MariaDB database. Timestamps contained in the incoming messages were converted to ISO 8601 representation and standardized to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) be-fore storage. Following completion of the data-collection campaign, the database records were exported and subjected to quality screening and curation. Quality screening of the robot-state data included identification and removal of records containing invalid localization states or physically implausible val-ues. The original robot-state records were retained as the raw data record, while the screened records were stored separately as the cleaned robot-state table. No interpolation, resampling, smoothing, or imputation was applied during this process. 

## Data Records 

Version 1.0.1 of the MoRoOp dataset is permanently archived in the KTH Data Repository [34]. A copy of the same version is additionally available through Hugging Face [35] to facilitate programmatic access and dataset exploration. All tabular data are provided in Apache Parquet format. Par-quet is a column-oriented file format that preserves the tabular schema and supports efficient storage and selective column access. The dataset is organized around four related data entities: jobs, opera-tions, dispatch events, and robot-state observations. Jobs represent material-transport requests generated by the job generator. Each job comprises one or more operations. Dispatch events record changes occurring during the lifecycle of individual operations. Robot-state observations provide time-stamped measurements and status information from the AMR. Jobs, op-erations and dispatch events can be related through their identifiers, while temporal alignment with robot-state observations is established using the recorded timestamps. data directory contains the dataset-level kits.parquet table and nine shift-specific subdirectories. The subdirectories follow the naming convention 

YYYY MM DD shift , where YYYY MM DD denotes the date of data collection and 13 shift identifies the morning or evening shift. Each shift directory contains five Parquet tables: dispatch events , jobs , operations , robot state raw ,and robot state cleaned . The file names include the corresponding date and shift identifier to preserve their association when individual files are accessed independently of the directory structure.

Consequently, the robot-state tables do not constitute regularly sampled time series, and the interval between successive records may exceed one second. The fields current position id , navigating to position id , and last navigated position id use the internal position identifiers of the AMR navigation system. Position identifiers 135, 136, 137, and 138 correspond to the charging station, warehouse, kitting station, and assembly station, respectively. The * robot state cleaned.parquet table contains the corresponding robot-state records after the quality-screening procedure described in the Technical Validation section. shift. 19 Technical Validation 

The experimental system and data collection pipeline were monitored through-out each shift using dedicated dashboards for the AMR and the job-generation infrastructure. This continuous monitoring allowed technical problems af-fecting the AMR, job execution, or data acquisition to be identified during operation. Following data collection, the completeness and consistency of the recorded tables were evaluated separately for each shift. Table 7 reports the numbers of records contained in the respective shift-specific tables. Among the nine shifts included in the released dataset, the number of recorded jobs ranged from 148 to 156 per shift. The corresponding numbers of operations and dis-patch events were also comparable across these shifts. These record counts indicate that the stochastic job-generation process produced broadly compa-rable workloads while retaining the intended variability in job inter-arrival times, sampled kits, sampled processing times and charging activities. The 2026 07 30 evening shift initially contained considerably fewer records than the remaining shifts. Subsequent investigation identified an implemen-tation error in the job-generation procedure. After an operation timeout occurred while the AMR was traveling to execute an empty-box refill job, the internal refill-job state was not reset correctly. Consequently, no fur-ther empty-box refill jobs were generated during the shift, although empty kit boxes continued to accumulate at the assembly station. The resulting recording therefore did not represent the job-generation procedure specified for the data collection campaign. The entire shift was excluded from the released dataset because the observed inconsistency resulted from a software implementation error rather than from a valid operational disturbance. The internal consistency of the remaining records was evaluated using the identifiers and lifecycle information contained in the different data ta-bles. Each job was checked for a valid unique identifier and references to its constituent operations. The operation identifiers were subsequently used to relate the jobs to the corresponding operation and dispatch-event records. Timestamps were checked for chronological consistency, and the recorded operation states and outcomes were examined for consistency with the op-eration lifecycle. These checks verified that the relationships among jobs, operations, and dispatch events were preserved in the released dataset. The robot-state records underwent an additional quality-screening proce-dure. Records containing invalid localization states or physically implausible values were identified and removed. The screening criteria were restricted to technical validity and did not target unusual but valid operational be-20 haviour. No interpolation, resampling, smoothing, or imputation was ap-plied after the removal of invalid records. The raw robot-state tables are provided alongside the cleaned tables to make the curation process trans-parent and to allow users to apply alternative quality-screening procedures. The validation procedure deliberately retained technically valid observations of operational disturbances. Data collection was conducted in an operating laboratory environment in which personnel could cross or temporarily ob-struct the planned trajectory of the AMR. Such interactions could result in waiting, changes in navigation behaviour, delayed operation execution, or unsuccessful operations. These records were retained when the associated robot states and system events were technically valid. The dataset therefore represents the operational conditions encountered during the data collec-tion campaign rather than an idealized, disturbance-free execution of the production logistics scenario. ## Usage Notes 

All timestamps are expressed in ISO 8601 format and standardized to Co-ordinated Universal Time (UTC). Records from the different data streams can therefore be aligned temporally using their timestamps. Job, operation, and dispatch-event identifiers provide additional relational links between generated jobs and their execution history. The robot-state data should be treated as an irregularly sampled time series. Although the AMR state was queried at 1 Hz, a record was stored 21 only when the reported state differed from the preceding observation. The time interval between successive robot-state observations can therefore ex-ceed one second. Users requiring regularly sampled data should perform resampling as part of their analysis and select an interpolation or aggrega-tion strategy appropriate for their application. Both raw and cleaned robot-state tables are provided. The raw tables preserve the observations recorded by the data-ingestion pipeline, including observations subsequently identified as technically invalid. The cleaned ta-bles exclude observations identified during the quality-screening procedure described in the Technical Validation section. Users developing alternative quality criteria are therefore encouraged to use the raw tables as the starting point for their own curation. The relationships between jobs, operations, and dispatch events can be reconstructed using the corresponding identifiers. The temporal relationship between these records and robot-state observations can be established using their UTC timestamps. When reconstructing operation trajectories, users should account for the fact that station processing times represent waiting periods assigned to the AMR and are not direct measurements of physical human or automated processing times. The dataset contains observations from a laboratory production-logistics environment. Consequently, the recorded robot behaviour reflects the spe-cific physical layout, navigation configuration, job-generation parameters, AMR hardware, and software components used during the data-collection campaign. The dataset should therefore not be interpreted as a statistically representative sample of industrial AMR operations. 

## Data Availability 

Version 1.0.1 of the MoRoOp dataset is permanently archived in the KTH Data Repository [34] and constitutes the version of record associated with this publication. The repository contains the complete released dataset, including the kit definitions and the nine released shift-specific datasets. A copy of Version 1.0.1 is also available through Hugging Face [35] to facilitate programmatic access and dataset exploration.
