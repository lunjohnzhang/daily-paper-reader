# GAMBIT: Learning to Plan Continuous Multi-Robot Trajectories 

Rishabh Jain 1, Akmaral Moldagalieva 2, Lorenzo Magnino 1, Michael Amir 1,Keisuke Okumura 1,3, Ajay Shankar 1, Wolfgang H¨ onig 2 and Amanda Prorok 1

Abstract — GAMBIT is an opening chess move in which a player sacrifices a piece, typically a pawn, to gain a positional advantage later in the game. Analogously, in multi-robot coor-dination, individual robots may need to forgo locally reward-maximising behaviours to improve overall team performance. Such self-sacrificial behaviours are difficult to capture with manually designed heuristics, particularly in dense, interaction-rich environments. Focusing on double-integrator continuous dynamics, this work studies how to learn such coordinated heuristics over motion primitives for multi-robot trajectory execution. Our framework, GAMBIT , first learns coordinated motion-primitive selection through imitation learning and sub-sequently fine-tunes the policy through reinforcement learning. We further introduce a safeguarded rollout mechanism with backup trajectories that guarantees collision-free execution at all times. Experiments demonstrate that GAMBIT substantially outperforms a range of baselines, including centralised motion planners and decentralised reactive planners, while exhibiting strong scalability. In particular, it coordinates over a thousand robots with planning latency below a few hundred milliseconds in continuous domains. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Generating trajectory plans using motion primitives has been a successful paradigm for deriving potentially long-horizon, feasible motions to steer a robot—with applications ranging from autonomous driving to manoeuvres of aerial robots [1]–[3]. While its multi-robot counterpart has also been studied [4], [5], a critical challenge in achieving fast and scalable planning remains the design of effective heuristics 

that evaluate how “good” each primitive is. Designing such a heuristic is challenging even in the single-robot case, as accurate cost-to-go estimates become difficult to obtain for higher-order dynamics beyond simple point-mass models; the multi-robot case further requires reasoning about coordina-tion among agents. One could design a trivial heuristic based on an interaction-agnostic single-robot heuristic, but such approximations can get significantly distorted in interaction-rich multi-agent environments, where effective coordination may require non-greedy motions, such as temporarily moving away from the goal to accommodate other agents. Learning agent-wise heuristics to synthesise coordination has proven effective in the discrete-state, grid-world multi-agent pathfinding (MAPF) domain [6]–[8], where the objec-tive is to find conflict-free paths for multiple agents on a four-connected grid. A natural question then arises: can we effectively transfer this knowledge to continuous state spaces? With some adaptation, this transfer appears possible using action synthesis based on motion primitives; however, the transition to continuous state spaces introduces fundamental challenges. B. Planning with Motion Primitives 

A common approach to deriving such control sequences is via numerical optimisation, e.g., formulating trajectory optimisation as a non-linear program subject to dynamics and model constraints over a continuous control space, and solving it in a potentially decentralised manner [16], [21]. In contrast, this work follows a planning approach based on mo-tion primitives, which restricts the infinitely many possible controls to a finite set, thus facilitating rapid discovery of coordinated actions. Motion primitives have been explored for multi-robot navigation [5], [22], [23], demonstrating their potential for computationally efficient coordination, but they typically rely on manually designed mechanisms for selecting the right primitive. Our primary objective is to automate this selection process through learning. Ac-cordingly, we distinguish GAMBIT from learning-based multi-agent control approaches that directly output continuous controls (e.g., [24], [25]); instead, we formulate the learning problem as one of ranking the available primitives, making the learned heuristic naturally compatible with numerous global search-based planning methods. Meanwhile, we also provide a safeguarded rollout scheme with motion primi-tives tailored to double integrator dynamics, demonstrating how the learned heuristic can be integrated into a practical deployment framework. We define a motion primitive as a finite sequence of control inputs m = [ u0, u1, . . . , ul−1]. When applied to an initial robot state x0, a motion primitive induces a rollout state trajectory [x0, x1, . . . , xl]. In this study, for simplicity, we set l = 1 ; i.e., each primitive represents a single acceleration command a ∈ Rd, although the proposed framework can be extended to larger values of l. We denote the set of available motion primitives by M . Our objective is then to learn a 

ranking function that ranks the primitives m ∈ M for robot i

given the current configuration Q; we interchangeably refer to this function as a heuristic , as it virtually assesses the quality of each motion primitive. While learning for motion-primitive-based planning has been studied in single-robot settings [26], [27], our primary interest lies in multi-robot systems, which present challenges that stem from the interplay between agents. 

C. Neural MAPF Policies 

Learning heuristics for multi-agent action selection has been studied extensively in the context of grid-world MAPF [9], under highly simplified discrete dynamics. Typi-cally, this line of work aims to design a policy that receives a local observation oi for agent i and outputs a distribution over five discrete actions: NORTH , WEST , SOUTH , EAST , and STAY .To obtain effective policies, established learning pipelines use either imitation learning (IL) or reinforcement learning (RL) [14], [28]. We aim to leverage these insights beyond grid-world dynamics; however, several challenges hinder their direct adaptation to our setting, as highlighted in Sec. I; that is, data scarcity , multi-modality , safeguarding gap ,and scenario generation . Below, we provide our GAMBIT 

approach to address these. III. GAMBIT PIPELINE 

The GAMBIT framework centres around a GNN-based policy, together with its training pipeline, comprising IL pretraining and RL fine-tuning, and a safety-preserving roll-out scheme. This section introduces the backbone policy architecture and then describes each component in turn. 

A. Policy Architecture 

While our pipeline is not restricted to a specific model architecture, we follow a successful paradigm adopted by GNN-based neural MAPF policies [8], [14]. The geometric locality of GNNs allows us to train the policy on instances with only a small number of robots while effectively captur-ing interactions between robots, and to deploy the learned policy to substantially larger teams at test time. Specifically, the policy π consists of (i) an encoder ϕenc , (ii) graph neu-ral network layers ϕcomm that encode interactions between agents, and (iii) an MLP decoder ϕdec that outputs an action distribution over the set of motion primitives M .

1) Observation Encoding: An observation for robot i,denoted by oi, is the concatenation of (a) its velocity, (b) the relative goal vector, (c) a field-of-view (FOV) representation of its surroundings fi, and (d) a motion-primitive embed-ding ξi. The FOV fi is represented as a three-channel d-dimensional grid with Nobs cells along each dimension. The first two channels encode obstacle and agent occupancy, respectively, while the third encodes the goal location. When the goal lies outside the FOV, its location is clipped to the FOV boundary, following prior learning-based MAPF approaches [14], [28]. The motion-primitive embedding ξi

encodes the outcomes of the available primitives. For each 

m ∈ M , we compute the relative displacement after execut-ing m followed by stopping, together with the corresponding relative goal vector. Their concatenation is processed by an MLP and averaged over all primitives to obtain ξi. Finally, the complete observation is encoded as z0

B. Pretraining by Imitation Learning 

Pretraining by IL followed by RL fine-tuning has emerged as a successful strategy for learning capable policies across domains, ranging from language modelling [30] to robot learning [31]; we follow this strategy. For expert data collec-tion, we employ an adapted version of db-LaCAM [5], an of-fline centralised planner, in which its low-level configuration generation is replaced with our enhanced PIBT procedure described in Sec. III-D, enabling long-horizon kinodynamic multi-robot motion planning as defined in Sec. II-A. We generate demonstration trajectories by running the planner on instances involving a few agents. We limit the team size because collecting demonstrations for large teams is computationally expensive even with leading planners in this domain, making the construction of a large corpus pro-hibitively costly. Thanks to the locality of GNNs, we instead generate small instances that require tight local coordination, allowing the learned interactions to generalise to larger teams to overcome data scarcity .

Scenario generation is therefore a key component for successful learning. In particular, we prepare two families of instances, (i) Blocked Random and (ii) Forest , visualised in Fig. 2. Given the number of robots, the former first places start–goal pairs at random positions and then creates a small wall between these positions by placing obstacles perpendicularly to the line segments connecting each start– goal pair, with some random perturbations. Unlike purely random instance generation, this structure is more likely to require coordination. The latter contains regularly placed obstacles that form numerous narrow passages, while start– goal pairs are sampled randomly, exposing the policy to obstacle avoidance and potential deadlocks with other robots. Our IL formulation treats motion primitive selection as a classification problem, rather than directly regressing con-tinuous actions. Accordingly, for each continuous expert action u, we construct its discrete target as the closest motion primitive m∗ ∈ M and train the policy to predict m∗ using cross-entropy loss. This formulation offers two advantages over directly regressing continuous actions followed by quan-tisation: (i) more stable learning, as we observed empirically, and (ii) a categorical distribution over M , which can be directly exploited by our safeguarded rollout scheme. 

C. Fine-tuning by Reinforcement Learning 

While IL provides the basic skills required for multi-robot navigation, IL alone does not necessarily yield askilful policy. In addition to the solution multi-modality ,this limitation is amplified by the fact that computing high-quality trajectories for motion planning is itself computation-ally challenging [32], and we therefore rely on suboptimal solutions obtained by expert planners within a practical time budget. Consequently, the demonstration dataset can contain suboptimal behaviours, which are inherited by the policy through imitation. We address this limitation through RL fine-tuning, allowing the policy to directly optimise solution quality. Empirically, we find that training with RL from scratch is unstable, whereas the two-stage IL–RL procedure yields stable training and a capable policy. For this RL phase, we use the same instance distribution as in the IL phase and employ MAPPO [33] with a simple reward directly focused on optimising the sum-of-costs and an additional reward for all robots reaching their goals. D. Safeguarded Rollout with Backup Trajectories

for robot i over the set of motion primitives M , which by itself does not determine the action to execute. PIBT has been extended to general motion primitives [5]; while this variant guarantees collision-free outcomes, it does not guarantee that a feasible action assignment can always be found, which is particularly critical in dense scenarios. Our key observation is that, under double-integrator dynamics, the backup trajectories introduced above can play a role analogous to the ‘stay’ action, thereby providing both safety, i.e., collision-free execution, and feasibility, i.e., a feasible action assignment can always be found in finite time. Algorithm 1 provides a concrete procedure for this safe-guarded rollout, which takes as input the current state xi and the first action bi of the backup trajectory for each robot i. Its overall structure follows the original PIBT algorithm, where a recursive subprocedure PIBT (Line 2–) attempts to assign an action greedily and is invoked sequentially for each agent (Line 1). The preference over actions is determined at Line 3 and can in principle be defined arbitrarily; in this study, we stochastically sample the order of motion primitives, based on the probabilities assigned by the policy π. The subpro-cedure returns valid if robot i successfully secures a non-colliding action; otherwise, it returns invalid , forcing the interacting agent to replan. The function collide stop (i, m )

determines whether robot i, by executing primitive m from its current state xi, would collide with robots whose actions have already been assigned. Our key modification to the original PIBT is that this collision check extends beyond the immediate execution of m to the corresponding stopping trajectories; this ensures that robots can remain collision-free in the future, provided that they execute their corresponding backup trajectories. Theorem 1: In finite time, Alg. 1 finds a safe action assignment together with collision-free subsequent stopping trajectories, provided that the input backup trajectories are jointly collision-free. 

Proof: (sketch) The collision-free property follows from the analysis of the original PIBT and its motion-primitive-based variant [5], [11]. In short, the subprocedure PIBT is invoked only for a robot whose action has not yet been assigned, and each robot finalises its action at most once. A robot i finalises its action mi only if the corresponding stopping trajectory does not collide with those of robots whose actions have already been assigned. We guarantee that unassigned robots have a safe action available by en-suring that robot i’s actions do not conflict with the backup trajectories of these unassigned robots; otherwise, PIBT is recursively invoked for the interacting robot j to resolve the conflict before the action of i is finalised. If no safe primitive can be assigned through this recursion, the involved robots fall back to their input backup trajectories, which are collision-free by assumption. Hence, the recursion terminates after finitely many assignments while preserving collision-free stopping trajectories for all robots. 

Theorem 2: Consider a multi-robot execution in which Alg. 1 is invoked every ∆t, the resulting actions are executed, and the corresponding stopping trajectories are retained as backup trajectories for the next invocation. Then, the result-ing multi-robot trajectories remain collision-free for all time. 

Proof: By the previous theorem, each PIBT invocation preserves collision-freeness and produces collision-free stop-ping trajectories, which are used as the backup trajectories for the next invocation. The claim therefore follows by induction over timesteps. 

3) Auxiliary Reward for RL: With the safeguarded rollout, an ordered list of motion primitives is sampled from the policy output π(oi), after which the selected actions may be overridden by PIBT collision shielding. While safety is en-sured, such interventions can weaken the learning signal for the policy itself to synthesise coordinated actions. Therefore, we augment the reward defined in Eq. (2) with an auxiliary term based on whether the policy’s actions are consistent with the PIBT-processed actions, penalising the overriding of the actions by the PIBT collision shielding.

B. Main Results 

Figure 2 summarises our main results. GAMBIT achieves high success rates across all tested scenarios, including both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings, at scale while maintaining highly competitive solution quality, deliv-ering the strongest overall performance among the evaluated methods. The computation time remains moderate; even with 32 robots, GAMBIT requires only about 1 s to derive feasible trajectories, suggesting real-time planning ability, which we investigate further in Sec. IV-C and IV-D. A closer look reveals that learning is a key component (see 

GAMBIT \IL \RL ), with both IL and RL providing substan-tial improvements in success rate. The performance of \PIBT 

further demonstrates that the safeguarded rollout is critical to achieving strong performance; i.e., direct execution of the learned policy is not performant. We conjecture that this is because multi-robot navigation policies generally need to learn multiple objectives jointly, including goal reaching and collision avoidance, whereas the safeguarded rollout largely relieves the policy of the latter, simplifying the learning problem and allowing the policy to focus its learning capacity on goal-directed coordination. Among the baselines, the domain-independent planners 

db-LaCAM and D4ORM struggle to scale, while MMD-XECBS ,which uses learning to adapt to the target domain, achieves superior planning performance but at a high computational cost. Decentralised local collision-avoidance planners such as 

RVO and EGO-Swarm struggle with interaction-heavy, dense instances where many robots are packed into a limited workspace, as these scenarios require coordination beyond mere collision avoidance. While remaining reactive at execu-tion time, GAMBIT incorporates such long-horizon reasoning through offline learning. While GAMBIT is capable of dense coordination, some failures remain. Our qualitative observation suggests that these failures are primarily caused by livelocks, where one or more robots repeatedly execute the same actions without making progress. Livelocks occur primarily near obstacles, e.g., when a robot must pass through a narrow gap between obstacles but safety margins prevent the motion, or when it must traverse around an obstacle to reach a goal on the opposite side. We envision two promising directions for overcoming this limitation: stronger policies through improved architectures and training pipelines, and integrat-ing the learned motion-primitive heuristic into long-horizon Fig. 4. C. Scalability Assessment 

Using the Forest scenario with an enlarged workspace, we evaluate the scalability of GAMBIT on instances with up to 

1,024 robots. At this scale, none of the baseline methods suc-cessfully solve the instances, and thus we evaluate GAMBIT 

and its non-learning variant (i.e., \IL \RL ). Figure 3 demon-strates that GAMBIT provides a highly scalable planning framework, maintaining high individual success rates even in densely packed settings. This scale is significantly beyond the reach of existing methods for computing full-horizon contin-uous multi-robot trajectories. Moreover, GAMBIT retains real-time responsiveness at scale: with 512 robots, it synthesises safe coordinated actions in < 100 ms /step , enabling real-time control at scale. While the non-learning, greedy variant still allows a portion of the robots to reach their goals (see the ISR evolution plot), some robots remain unsuccessful due to its coordination-unaware heuristic, emphasising the importance of learning coordination-aware heuristics. 

D. Multirotor Deployment 

We finally deploy GAMBIT on physical multirotors [19]. Figure 4 shows overlaid trajectories from one demonstration in which eight multirotors swap their start–goal locations in a motion-capture arena containing obstacles. We run GAMBIT 

at 10 Hz to synthesise motion online, yielding collision-free execution under closed-loop control even at a peak speed of 

2.2 m /s in the instance shown. V. C ONCLUSION 

We presented GAMBIT , a framework for learning motion-primitive heuristics for dense multi-robot coordination. While the resulting policy combined with our safeguarded rollout already achieves strong planning performance and exceptional scalability, we view GAMBIT more broadly as a powerful toolkit for building more capable multi-robot plan-ners. Indeed, learned components have increasingly demon-strated their potential to enhance multi-agent planning [8], [9], [18].
