# DNPush: Distributed Nonparallel Pushing Force Control of Multi-Robot Systems for Object Transportation 

Duy Anh Nguyen 1, Pham Duy Hung 2, and Trung Dung Ngo 1∗

Abstract — This letter proposes DNPush, a Distributed Non-parallel Pushing force controller for cooperative object trans-port without inter-robot communication. Each robot uses local force measurements, a preassigned force weight, and a locally expressed desired transport direction, without requiring an object model, contact locations, a CoM estimate, or moment arm magnitudes online. DNPush regulates nonparallel force magnitudes from local force direction misalignments through a reciprocal-sine law. Under the stated contact conditions, Lyapunov analysis with LaSalle’s invariance principle proves convergence to a unique rotational equilibrium, where per-pendicular force contributions cancel and the net torque from normal forces is zero. Object velocity converges to the desired transport direction, and suitable contact regions exist for convex polygons. Simulations isolate the reciprocal-sine scaling mech-anism, five-robot coordination, and active rotational damping. Hardware experiments with soft tactile differential-drive robots achieve a velocity direction error of 0.61 ◦ ± 0.79 ◦ over five trials with a fixed transport direction and demonstrate object transport through waypoints with three robots. 

Index Terms – Cooperative object transport, dis-tributed control, multi-robot systems, tactile force control 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Cooperative pushing mobile robots move objects through direct body contact, unlike grasping , which relies on me-chanical attachment, or caging , which maintains geometric enclosure [1]. Each robot pushes the object at a contact location, which determines the direction of the applied force and its rotational effect about the object CoM [2], [3]. For a given set of contact locations, the pushing forces must be coordinated to achieve the desired object motion. A centralized controller can allocate the force magnitudes using global information about the object geometry and pushing directions. However, global information may be limited, and reliable inter-robot communication cannot always be assumed [4]. Distributed control reduces this dependence by allowing each robot to regulate its own pushing force using local information. The challenge is to design local control laws so that the resulting pushing forces collectively produce the desired object motion [1], [4]. II. R ELATED WORK 

Cooperative object transport using multi-robot systems is commonly grouped by transport strategies, categorized as caging, grasping, and pushing [1]. Caging methods surround the object and maintain geometric containment during transport [6], [7]. Caging requires the robots to main-tain a coordinated enclosing configuration around the object throughout transport. Grasping methods physically attach the robots to the object, allowing them to transmit forces and torques in multiple directions. Several distributed controllers exploit these fixed connections to regulate shared object motion under limited inter-robot communication or uncertain object parameters [8], [9]. This bidirectional interaction supports coordinated translation and rotation, but requires secure mechanical attachments and suitable grasp points. Using pushing forces for object transport avoids the above-mentioned requirements, but each robot is restricted to uni-lateral contact forces. Classical manipulation research estab-lished the mechanics and controllability of planar pushing [2], [3]. Earlier behavior- and occlusion-based methods used simple local rules to coordinate cooperative pushing without explicit communication [10], but did not directly regulate the magnitudes of multiple nonparallel contact forces. Recent cooperative pushing studies have increasingly considered contact configuration and pushing planning. Contact loca-tions have been optimized to improve the achievable object wrench under frictional constraints [11]. In [12], hybrid optimization methods have also been developed to generate and switch among their associated pushing forces for multi-robot transport in complex environments. Force- and tactile feedback methods have been developed for robust pushing under uncertain object properties, and contact changes [13], [14] while tactile SLAM methods estimate object shape and pose online during planar pushing [15]. Most of these works address contact selection, pushing mode planning, globally coordinated force allocation, or reactive manipulation under uncertain interactions. DNPush instead considers a fixed nonparallel contact configuration and addresses how several robots coordinate their pushing force magnitudes using only local tactile feedback and no inter-robot communication. The most related work is the previous study of Distributed Cascade Force Control (DCFC) for soft tactile multi-robot object transport [5]. DCFC used local tactile force regulation and established rotational convergence without inter-robot communication. However, it required parallel pushing forces applied at contacts on a common straight object edge. This requirement restricts its application to objects with a sufficiently long straight boundary segment that accommo-dates multiple robots. DNPush overcomes this limitation by allowing nonparallel boundary contacts of pushing forces on the transported object and changing the role of force direction misalignment. Instead of reducing the misalignment to zero, DNPush uses it to set the local force magnitude, balancing the force components perpendicular to the desired direction, and creating a restoring torque under the ordered opposite moment condition.

C. Discussion and Limitations 

The analysis assumes fixed contacts, ideal force tracking, no upper force saturation, and ε = 0 . Rotational convergence further assumes zero tangential contact torque, while velocity convergence assumes zero tangential contact force resultant. The fixed-direction hardware trials directly evaluate the nom-inal transport objective, whereas the three-robot waypoint trial uses piecewise changes in the desired direction and is not covered by the fixed-direction convergence analysis. In hardware, finite force limits, tangential interactions, sensing noise, and tracking errors can produce residual errors. Fixed contact is not formally guaranteed, and future work will relax the fixed-contact assumption through online contact planning and contact-mode switching.

Fig. 7: Fixed-direction hardware repeatability over five trials. VII. C ONCLUSION 

This letter presented DNPush, a distributed pushing force controller for object transport without inter-robot communi-cation. We showed that local force direction misalignment need not be eliminated in distributed pushing. Under an ordered opposite moment contact condition, the direction misalignment can instead be used as a local signal to balance the force components perpendicular to the desired direction and stabilize object rotation. Under fixed contacts and the nominal contact model, the analysis showed that object rotation asymptotically converges to a unique equilibrium, the resultant normal force aligns with the desired direction, and the object’s velocity converges to the desired transport direction. Simulations isolated the force scaling mechanism, demonstrated five-robot coordination, and confirmed the effect of active rotational damping. Hardware experiments with soft tactile differential-drive robots achieve a velocity direction error of 0.61 ◦ ± 0.79 ◦ over five trials with a fixed object transport direction and further demonstrate three-robot object transport through predefined waypoints.
