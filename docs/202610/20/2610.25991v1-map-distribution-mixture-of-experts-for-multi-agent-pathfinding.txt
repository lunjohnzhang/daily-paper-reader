ABSTRACT 

Designing capable neural policies for multi-agent pathfinding (MAPF), which map local observations to action distributions, represents an important challenge in multi-agent learning, with practical relevance to scalable collective automation problems such as warehouse logistics. In contrast to prior studies focusing primarily on individual policy design, this study explores a meta-level Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) approach to learning-based MAPF. Specifically, given a collection of compact yet high-performing experts specialised for different map distributions, our architecture learns a router that selectively fuses their outputs according to each agent’s local situation. We instantiate and validate this MoE approach with a commonly used graph neural network-based policy as well as a newly developed recursive action-token refinement model. Combined with supervised and reinforcement learning fine-tuning of the experts, our experiments show that MoE generally improves upon the pretrained policy and outperforms existing leading methods. At the same time, our findings reveal that appropriately trained, parameter-matched monolithic models still outperform MoE, highlighting a nuanced role for map-distribution MoE for MAPF. ## 1 Introduction 

Multi-agent pathfinding (MAPF) has emerged as a challenging testbed for large-scale multi-agent learning, requiring dense coordination among agents, while also being closely connected to real-world applications such as logistics automation. Among the various paradigms for integrating learning into planning, one central topic is the development of capable neural MAPF policies , in which each agent selects actions based on local observations and interactions [1]. In fact, various policy architectures have been developed, together with efficient training pipelines based primarily on reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL). Recent studies have further shown that integrating neural MAPF policies with search can outperform purely search-based MAPF solvers in certain problem regimes [2, 3]. Existing architectures, however, largely rely on monolithic policies, where a single policy is expected to handle all situations encountered during execution. In contrast, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures [4] have proven effective across other domains. At its core, MoE combines multiple specialised experts through a learned mechanism that selectively uses their outputs according to the given input. A notable example is the development of large language models (LLMs), where modern architectures increasingly adopt sparse MoE to efficiently leverage specialised experts [5, 6]. Related ideas of combining specialised skills have also been explored in robotics, enabling increasingly versatile and complex behaviours [7, 8]. This naturally raises the question: can we exploit the power of MoE for neural MAPF policies, and if so, how? This work addresses this question through a systematic investigation. More precisely, two key considerations arise: (i) how to prepare the experts and (ii) how to combine them. For the former, inspired by the observation in [3] that fine-tuning a neural MAPF policy for a specific map can substantially enhance its performance, we prepare a collection of experts specialised for different map distributions. ## 2 Preliminaries 

2.1 Problem Formulation 

We consider a commonly used MAPF formulation [13], defined by a set of agents A = {1, 2, . . . , n } operating on a four-connected grid G = ( V, E ), where each agent i ∈ A has distinct start and goal vertices, denoted by si, g i ∈ V . At each timestep, each agent either stays at its current vertex or moves to an adjacent vertex. Vertex and edge collisions are prohibited; that is, two agents cannot occupy the same vertex at the same timestep or traverse the same edge in opposite directions within one timestep. Equivalently, using a configuration Q ∈ V n that represents the locations of all agents, MAPF seeks a sequence of transitionable collision-free configurations that moves every agent from its start to its goal. The solution cost is measured by the sum-of-costs (SoC), defined as the sum of the travel times of all agents until they reach their goals and remain there. 2.2 Neural MAPF Policies 

This study regards MAPF as a collective sequential decision-making problem, where each agent i receives a local observation oi from the current configuration Q and selects its action according to the action distribution 

π(oi) over five possible actions, including staying in place. Our objective is then to learn a policy π that solves MAPF while achieving low SoC. Such a policy is typically trained via either RL or IL from expert search-based MAPF planners. Many policy architectures have been developed, with representative examples including PRIMAL [14], MAPF-GPT [11, 15], and HMAGAT [12]; see [1] for a comprehensive review. Beyond improving individual policy architectures, this study explores an orthogonal, architecture-agnostic direction: how to effectively construct experts for target domains, and fuse them through MoE. 

2.2.1 Observation 

While the proposed MoE framework itself does not assume a specific form of observation, following common practice in the literature [3, 10, 14], we use an observation tensor oi centred around agent i’s current position 

Q[i] ∈ V , with shape 4 × (2 Robs + 1) 2, where Robs ∈ N>0 determines the field-of-view (FOV) size. The four channels represent: (i) the presence of obstacles, (ii) that of other agents, (iii) a projection of the goal direction, all represented by binary values, and (iv) cost-to-go values, where the value is normalised as 

(dist (v, g i) − dist (Q[i], g i)) /(2 Robs ) for each location v ∈ V within the FOV. 

2.2.2 GNN-based Policy and Communication 

To validate the efficacy of MoE, this study conducts expert fusion over two distinct neural MAPF policy architectures. One is a newly developed architecture designed to achieve high performance with a compact policy, described in detail in Sec. 4, while the other follows a standard GNN-based policy, MAGAT [3, 10], as described below. 1

Given a configuration Q, MAGAT assumes a communication graph Gcomm , where each vertex corresponds to an agent and an edge (i, j ) exists if agents i and j are within a communication radius Rcomm ∈ N+ of each other. MAGAT then consists of (i) a CNN-based encoder that maps the observation oi to an initial latent embedding z0 

> i

, (ii) L communication layers that perform GNN message passing over Gcomm , where each layer l produces zli from the embeddings of the previous layer, and (iii) a multilayer perceptron (MLP) decoder that maps zLi to an action distribution. The communication layers use a three-dimensional edge feature ωij , consisting of the relative position in the x- and y-directions and the Manhattan distance between the corresponding agents. 

2.2.3 Collision Shielding 

A neural MAPF policy alone outputs an action distribution, leaving a design choice in how to decode it into collision-free actions. Earlier work stochastically samples actions from the distribution and then resolves inter-agent collisions by simply stopping one of the conflicting agents, an approach known as naive 

collision shielding (CS) [17, 18]. Meanwhile, recent studies have shown that leveraging PIBT [19] to sample collision-free actions substantially improves the learned policy, an approach called CS-PIBT [2, 3, 12, 20, 21]; we mainly follow this strategy. PIBT is a popular MAPF algorithm underlying modern scalable MAPF planners in both learning-free and learning-based settings [2, 12, 22]. It receives a configuration and an ordered action list for each agent, called a preference , and generates a collision-free next configuration while respecting the agent-wise preferences.

## 3 MoE over Map-Specific Experts 

A key consideration in constructing an MoE is how to prepare a diverse and complementary set of expert policies. Two general strategies are commonly used: (i) training the experts first and subsequently learning a routing function to combine them, and (ii) jointly training the experts and router. We adopt the first strategy, motivated by prior work [3] showing that supervised fine-tuning of a pretrained policy on one target map can substantially improve its performance on that map. Following this insight, we first train a base policy and then independently fine-tune copies of it into a collection of experts, each specialised for a particular map distribution , before learning the router that combines their outputs. Such a training recipe has been used in MoE-based LLMs [23]. Based on common benchmark categorisations [18, 24], this study considers four distinct map distributions: 

Maze , Warehouse , Room , and Random . We select their instance configurations while accounting for difficulty, such that they provide useful training signals for high-performing policies. These map distributions are currently selected manually, leaving their automatic selection to future work. 

3.1 Expert Training 

Starting from a pretrained policy, our reproducible fine-tuning pipeline for each target map distribution, applicable across different neural architectures, consists of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) followed by RL-based fine-tuning. 

3.1.1 Pretraining and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) 

We pretrain a policy to imitate teacher trajectories generated by an expert MAPF solver. All agents share the policy parameters θ, and training minimises the cross-entropy between the predicted action distributions and the teacher actions, as commonly adopted in IL-based MAPF policies. The resulting parameters θ0 provide a common initialisation for adaptation to the target map distributions. Next, for each target map distribution k, we collect additional teacher trajectories by solving instances sampled from that distribution by the expert MAPF solver. We initialise the corresponding expert with θk = θ0

and fine-tune it on these trajectories using the same cross-entropy objective. 

3.1.2 Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning 

While SFT alone provides decent performance, we empirically observe that RL fine-tuning further improves each SFT expert πk using rewards from its own rollouts on the target map distribution. Our approach consists of two key components: PIBT-based safeguarded rollouts and GRPO-based [9] policy updates. 

Rollout Procedure For each instance, we collect B rollouts from the same initial configuration, ending each rollout when all agents reach their goals or the timestep limit Tmax is reached. At each timestep, CS-PIBT [20] converts the policy’s output distributions into a collision-free joint move. For each agent, it samples an ordering of legal actions without replacement according to the policy probabilities, which serves as the preference ordering for PIBT. PIBT then sequentially attempts to assign compatible moves according to these preferences, trying subsequent candidates when necessary. For the policy update, we record the prefix of each ordering up to the last action examined; i.e., ut,i = [ v(1) , v (2) , . . . , v (m)] denotes the sequence of candidate vertices attempted for agent i at timestep t during PIBT. ## 4 Lightweight yet Capable Architecture: ReCAT 

Our MoE design is highly flexible, allowing, for example, experts with different architectures to be combined within a single policy. However, this flexibility comes at the cost of increased inference overhead. Unlike typical MoE architectures used in LLMs, where only a subset of experts is activated for each token, our design usually requires all experts to be evaluated for every agent, as each expert may employ its own interaction modelling among agents. Consequently, the inference cost grows with the number and complexity of experts, making compact yet capable expert architectures particularly desirable. This motivates us to develop a new architecture that remains compact yet highly performant. To this end, this section introduces Recursive Collective Action Token Network (ReCAT) , illustrated in Fig. 2, which integrates prominent architectural components that have proven effective across different domains. As indicated by its name, ReCAT represents each candidate action as a token. A spatial encoder first encodes the local observation into each action token, after which a recurrent GNN iteratively refines these tokens by exchanging messages both within and between agents. Finally, a linear projection converts the refined tokens into action logits.

4.3 Recurrent Token Update 

We next recursively refine the action tokens while capturing interactions among agents. Inspired by looped Transformers [33, 34] and recurrent GNNs [35], we repeatedly apply a shared update module, enabling multiple rounds of refinement without increasing the number of parameters. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the refinement is performed through GNN message passing. For this purpose, we expand the communication graph Gcomm used in MAGAT into an action-level graph Gact = ( Vact , E act ),whose nodes correspond to the five action tokens of each agent, i.e., |Vact | = 5 n. Within each agent, all five action tokens are mutually connected, including self-edges, with zero edge features, whereas across agents, tokens corresponding to the same action are connected according to Gcomm with edge features ωji , as in MAGAT. Thus, |Eact | = 25 n + 5 |Ecomm |. GATv2 [16] then performs message passing over Gact , and a gated recurrent unit (GRU) [36] updates each token using the aggregated message and its hidden state from the previous round. 5.1 Experimental Setup 

5.1.1 Dataset 

Following prior GNN-based policy training studies [3, 12], the pretraining dataset contains 21 ,000 instances with 20% Random and 80% Maze instances, where widths and heights are between 17 and 21 cells and contain 16, 32, or 48 agents. We generate teacher trajectories with LaCAM3 [22]. The supervised fine-tuning datasets contain approximately 3,000 instances per target distribution, with 48, 64, or 80 agents. For router training, we combine the four SFT datasets, resulting in approximately 3,000 × 4 instances in total. To exclude low-quality demonstrations, we retain solutions whose makespan, normalised by max i∈A dist (si, g i), is at most 1.4 for each target distribution. For all training stages, we split the instances into training and validation sets in an 8:2 ratio. As teacher trajectories contain many wait actions from agents that have already reached their goals, which may weaken the learning signal, we follow [15] and mask 80% of these action labels in the cross-entropy loss while retaining all other action labels. The corresponding agents remain in the communication graph. This masking excludes approximately 38% of all action labels in the initial pretraining dataset. 8Map-Distribution Mixture-of-Experts for Multi-Agent Pathfinding F. Suzuki and K. Okumura 

5.1.2 Training Procedure 

We use AdamW [37] for pretraining, SFT, and router training, with weight decay of 0.05 and cosine annealing of the learning rate to 10 −6. For pretraining and SFT, we adjust the batch size according to the fraction of retained labels to maintain an effective batch size of approximately 48. 

Pretraining Pretraining runs for 200 epochs with an initial learning rate of 10 −3. Following [12], we perform on-demand data aggregation every four epochs by running the current policy with naive collision shielding on 500 instances. For each failed rollout, we invoke LaCAM3 from its final configuration and add the resulting teacher trajectory to the training dataset. Furthermore, inspired by [11], once the rollout success rate reaches 80%, we additionally revisit successful rollouts whose makespan is at least 1.2 times that of the teacher trajectory. For up to 30 such instances per aggregation round, we sample configurations at 16-timestep intervals and invoke LaCAM3 from each sampled configuration. We add the resulting teacher trajectory if its makespan is at most 1/1.2 of the remaining policy rollout. Pretraining takes approximately 14 hours and 21 hours for the base MAGAT and ReCAT models, respectively, including validation and on-demand data aggregation. 

SFT It runs for 52 epochs with an initial learning rate of 10 −5, using only the pre-collected trajectories for the target distribution without on-demand data aggregation, taking a few hours per expert. 

RL Fine-Tuning Each supervised expert undergoes 100 epochs of RL on its target map distribution. In each epoch, we sample 25 instances with 48, 64, or 80 agents and collect B=4 rollouts per instance, each capped at Tmax =128 timesteps. We then perform three optimisation passes over minibatches of the collected rollouts using Adam [38] with a learning rate of 10 −6, a clipping threshold ϵ = 0 .2, reference-policy KL coefficient β = 0 .01 , entropy coefficient α = 0 .001 , and the gradient norm clipped at 0.5.

Router Training With the expert weights frozen, we train the router for 30 epochs with an initial learning rate of 10 −3 and a fixed routing temperature of τ = 1 . Router training takes approximately 2.5 hours for MAGAT-MoE and 5.5 hours for ReCAT-MoE. 

5.1.3 Model Configurations 

We use the same observation representation and communication graph for MAGAT and ReCAT, with an observation radius of Robs = 5 and a communication radius of Rcomm = 5 . Our base MAGAT has 485K parameters and consists of three stacked GNN layers. Our base ReCAT has 121K parameters, with Benc = 4 

residual spatial encoder blocks with C = 64 channels, action tokens of dimension d = 96 , and L = 4 

communication rounds. The MLP router has 190K parameters. 

5.1.4 Baselines 

We compare MAGAT-MoE (2.1M parameters) and ReCAT-MoE (676K), with routers trained by supervised learning while the experts remain frozen, against their respective pretrained policies and distribution-specific experts obtained through SFT and RL fine-tuning, to assess how well the mixture preserves specialist performance. We also evaluate Uniform , which combines the same four frozen SFT+RL experts by averaging their action logits before applying softmax to rate the learned routing efficacy. For comparison with existing methods, we consider two leading neural MAPF policies. HMAGAT 

(1M) [12] is an IL policy that models agent interactions using hypergraph neural layers and employs RL to adapt each agent’s action-sampling temperature. MAPF-GPT-DDG (2M) [11] is a compact variant of 9Map-Distribution Mixture-of-Experts for Multi-Agent Pathfinding F. Suzuki and K. Okumura 

the Transformer-based MAPF-GPT policy [15] that achieves comparable performance with a smaller model through targeted expert data augmentation. For these, we use the authors’ official code repositories. We also prepare parameter-matched monolithic versions of MAGAT (2.1M) and ReCAT (782K) by increasing their latent dimensions accordingly. We first pretrain these models on the same instances as the smaller pretrained base models, taking approximately 15 hours for MAGAT and 30 hours for ReCAT. For a fair comparison, as the MoE policies are exposed to instances with larger numbers of agents during training, these monolithic models subsequently undergo SFT on the concatenation of the four SFT datasets, using the same settings as expert training. RL fine-tuning follows the expert training procedure, with the four training distributions sampled with equal probability. SFT takes approximately 5 hours for MAGAT and 10 hours for ReCAT, while RL fine-tuning takes approximately 8 hours per policy. All main evaluations use CS-PIBT, given its substantial performance improvement over naive collision shielding. 

5.1.5 Metrics and Instances 

We report the success rate (all agents reaching their goals) and mean relative SoC with respect to LaCAM3 solutions obtained with a 60 s time budget. For unsuccessful instances, agents that have not reached their goals are assigned the timestep limit as their individual costs when computing SoC. For in-distribution evaluation ( Maze , Warehouse , Room , and Random ), there is no overlap between training and test instances. For out-of-distribution evaluation, we use a fixed map layout for each of Sortation , derived from [39], and 

Stripes , from [3]. We also generate obstacle-free Empty maps with widths and heights between 17 and 21 cells; see Fig. 3. For each agent count, we use the same 100 fixed instances, each with a limit of 128 timesteps. 

5.2 Main Results 

Figure 3 summarises success rates and mean relative SoC, while Table 1 reports aggregated scores over six map distributions with and without CS-PIBT. Several key observations highlight the benefits of MoE across both underlying architectures. 

Map-distribution Experts The success-rate heatmaps show that our fine-tuning strategy substantially improves policy performance when the training and test distributions match, but these gains do not transfer uniformly to other distributions. For example, for both architectures, the Warehouse expert achieves a higher success rate than the pretrained policy on Warehouse , but lower success rates on Maze and Random . This indicates that the resulting fine-tuned policies indeed become specialists for specific map distributions. 

Efficacy of MoE Unlike individual experts, whose success rates can drop substantially on certain distributions, 

MoE policies retain the experts’ strong in-distribution performance while generally improving upon the pretrained models across distributions. MoE generally outperforms Uniform too, indicating that the learned router effectively exploits complementary strengths across experts rather than merely averaging their outputs. Furthermore, despite using fewer parameters, ReCAT-MoE outperforms the leading HMAGAT and MAPF-GPT-DDG baselines across all distributions when considering success rate and solution cost jointly. MAGAT-MoE also achieves comparable aggregate performance to both baselines in terms of success rate and solution cost, further demonstrating the effectiveness of our training pipeline. Table 1 also shows that the benefits of the learned mixtures extend to the setting without CS-PIBT. Nevertheless, as seen for ReCAT on Stripes , the MoE policy does not always match the best-performing expert for each map distribution, leaving room for improvement in expert utilisation.

## 6 Discussion 

Across two distinct policy architectures, this study demonstrates that MoE over map-distribution-specialised experts provides a capable approach to neural MAPF. In particular, despite its compact construction, ReCAT-MoE achieves strong performance in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings. However, we also find that, under the same parameter budget, monolithic policies still provide stronger performance, suggesting that realising the benefits of MoE—a paradigm that has proven highly successful in recent LLMs—remains challenging in the MAPF context. We believe that alternative MoE architectures warrant further investigation. In the following, we position our study within the broader literature and discuss its limitations and future directions. 

MoE MoE [4] is a long-established concept in machine learning that combines multiple experts through a routing function. Broadly, MoE training can follow two approaches: learning a router over frozen experts or jointly learning the router and experts. While recent LLMs predominantly follow the latter approach [6, 40], our approach largely falls into the former, leveraging the fact that map-wise fine-tuning provides strong specialised experts for MAPF [3]. Meanwhile, the superior performance obtained by jointly fine-tuning the experts and router with RL suggests that expert specialisation may also emerge implicitly through joint learning. This points to a key limitation of our current approach, i.e., the need to explicitly construct experts for predefined map distributions, while suggesting that learning expert specialisation implicitly may open a different avenue for MoE in neural MAPF. 

Multiple Algorithms and Policies in MAPF We clarify that this is not the first study to utilise multiple algorithms or policies in MAPF. For example, algorithm selection [41, 42] aims to choose a suitable algorithm from a portfolio of MAPF algorithms for a given target instance; by applying the selected solver, this meta-scheme aims to find solutions efficiently. Tang et al. [43] adopt best-trajectory sampling over multiple policies configured with different hyperparameters. Other approaches switch between policies according to predefined rules [3, 19, 44], or combine collision-avoidance and goal-reaching policies by additively 13 Map-Distribution Mixture-of-Experts for Multi-Agent Pathfinding F. Suzuki and K. Okumura 

combining their outputs, similarly to potential-field methods [45, 46]. In contrast, our contribution lies in an explicit MoE formulation that uses a learned router to adaptively fuse expert outputs according to each agent’s local situation, together with an effective recipe for creating strong specialised experts. This adaptive fusion during rollout is critical, as demonstrated by the results in Fig. 3. 

ReCAT As described earlier, our newly designed compact neural MAPF policy, ReCAT, integrates several techniques that have proven effective in other domains. Meanwhile, concurrent preprints have also explored recursive refinement for interaction modelling in neural MAPF policies [47, 48], similarly showing promising results. 

Automatic Map Generation Our MoE analysis implicitly suggests that certain map distributions are more useful than others for training capable policies, motivating another interesting direction: automatically generating map distributions for training. Such automatic curriculum generation has proven effective in RL [49], while map generation itself is an active area of MAPF research [50]. Integrating these techniques into neural MAPF policy training represents an exciting direction for future work.

## Appendix A Map Distributions 

Figure 4 shows four layouts from each in-distribution map generator. Start–goal pairs are sampled within connected components. Maps that cannot accommodate the requested number of agents are resampled. Generator parameters are sampled uniformly within the ranges below. 

Maze We generate maze-like layouts by growing walls from randomly selected cells in two-cell steps, filling the intervening cell. Even map dimensions are rounded up to the next odd integer. Each wall seed receives 1–8 extension attempts, with a straight-continuation weight of 0.75 –0.85 to favour long corridors. The density parameter, sampled between 0.2 and 1.0, controls the number of wall-growth attempts. 

Warehouse We arrange rectangular shelves in a regular grid, with shelf widths of 2–8 cells and heights of 2–6 cells. Aisles span 1–2 cells between shelf columns and one cell between rows, with side padding of 1–3 cells. Map dimensions are determined from an initial arrangement of 1–4 shelf rows and columns. 

Room We generate a grid with 3–6 rows and columns of rooms, whose shared width and height are independently sampled between three and five cells. Adjacent rooms are separated by one-cell-thick walls, each with a randomly placed doorway. A one-cell-wide perimeter remains free in each room, while interior cells are independently blocked with a probability sampled between 0.8 and 1.0 per map. 

Random Obstacles are placed randomly, with an obstacle density sampled between 20% and 40%.
