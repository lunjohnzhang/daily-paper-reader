# ABSTRACT 

Developing coordination policies for complex multi-agent systems relies on either labor-intensive algorithm design or opaque task-specific reinforcement learning. With recent advances in autonomous coding agents, we show that policy search directly in code space offers a compelling third path that combines automated optimization with explicit interpretability. We investigate a human-directed work-flow in which coding agents iteratively propose, implement, evaluate, diagnose, and eventually refine algorithms guided by simulation feedback and explicit cor-rectness verification. We conducted two case studies and found that this workflow robustly outperformed any manually engineered solutions. On a challenging map in the 2024 League of Robot Runners competition, a two-day search discovered a policy that exceeds the stronger locally reproduced competition entry by 35.0% on the densest instance and generalizes to unseen map instances. In simulated real-world warehouses with irregular lanes, continuous robot geometry, and asyn-chronous motion, a week-long search discovered an entirely new class of multi-agent pathfinding algorithm that outperforms a calibrated proprietary solution in throughput by 58.9%. Under stated assumptions, we further prove that the new algorithm is collision-free and completes, in finite time, every accepted goal that is not withdrawn or replaced. Together, the studies show that policy search in code space powered by state-of-the-art coding agents offers a highly competitive path to building real engineering solutions that are both effective and analytically tractable. # 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multi-agent coordination underlies warehouse fulfillment, factory automation, and parcel sorting. In lifelong multi-agent pathfinding (LMAPF), tasks arrive continuously, requiring a fleet of robots to coordinate both task allocation and collision-free navigation through a shared spatial network (Wur-man et al., 2008; Ma et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021b). The operational objective is to maximize system throughput subject to collision avoidance and strict real-time compute budgets. Because uncoordi-nated routing decisions cause severe congestion and deadlocks, effective warehouse operation typi-

Existing methods generally fall into two paradigms. First, the standard approach relies on human experts to engineer sophisticated solvers, dispatch rules, and traffic guidance heuristics (Okumura et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Although effective, manual engineering is bot-tlenecked by human engineering throughput and cannot scale algorithm discovery with available compute. Second, learning-based methods attempt to bypass this manual effort by training neural policies directly through simulated interaction (Damani et al., 2021; Skrynnik et al., 2024; Andrey-chuk et al., 2025). However, neural policies often lack formal safety guarantees, remain difficult to inspect and verify, and resist modular modification when operational constraints shift. We study a third approach: policy search in code space (PSCS), driven by coding agents. In this paradigm, human designers specify the system objective, physical constraints, and scaffolding inter-faces, while coding agents iteratively refine the implementation of the planner and scheduler directly in source code, which we refer to as code space . Guided by simulation benchmarks, unit tests, and execution traces, the agent discovers procedural algorithms that are computationally efficient, inter-pretable, and maintainable. Unlike manual design, the search scales directly with compute; unlike black-box learning, the resulting policy remains fully transparent and verifiable. Automated code generation has demonstrated promise in discovering novel mathematical algorithms and discrete heuristics (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Novikov et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024). How-ever, existing studies focus primarily on idealized theoretical benchmarks or stylized combinatorial problems. It remains an open question whether PSCS can scale to demanding cyber-physical sys-tems such as LMAPF, where task allocation, spatial planning, and low-latency runtime engineering must interact robustly under realistic constraints. Specifically, we investigate whether coding agents can navigate these coupled requirements across both standardized benchmarks and complex indus-trial environments. We evaluate PSCS across two distinct domains. The first is the 2024 League of Robot Runners (LoRR) benchmark (Chan et al., 2024), a synchronous grid competition environment operating with 

100 to 800 robots. In this domain, PSCS systematically refines existing baselines to discover layout-specific routing heuristics, outperforming the stronger locally reproduced competition entry by up to 35.0% in mean throughput (Figure 1a). The second is a high-fidelity simulation of an operational warehouse from KNAPP characterized by irregular lanes, continuous vehicle geometry, and asyn-chronous execution. In a challenging production map, our synthesized algorithm outperforms an optimized proprietary baseline by 58.9% (Figure 1b) while preserving provable progress certificates against deadlock under stated assumptions. The contributions of this work are as follows: 1. We demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of policy search in code space (PSCS) for automated, nontrivial combinatorial algorithm design in LMAPF. 2. On the LoRR 2024 benchmark, we show that PSCS effectively refines established planners to discover map-specific heuristics, outperforming the stronger locally reproduced compe-tition entry by up to 35.0% in mean throughput. 3. In high-fidelity industrial warehouse environments, we show that PSCS discovers an en-tirely new class of algorithms that outperforms an optimized proprietary baseline by 58.9% while maintaining provable progress certification under stated assumptions. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

This work sits at the intersection of multi-agent pathfinding (MAPF) and automated program syn-thesis. Rather than hand-crafting domain heuristics or optimizing parametric weights, we search for coordination policies directly in code space. 

Multi-Agent Pathfinding. MAPF seeks collision-free trajectories for a team of agents (Stern et al., 2019). In lifelong MAPF (LMAPF), agents continuously receive new tasks upon completing previous assignments (Ma et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021b). Because optimal solvers such as conflict-based search (Sharon et al., 2015) do not scale to hundreds of agents, large-scale deployments rely 2on fast suboptimal planners that human experts design and tune for specific topologies. Represen-tative rule-based methods include PIBT (Okumura et al., 2022) for one-step priority inheritance, Causal-PIBT (Okumura et al., 2021) for asynchronous execution, and WPPL (Jiang et al., 2024), which combines windowed PIBT with large neighborhood search (Li et al., 2021a; 2022) and traffic guidance graphs. Although guidance graph optimization (Zhang et al., 2024) automates edge-cost tuning over these graphs, the underlying planning algorithms remain fixed. Alternatively, learning-based formulations train neural policies via reinforcement or imitation learning (Sartoretti et al., 2019; Damani et al., 2021; Skrynnik et al., 2024; Andreychuk et al., 2025), with recent neural plan-ners and schedulers outperforming earlier benchmark baselines (Jiang et al., 2025; Gaber et al., 2026). Large language models (LLMs) have also been explored as direct online planners; how-ever, prompting LLMs to emit next-step moves sequentially struggles to scale beyond small stylized instances (Chen et al., 2025). 

Automated Program Synthesis. Policy search in code space builds upon a broader lineage of pro-gram synthesis and algorithmic discovery. Early programmatic reinforcement learning formulated policies as interpretable programs, though restricted to small, domain-specific languages (Verma et al., 2018; Trivedi et al., 2021). Modern LLMs lift these grammar constraints, enabling coding agents to manipulate general-purpose programming languages such as C++ and Python directly. Re-cent frameworks leverage this capability to evolve individual functions or isolated files for discrete algorithms and combinatorial optimization heuristics (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024; Novikov et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024). Complementary lines of work optimize prompt graphs and textual variables through verbal feedback and execution traces (Yuksekgonul et al., 2025; Agrawal et al., 2026). More recently, automated loops have advanced to whole programs and repositories, in SAT solving, model training, and optimization contests (Yu et al., 2025; Karpathy, 2026; Imajuku et al., 2025), as well as dispatch and charging policies for automated guided vehicles without spatial path planning (Li & Li, 2026). Our study differs from existing literature in the following aspects. 1 ) Whereas existing automated MAPF methods predominantly tune heuristic parameters or train parametric neural weights, our work searches over planner and scheduler implementations directly in code space. 2 ) Furthermore, while prior program synthesis frameworks rely on sophisticated evolutionary harnesses for stylized benchmarks, we demonstrate that state-of-the-art coding agents discover effective algorithms for demanding LMAPF problems without rigid search harnesses. 

# 3 PROBLEM SETUP 

We consider an environment consisting of n agents, a finite directed configuration graph G =(V, E ), and a discrete planning horizon of T clock ticks. Each vertex u ∈ V represents an agent configuration, such as a discrete position and orientation. Each directed edge e = ( u, v ) ∈ E

denotes a feasible motion with strictly positive traversal duration δ(e) ∈ Z>0 ticks. At any tick 

t ∈ { 0, . . . , T }, an agent is either stationary at a vertex u ∈ V or traversing an edge e ∈ E with 

τ ∈ { 1, . . . , δ (e)} ticks remaining before arrival. Waiting is permitted only at vertices; edge traver-sals are non-preemptive and must proceed until completion. Collision avoidance is formalized through a spatial conflict map. Let Γ : V ∪ E → 2V ∪E denote a conflict function mapping any occupied vertex or edge to the set of configurations and edges that no other agent may occupy simultaneously. A valid multi-agent configuration requires that for any pair of agents occupying elements x, y ∈ V ∪ E, y / ∈ Γ( x). A stationary agent at vertex u occupies only 

u. A moving agent traversing e = ( u, v ) occupies edge e throughout traversal, its departure vertex 

u at the initiation tick, and its arrival vertex v at the completion tick. This general mapping captures classical grid-based vertex and swap conflicts, as well as complex footprint conflicts in continuous warehouse layouts. # 4 POLICY SEARCH IN CODE SPACE 

In policy search in code space (PSCS), we search for the source code of the coordination policy itself (Figure 2). We initiate the search by specifying the environment, operational constraints, performance objec-tives, and an initial algorithm or reference implementation. Rather than using a rigid, specialized evolutionary harness, we prompt the coding agent directly in natural language, with minimal steering or redirecting as the search progresses. Remarkably, we find state-of-the-art coding agents already effective without additional augmentation from an external search harness. Once the search is initiated, the coding agent iteratively refines the codebase through a four-stage loop: 1 ) proposing algorithmic edits, 2 ) implementing source-level modifications, 3 ) evaluating candidates in closed-loop simulation, and 4 ) diagnosing bottlenecks from execution traces and pro-filer logs. Importantly, language models are queried strictly offline during development; policy execution at runtime is handled entirely by compiled binaries with zero model inference latency. During the search, all candidate policies must pass the following hierarchical verification before ac-ceptance. First, a candidate must compile cleanly and pass unit tests enforcing collision avoidance, deadlock prevention, and compute budgets. Next, the coding agent uses fast short-horizon simula-tions to filter unpromising candidates. Lastly, surviving candidates undergo longer, multi-seed runs to confirm throughput improvements across stochastic task arrivals. Any candidate that fails the verification is rejected. We apply this workflow to two distinct case studies: refining competitive heuristics on an LMAPF competition benchmark (Section 5), and discovering a routing policy for industrial warehouses with a provable progress guarantee under stated assumptions (Section 6).

6.2 RESULTS AND ANALYSIS 

We benchmark our synthesized policy, named Core-Graph-Anchored Routing (CGAR), against an optimized proprietary production baseline. As in Section 5, all evaluations execute on identical hard-ware, pairing eight trials per map with fixed initial poses and shared random seeds across methods for order arrivals (CGAR’s decisions are deterministic). Table 2 reports throughput across eight paired two-hour evaluation runs for all six warehouse facil-ities. CGAR outperforms the proprietary solution by 58.9% on WAREHOUSE-01 and achieves higher throughput across the remaining five layouts where the baseline was uncalibrated. The proprietary solution relies on extensive manual calibration of lane directions, traversal costs, and vertex-level stopping rules for each specific floor plan, limiting its transferability across facilities. In contrast, because CGAR operates on topology-agnostic coordination, a single synthesized policy transfers directly across all layouts without any site-specific recalibration. Across fleet sizes from 8 to 65 robots and mean route lengths from 21 to 74 m, CGAR consistently sustains 9.5 to 16.9 orders per hour per robot. Figure 7 illustrates how the coding agent synthesized and refined CGAR on WAREHOUSE-01. The initial seed implementation enforced the progress guarantee too rigidly, actuating robots in near-sequential fashion, completing almost zero orders. The agent then unlocked concurrent execution by certifying simultaneous transitions that preserved a return path to a position where progress is guaranteed ( ∼350 orders/hour). It next resolved stop-and-go patterns and fleet freezes by stabilizing lane admission decisions ( ∼440 orders/hour). Throughput then plateaued near 445 orders/hour be-cause robots took unnecessary detours around temporarily occupied corridors. The upgraded coding models (Astra and Fable 5.1) resolved this by introducing route commitments and fair queue release (630.6 orders/hour). Finally, the agent improved long-distance yielding maneuvers and refined or-der dispatching (775.3 orders/hour). Across the entire synthesis process, the search utilized 177.8 elapsed development hours and consumed 22.0M output tokens ( ≈$4,800). The resulting CGAR architecture decouples formal progress certification from heuristic throughput optimization: At its foundation, an invariant ensures that every admitted motion keeps a recovery plan back to the core; under the assumptions of Theorem 1 (Appendix A), this guarantees that every accepted goal that is not withdrawn or replaced is completed in finite time. Operating above this safety layer, throughput heuristics optimize traffic flow without the risk of inducing deadlock under the same assumptions. Specifically, the planner treats occupied lanes as temporary delays rather than static obstacles whenever the current occupant has committed to departure, avoiding unnecessary detouring loops. Concurrently, the task dispatcher assigns orders based on predicted lane congestion and trip duration, preventing bottlenecks before traffic accumulates.

Together, these findings establish PSCS as a viable, highly effective methodology for complex combinatorial algorithm design. Both the search methodology and the resulting policies exhibit strong generalization capabilities. Because competitive LoRR entries rely on map-specific calibration, we evaluate whether PLATM transfers effectively to unseen layouts when provided comparable tuning. As detailed in Ap-pendix B.1, recalibrating PLATM on a newly generated layout yields throughput within 3.9% of the competition benchmark across all fleet densities. In the industrial warehouse domain, CGAR exhibits zero-shot generalization: evaluated on the unseen synthetic layout WAREHOUSE-00 with-out any parameter adjustment, it sustains 16.98 orders per hour per robot, matching the operational efficiency observed on production facilities of comparable scale (Appendix B.2). A primary operational constraint of PSCS is the computational and financial cost of empirical eval-uation. The synthesis of PLATM required 42.3 wall-clock hours and approximately $700 in API usage, whereas CGAR required 177.8 active development hours and approximately $4,800. This expenditure is driven largely by the simulation bottleneck: each candidate policy must be evaluated over extended temporal horizons and multiple stochastic realizations before returning informative performance feedback to the coding agent. Accelerating policy evaluation is therefore a critical research direction, motivating techniques such as temporal parallelization (Farias et al., 2025), im-portance sampling, and efficient surrogate simulators. A notable trait of policies synthesized through PSCS is their accumulated software complexity. Whereas human engineers prioritize modularity, architectural symmetry, and maintainability, the coding agent optimizes strictly for empirical performance. As the agent resolves successive edge cases and failure modes, the codebase accumulates tightly coupled heuristics, yielding monolithic implementations that are challenging for developers to audit or modify. Future iterations of PSCS should incorporate software engineering best practices into the search objective, such as penalizing cyclomatic complexity or introducing periodic refactorization. We also note a methodological consideration in our experimental execution: the policies in Sec-tions 5 and 6 were developed through organic, human-in-the-loop steering rather than a fully au-tonomous, hardcoded search harness. It remains an open question to what extent rigidly encapsulat-ing search logic within a static agentic scaffold risks overfitting to the limitations of a specific model release. Systematically characterizing the optimal boundary between programmatic search scaffold-ing and open-ended, model-driven steering represents an important direction for future inquiry. Looking forward, PSCS represents a compelling paradigm for combinatorial algorithm design in verifiable domains. Like traditional software engineering, PSCS produces source code that incurs zero neural inference latency at runtime and admits formal safety verification under stated assump-tions, as demonstrated by the progress certificates of CGAR (Theorem 1). Simultaneously, like deep learning, it scales with compute and optimizes directly against empirical loss landscapes through automated iteration. By uniting the formal verifiability of symbolic programs with the scalabil-9ity of data-driven optimization, PSCS offers a practical alternative to both manual engineering and end-to-end learning. CGAR is briefly described in Section 6. This appendix gives the full algorithm (Section A.2). It proves that every execution is collision-free and that every goal the task layer does not withdraw or replace is completed in finite time (Section A.3). A.1 OVERVIEW 

CGAR is a PIBT-style planner (Okumura et al., 2022) for robots with continuous geometry that move asynchronously. It has two layers. The throughput layer proposes motions using priority inheritance and heuristics. The safety layer decides which proposals are committed, and supplies certified motions when no proposal can be committed. This follows the Simplex architecture (Sha, 2001): an unverified controller runs freely as long as a verified fallback remains available. Before a run, CGAR selects a set of ground poses on the map, called core nodes , and fixed motions that connect them. Together, they form the core graph . The robots are on the core graph when every robot occupies a different core node and no motion is pending. The safety layer rests on one invariant: at every moment, all robots can be brought back to the core graph . CGAR commits a motion only if two conditions hold. First, the motion is collision-free. Second, after the motion is committed, there exists a recovery plan : a plan that brings all robots back to the core graph. These two checks maintain the invariant. Once the robots are on the core graph, and there are more core nodes than robots, any robot can reach any core node by shifting other robots along a vacancy, like a sliding puzzle. Goals are served oldest-first. If the oldest goal remains primary for D seconds, CGAR activates a deadline-triggered fallback at the next decision boundary. It stops all other work, executes the recovery plan, and moves the primary robot to its goal with vacancy shifts. A.2 ALGORITHM 

We use the configuration graph G = ( V, E ) and the conflict map Γ of Sections 3 and 6.1. Execution is asynchronous: the actual duration of a motion may differ from its nominal duration, and robots start and finish their motions at different times. CGAR makes a decision at every decision boundary :when a motion or service finishes, when the task layer assigns, replaces or withdraws a goal, or at a periodic controller tick (1 s by default). A path is a short sequence of motions of one robot, and each robot executes at most one committed path at a time. A new path is committed only if two conditions hold. (i) The volume it sweeps, the union of S(e) over its motions e (Section 6.1), is disjoint from the volume swept by every other path in progress, so the two cannot collide, whatever their timing. A recovery plan is a sequence of steps that brings all robots back to the core graph, starting once every committed path has finished. Each step is a set of paths, at most one per robot, that satisfy (i) and (ii) with respect to each other and to every robot standing still, so they can run at the same time. Theorem 1. Under (K1)–(K5), (E1), (E2) and (S), every execution of CGAR is collision-free, a recovery plan exists at every decision, and every goal is completed in finite time unless the task layer withdraws or replaces it. 

Lemma A.1 (Safe Commitment) . Suppose the robots start at non-overlapping positions, and every committed path satisfies (i) and (ii) when it is committed. Then no two robots ever overlap, however long each motion takes. Proof. A robot never leaves the volume swept by its last committed path, which includes the path’s end. By (i), this volume is disjoint from that of every path in progress when the path was committed, and by (ii), from every robot standing still at that time. Paths committed later are checked against this one, or against the robot standing at its end, in the same way. 

Lemma A.2 (Recovery Is Always Possible) . CGAR always stores a recovery plan. Proof. Initially, a recovery plan exists by (E2). We show that every decision keeps one. Between decisions, robots only carry out committed paths, so the stored plan stays valid. At a decision, CGAR commits one of the following. • Outside the protected fallback, paths are committed only if a recovery plan exists after-wards. • Recover executes the next step of the stored plan. The remaining steps form a recovery plan. • Promote moves one robot along a core path while all robots are on the core graph. By (K1) and (K2), all robots are on the core graph again once it finishes, so no further recovery is needed. • Serve moves the primary from its entry node to its goal while all other robots are on the core graph. By (S), this conflicts with no other core node, and the path back to the entry node is a recovery plan. Drain commits nothing. 

Lemma A.3 (Vacancy Shift) . Let the robots be on the core graph, let robot r stand at core node u,and let u → v be a core edge of P . Then at most n − 1 other robots can each shift one core node along a path in H that avoids u, after which r moves to v. This executes at most n core paths, one at a time, and the robots are on the core graph after each core path. Proof. By (K5), at least n core nodes are reachable from v in H without passing through u. At most 

n − 1 robots other than r stand on them, so at least one of them is vacant. Let h be a vacant one closest to v, and take a shortest path v = q0, . . . , q k = h in H that avoids u. Every core node on this path before h is occupied, so k ≤ n − 1. For j = k, . . . , 1, if a robot stands at qj−1, move it to qj , which is vacant at that point. Then v is vacant, and r moves from u to v. This uses at most 

k + 1 ≤ n core paths. Each core path moves one robot while the others stand on core nodes, so by (K1) and (K2) it causes no conflict and ends with the robots on the core graph.

New goals receive larger tickets, so the protected goal stays the primary’s goal until it ends.
