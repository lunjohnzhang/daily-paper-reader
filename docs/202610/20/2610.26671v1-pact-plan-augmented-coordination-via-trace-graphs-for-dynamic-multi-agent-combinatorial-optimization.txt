## ABSTRACT 

Multi-agent approaches have been investigated recently as a means of improving the scalability of reinforcement learning for neural combinatorial optimization. Existing methods assume that all problem instance information is known upfront. However, real-world problems are often dynamic : relevant information is pro-gressively revealed as the agents take actions (e.g., arriving at an edge in a routing problem only to discover it is untraversable). In such cases, existing methods fail to coordinate agents to explore and exploit the progressively revealed informa-tion. To address the arising coordination challenge, we introduce PACT ( Plan-

Augmented Coordination via Trace-graphs). PACT augments the environment graph with auxiliary nodes representing the plans of agents, which are connected to the resource nodes to indicate future dependencies. The proposed message-passing scheme over the augmented graph grounds inter-agent coordination in the environment and enables proactive, rather than reactive, conflict resolution. We show that PACT improves solution quality in dynamic problems relative to base-lines and exhibits the best transfer to larger instances on which, remarkably, it outperforms centralized oracles with privileged access to instance uncertainty. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Many routing, scheduling, and allocation problems require several agents to construct a joint com-binatorial solution under a shared objective such as total travel cost or makespan, a setting known as multi-agent combinatorial optimization (MACO) (Berto et al., 2025). Existing formulations typi-cally assume that the instance to be solved is fully specified before construction begins. In many real systems, however, relevant information becomes available only through execution: a route may be discovered to be untraversable only when an agent reaches it, or a machine may become known to be unavailable only when an operation is attempted (Pillac et al., 2013; Ouelhadj & Petrovic, 2009). We call this setting dynamic multi-agent combinatorial optimization (DMACO). Classic combinatorial optimization typically assumes fully disclosed instances upfront. Although reactive re-optimization can adapt to newly revealed information, solving must recur continuously (Vieira et al., 2003; Bertsimas et al., 2019), and such reactive paradigms generally do not account for how current decisions affect information and coordination requirements that arise later (Ulmer, 2019). Neural combinatorial optimization (NCO) amortizes the cost of re-solving by learning poli-cies over instance distributions (Kool et al., 2019; Kwon et al., 2020; Kool et al., 2022) and has recently been extended to multi-agent settings (Berto et al., 2025; Luttmann & Xie, 2026). Nonethe-less, these NCO approaches still assume fixed and fully observable instances prior to execution. Since information in DMACO is revealed through agents’ actions, we formulate it as a sequential decision-making problem, naturally suited to multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) (Bern-stein et al., 2002; Oliehoek & Amato, 2016; Yu et al., 2022). Existing MARL approaches typically manage coordination via agent-centric representations (Sunehag et al., 2018; Guestrin et al., 2002; Sukhbaatar et al., 2016). Alongside neural MACO solvers that resolve step-level conflicts during 1arXiv:2610.26671v1 [cs.LG] 19 Oct 2026 parallel decoding (Berto et al., 2025), these methods broadly share a common limitation: they lo-calize coordination at the agent or step level, without explicitly modeling the shared environmental structure that drives their interdependencies. In DMACO, coordination requirements are funda-mentally induced by the environment. For instance, multiple agents might contend for a capacity-limited resource, or one agent’s action might re-solve local uncertainty crucial to another’s future trajectory. Because these dependencies are grounded in the environment, relying solely on agent-level rela-tions creates a structural mismatch : the underlying graph elements driving the coordination are excluded from the representational mechanism. Furthermore, restricting coordination to the current step introduces a temporal mismatch . An agent might commit to a trajectory that appears locally conflict-free, only to conflict with another agent’s plan several steps later, as illustrated in Figure 1. To address these structural and temporal mismatches, we introduce PACT ( Plan-Augmented 

Coordination via Trace-graphs), a novel coordination framework for DMACO. First, each agent proposes multiple candidate plans. Every candidate plan is then attached to the environment graph via its projected trace , a record of the graph elements on which it places demand. By perform-ing coordination on this plan-augmented trace-graph, PACT enables the underlying environmental structure to explicitly mediate dependencies across agents’ future candidate plans. We refer to this mechanism as environment-mediated coordination . Our key contributions are as follows: • We propose PACT, a novel coordination framework for DMACO problems that represents coor-dination requirements directly through the environmental structure that induces them, rather than through ungrounded relations defined between agents. • We introduce the plan-augmented trace-graph, which attaches candidate plans to the elements they demand. The proposed message-passing scheme resolves contention over remaining capacities, thereby coordinating agents over future decision steps rather than only the current one. • On three DMACO problems spanning routing and scheduling, we show that PACT outperforms neural MACO solvers and MARL methods in solution quality and zero-shot generalization on instances up to 64 × larger than the training set, at a fraction of the runtime of online replanning. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Neural Multi-Agent Combinatorial Optimization. NCO amortizes the cost of solving recurring optimization problems by learning solution policies over instance distributions (Bengio et al., 2021; Cappart et al., 2023). Constructive approaches generate solutions autoregressively (Kool et al., 2019; Kwon et al., 2020), with multi-agent extensions coordinating agents’ decisions to construct a joint solution. Sequential methods decode agents’ actions in turn (Son et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024), whereas parallel methods simultaneously decode agents’ actions with inter-agent communication (Zong et al., 2022; Hua et al., 2025). These approaches focus on specific MACO problems, while PARCO (Berto et al., 2025) provides a general framework for parallel decoding across MACO prob-lems, resolving conflicting selections through a learned priority-based handler. MACSIM (Luttmann & Xie, 2026) learns through self-improvement to predict joint agent-action assignments that are 2conflict-free by construction. These methods assume that problem instances are fully specified be-fore solution construction begins and coordinate agents at the current step. They therefore do not explicitly reason about information revealed during execution or dependencies between agents’ de-cisions across future steps. 

Coordination in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning. Cooperative MARL typically employs centralized training with decentralized execution (Bernstein et al., 2002; Oliehoek & Amato, 2016), under which representative algorithms such as multi-agent PPO (MAPPO) (Yu et al., 2022) train a shared centralized critic, whereas independent PPO (IPPO) (De Witt et al., 2020) uses decentralized critics. Coordination is commonly achieved via value factorization, such as additive decomposi-tion in VDN (Sunehag et al., 2018) and monotonic mixing in QMIX (Rashid et al., 2020), explicit representations like deep coordination graphs (DCG) (B¨ ohmer et al., 2020), or inter-agent commu-nication (Sukhbaatar et al., 2016; Foerster et al., 2016; Das et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2024). In all these families, the coordination medium is agent-centric (e.g., agent utilities, pairwise payoffs, or directed messages). This introduces two limitations when these approaches are applied to DMACO. First, pairwise decompositions struggle to capture simultaneous multi-agent dependencies without incurring the exponential inference costs of higher-order factors (Guestrin et al., 2002; Kok & Vlas-sis, 2006). Second, these mechanisms coordinate the agents’ actions at the current state, whereas conflicts in the future are reflected only implicitly through learned value estimates. In DMACO settings, PACT addresses both limitations through environment-mediated coordination. By connecting each candidate plan to the graph elements its trace occupies, PACT captures interac-tions between candidate plans, such as contention for shared capacity or dependence on unrevealed information, at the element that induces them. This element-centric aggregation accommodates multi-agent dependencies without explicit agent-pair factors, while candidate traces expose coordi-nation requirements spanning future decision steps.

## 4 METHODOLOGY 

We now present PACT, whose overall architecture is illustrated in Figure 2. Each agent first gen-erates a set of candidate plans instead of committing to a single next action (Section 4.1). The candidate plans are incorporated into the graph through the elements they occupy, forming a plan-augmented trace-graph over which inter-agent coordination is performed (Section 4.2). Each agent subsequently selects one of its coordinated candidate plans and executes its first action (Section 4.3). Finally, all modules are trained end to end (Section 4.4). The complete forward pass of PACT is summarized in Algorithm 1. 4.1 CANDIDATE PLAN GENERATION 

As was illustrated in Figure 1, coordination over individual actions is inherently myopic: agents whose current actions are compatible may still contend for the same resources several steps ahead. PACT therefore coordinates over short-horizon candidate plans. However, planning in the joint ac-tion space scales exponentially with both the number of agents and the planning horizon (Bernstein et al., 2002; Kearns et al., 2002). We instead decouple plan proposal from multi-agent coordination: each agent independently generates a compact set of candidate plans, while interactions among these plans are handled by the coordination mechanism in Section 4.2. PACT learns its plan generation and plan-selection policies from the return. Unlike a conventional policy in which the sampled policy output is the environment action, PACT produces each agent’s action through a structured sequence of stochastic decisions: candidate plans are first generated and subsequently selected after coordination. The policy objective therefore needs to account for the internal decisions that give rise to the executed action. We decompose the policy update into three components: candidate plan proposal (prop ), plan rollout (roll ), and plan selection (sel ). ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETTING 

Problems. We evaluate PACT on three representative DMACO problems, spanning both routing and scheduling domains. In the Multi-Agent Canadian Traveller Problem (MACTP) (Papadim-itriou & Yannakakis, 1991; Shiri & Salman, 2017), a team traverses a graph to collectively visit goal nodes at minimum total travel cost, with edge traversability revealed upon reaching an endpoint or attempting a crossing. Travelers are agents and goal nodes have unit capacity. In the Dynamic Flexible Job-Shop Problem (DFJSP) (Ouelhadj & Petrovic, 2009), jobs comprise precedence-constrained operations assigned to eligible machines; whether an uncertain machine is operational or broken is revealed when an operation is dispatched to it. In the Non-Clairvoyant Flexible Flow Shop Problem (NCFFSP) (Motwani et al., 1994), jobs pass through ordered stages of interchange-able machines whose latent processing-time factors are revealed upon dispatch. Both scheduling problems minimize makespan, with machines acting as agents and jobs as unit-capacity elements. Appendix A provides the formal models and revelation rules. 

Baselines. We compare PACT with two multi-agent NCO methods, PARCO and MACSIM, which are evaluated as centralized joint-construction solvers through task wrappers, retaining their origi-nal training objectives and model configurations. For PACT, PARCO, and MACSIM two decoding strategies named greedy (g.) and sampling (s.) are used. We also compare four cooperative MARL baselines (MAPPO, IPPO, VDN, and DCG), using matched graph encoders over the common obser-vation interface and shared parameters across agents. MAPPO, IPPO, and VDN produce per-agent actions, whereas DCG coordinates joint actions through max-plus message passing on an agent coor-dination graph. PACT uses a critic conditioned on the graph observation and performs decentralized execution with communication through the plan-augmented trace-graph, in which each agent selects its own candidate plan. Gurobi (Gurobi Optimization, LLC, 2026) and OR-Tools (Perron & Furnon) provide online replanning baselines and oracle references. Appendix B.1 details the baseline imple-mentations and adaptations. 

Training. For each problem, all learning-based methods are trained on the same instance set with a mixture of small sizes. The mixture spans n ∈ { 10 , 13 , 16 } nodes for MACTP, N ∈ { 12 , 16 , 20 }

jobs for DFJSP, and N ∈ { 12 , 16 , 20 } jobs with 3 stages and 3 or 4 machines per stage for NCFFSP. Dataset generation seeds are fixed, and each method is trained with 10 independent seeds. The training configurations are given in Appendix B. 

Evaluation. All methods use the same 100 test instances per configuration, and learning-based results are averaged across the 10 training seeds. Instance sizes are denoted by nodes × agents ×

goals ( n × A × G) for MACTP, jobs × machines × operations ( N × M × O) for DFJSP, and jobs 

× machines per stage × stages ( N × Mℓ × S) for NCFFSP. The first configuration in each problem block is in-distribution (ID), matching the training mixture’s largest size; the remaining four evaluate zero-shot transfer (ZST) to larger instances. The final two NCFFSP configurations also increase the stage count from 3 to 4. Appendix C.2 details the evaluation protocols. 5.2 MAIN RESULTS

## 6 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we introduced PACT, a framework for DMACO that enables agents to coordinate over candidate plans through the environmental structure that induces their dependencies. By augmenting the environment graph with candidate plan traces and performing capacity-aware message passing over the resulting trace-graph, PACT extends coordination beyond the current decision step while retaining closed-loop execution as new information is revealed. Our experiments on routing and 9scheduling problems demonstrate that PACT consistently achieves strong solution quality and com-pletion success across both in-distribution and zero-shot transfer settings, with particularly strong generalization to larger instances. In terms of limitations, the global communication assumption be-hind MPOMDPs may be relaxed in future work by imposing communication radiuses inside which information is shared. Additionally, PACT samples independently from agents’ policies πi to con-struct the joint action at inference time. This appendix defines the optimization problems and residual deterministic models (i.e., models of the remaining problem at each decision step) used by the baselines. A.1 MACTP 

Problem Definition. MACTP generalizes the Canadian Traveler Problem (Papadimitriou & Yan-nakakis, 1991) to a cooperative team that starts from distinct nodes and covers shared goals on a connected undirected graph with positive traversal costs. Agents try to reach each goal by traversing a graph, but a subset of edges may be blocked, with a given probability, and the true state of the edge is only revealed when an agent reaches one of its endpoints. The blockage probability of each edge is determined at instance generation time. In order to fulfill a goal, an agent must take the action to claim it, which retires that agent. Agents may pass through a goal without claiming it, and may pass through an already claimed goal. The episode succeeds once all goals have been claimed. A representative MACTP example was shown in Figure 1 in the main text. We formulate the optimization problem as finding a minimum-cost collection of walks that jointly cover the remaining goals.

MPOMDP Formulation. In the notation of Section 3, the latent variable ξ defines the blockage probability of the edges, while the graph state Gt is the known graph state. Node features contain goal coverage and claim status. Edge features contain traversal cost, blocking probability, and the currently known status, while each agent carries its present location as an agent feature. The active set Mt contains the whole team, although retired agents can only remain at their current nodes and live agents move only through deterministic or revealed-open edges. An agent may retire by claiming an unclaimed goal, where unit capacity and unit claim demand enforce exclusivity, while non-goal nodes impose no shared constraint. The step cost C(Gt, at, ξ ) is the team’s total traversal cost, with retired agents incurring zero cost. Episodes end after all goals are covered or all agents retire, and truncated episodes additionally receive an estimate of the remaining completion cost. A.2 DFJSP 

Problem Definition. In DFJSP, every job follows an ordered chain of operations, and each op-eration is eligible for a specified subset of machines. Some machines are unavailable from the beginning of an episode, but their condition remains hidden until the first attempted dispatch. A failed dispatch leaves the operation unscheduled and delays its job by a fixed amount, after which the broken machine is removed from future decisions. Failures are correlated through latent groups whose memberships and priors are observed, while the group condition and each machine-specific outcome remain hidden. Machines act as agents and may schedule operations before becoming idle, with processing beginning once both the selected machine and job are available. The objec-tive function is the makespan of the resulting schedule. Figure 5 visualizes an illustrative DFJSP instance. Constraints and Online Replanning. Each remaining operation is assigned once and must follow its job predecessor, while the release constraints preserve the current job and machine timelines. The pairwise disjunctions prevent operations on the same machine from overlapping. At each replan-ning step, completed operations and revealed-broken machines are removed, whereas unrevealed machines remain provisionally available with their known processing times. 

MPOMDP Formulation. The latent variable ξ records the condition of every uncertain machine, with correlations induced by group-level variables. Each element of Gt represents a job and records its next unscheduled operation and earliest feasible start time. Machine features contain availability, group membership, and the current condition estimate, while an eligibility edge carries the process-ing time for the job’s next operation. The active set Mt contains every machine not known to be broken, including busy machines, which may either dispatch an eligible job or wait. A scheduled operation starts no earlier than the release times of both participants, while unit job capacity permits at most one dispatch to each job per round. The step cost C(Gt, at, ξ ) is the increase in makespan, so its undiscounted sum equals Cmax for a completed schedule. A truncated episode additionally receives a penalty of processing all remaining operations serially on their slowest available machines, providing a pessimistic completion cost that prevents stalling from being beneficial. 17 A.3 NCFFSP 

Problem Definition. In NCFFSP, jobs pass through a common sequence of stages, and each stage is served by parallel machines whose processing times depend on both the job and the selected machine (Kwon et al., 2021). Some machines also have hidden condition factors that scale their processing times and are correlated through latent batches. Batch membership and its prior are observed, but a machine’s realized factor appears only after its first dispatch. Unlike DFJSP, every dispatch succeeds because uncertainty changes processing duration rather than machine eligibility, and busy machines may schedule jobs that begin once both participants are available. The objective is the final makespan, and non-clairvoyance here refers to the initially unknown machine-dependent duration factors (Motwani et al., 1994). Figure 6 illustrates an NCFFSP workflow. Constraints and Online Replanning The assignment and non-overlap constraints play the same roles as in DFJSP, while Eq. (28) enforces the shared stage order. The release bounds preserve the current job and machine timelines, while online replanning uses nominal durations until a machine is revealed and realized durations thereafter. Every machine remains eligible for future assignments. 

MPOMDP Formulation. NCFFSP uses the job-machine graph interface of DFJSP, but its latent variable ξ contains machine-dependent duration factors rather than binary failure states, and each job element records its next stage and earliest start time. Machine features store the served stage, current availability, batch membership, and either the prior or the revealed factor, while each eligibility edge carries the effective processing time known at that step. Every machine remains in Mt, including those that are currently busy, and may either dispatch a job at its stage or wait. Each job has unit capacity within a round, and a dispatch directed to that job contributes unit demand, while the scheduled start time respects both participants’ release times and prevents assignments on the same machine from overlapping. The step cost C(Gt, at, ξ ) is the increase in makespan, whose undiscounted sum equals the final makespan for a completed schedule. A truncated episode additionally receives a penalty of the makespan required to complete the schedule from the truncated state under a uniformly random legal policy. This penalty is separate from the NCFFSP objective and ensures that giving up is no better than acting randomly.

B.1 BASELINES 

Classic Solvers. The Gurobi and OR-Tools classic solvers are ran in two modes. The baselines marked Oracle in the results solve complete realizations using privileged access to the uncertainty with a time limit of 10 minutes. The Replan baselines repeatedly solve the remaining problem under the information available at each decision step, with a time limit of 2 seconds for each re-solve. 

Gurobi. Gurobi (Gurobi Optimization, LLC, 2026) solves the mixed-integer linear programs in Appendix A using branch-and-cut. We retain the best feasible solution found for each instance under the time budget and report the mean objective value across the evaluation set. 

OR-Tools. OR-Tools (Perron & Furnon) provides a complementary CP-SAT implementation un-der the same information settings and evaluation configurations. 

Graph-based MARL Baselines. MAPPO, IPPO, VDN, and DCG use matched graph encoders over the common observation interface, and their per-agent networks share parameters within the team while the flow of information among agents follows the centralized or decentralized structure of each algorithm. 19 Table 2: Summary of licenses for baselines. MAPPO. Multi-Agent PPO (Yu et al., 2022) uses a shared-parameter decentralized actor and a centralized critic, so each actor conditions on the common graph information and its own agent features while the critic aggregates information across the team. Both networks are trained with the clipped PPO objective and generalized advantage estimation. 

IPPO. Independent PPO (De Witt et al., 2020) uses the same shared-parameter decentralized ac-tor as MAPPO, but replaces the centralized critic with a decentralized value function. The critic produces one estimate per agent from that agent’s observation, and training uses the same clipped PPO objective and advantage estimator. 

VDN. Value Decomposition Networks (Sunehag et al., 2018) learn shared per-agent action-value functions and select each agent’s action independently, after which the selected utilities are summed into a team value, Qtot = P 

> i

Qi, trained from a common temporal-difference target. 

DCG. Deep Coordination Graphs (B¨ ohmer et al., 2020) combine per-agent utility terms with pair-wise payoff terms defined on a fixed, fully connected coordination graph, and at each step max-plus message passing approximately maximizes the resulting joint action-value function to select the agents’ actions. 

PARCO. PARCO (Berto et al., 2025) is a parallel autoregressive neural solver in which a shared transformer encodes the instance and communication layers exchange context among agent queries. A multiple-pointer decoder proposes simultaneous actions before a learned priority rule resolves conflicts and assigns fallback actions to displaced agents. 

MACSIM. MACSIM (Luttmann & Xie, 2026) also constructs multi-agent solutions autoregres-sively from a shared instance representation. To produce a conflict-free set of simultaneous actions, it samples agent-task pairs without replacement from a joint categorical distribution at each con-struction step. 

Neural Solver Adaptation. We implement task wrappers for PARCO and MACSIM through their respective environment interfaces while preserving the instances, transition rules, action constraints, and objectives used to evaluate the other methods. 

Solution Decoding Strategies. We evaluate PARCO, MACSIM, and PACT under two solution decoding strategies: greedy and sampling . Greedy decoding uses one deterministic rollout, while sampling decoding generates 128 independent stochastic rollouts per method and instance and re-tains that method’s best realized solution (Berto et al., 2025; Luttmann & Xie, 2026). For greedy decoding, PARCO and MACSIM select the argmax of their action distributions, while in PACT the internal candidate plan generation, virtual rollout, and plan selection are all made deterministic. MACTP. Following SALSA-CLRS (Minder et al., 2023), each instance uses a sparse, connected Erd˝ os–R´ enyi graph over n nodes, with agent starts and goals sampled uniformly at random and positive traversal costs assigned to its edges. In the reference configuration, 25% of the edges are stochastic, and their traversability is sampled at generation time but remains hidden until encoun-tered, as described in Appendix A.1. 

DFJSP. We follow the synthetic FJSP generation procedure of Song et al. (2022): each job con-tains four to six ordered operations, each operation is assigned a random subset of eligible machines, and its machine-dependent processing times vary within 20% of an operation-level mean. Each ma-chine is initially designated stochastic with probability 0.6, after which a feasibility repair ensures that every operation retains at least one always-available eligible machine. Stochastic machines have fixed hidden availability and correlated breakdown probabilities within small machine groups, so observing one machine can inform the condition of others. Availability is revealed only when a job is first dispatched to the machine, and the resulting transition follows Appendix A.2. 

NCFFSP. Each instance contains N jobs that pass through S processing stages, with several inter-changeable machines available at each stage. Following Kwon et al. (2021), nominal job–machine processing times are sampled independently and uniformly from the integers {2, . . . , 9}. Each real machine is independently designated stochastic with probability 0.6 and receives a fixed hidden con-dition factor generated from a group-correlated latent variable u ∈ [0 , 1] through ξ = 0 .8 + 7 .2u4.The factor is revealed on the machine’s first dispatch and scales its processing times thereafter, as described in Appendix A.3. C.4 ABLATION STUDY DETAILS 

In this section, we provide further details regarding the evaluation protocol and results of the ablation study reported in Section 5.3 in the main text. All ablation experiments are conducted with greedy evaluation on the largest zero-shot transfer configuration of each problem. We use the PACT model with K = 4 candidate plans and R = 2 

coordination rounds as the reference model. We define the performance ratio as cost divided by the reference model’s mean cost on the same problem, and compute the time ratio in the same way using end-to-end runtime. Both ratios equal one for the reference model, and lower values are better. We present the ablation results in Figure 4. For each method or configuration, bars show mean performance ratios across seeds, with error bars indicating one standard deviation, while lines show mean time ratios. 

Effect of Coordination. We compare PACT, PACT without coordination, and MAPPO in Fig-ure 4(a). PACT without coordination omits message passing on the plan-augmented trace-graph, so each agent selects its candidate plan independently. PACT achieves the lowest mean performance ratio on all three problems. Removing coordination substantially degrades performance, particu-larly on MACTP and NCFFSP, and makes it worse than MAPPO on DFJSP and NCFFSP. This indicates that the gains arise from using shared elements to mediate candidate selection rather than from candidate generation alone. 

Number of Candidate Plans. We vary the number of candidate plans K ∈ { 1, 4, 8, 16 } in Fig-ure 4(b). With K = 1 , each agent exposes only one plan to the trace-graph, so coordination cannot redirect it to an alternative. This setting degrades performance on every problem and produces high variability on NCFFSP, whereas four candidates provide most of the observed improvement. Larger sets yield no consistent gain beyond the variation across seeds. The time ratio reflects both per-step computation and episode length, so longer episodes can offset the savings from fewer candidates. The sharp escalation in the time ratio on MACTP at large K is consistent with Hp = 4 , compared with Hp = 1 on DFJSP and NCFFSP. 27 Number of Coordination Rounds. We vary the number of coordination rounds R ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4} in Figure 4(c). The first round updates candidate plans from the loads induced by the initial preferences. Subsequent rounds recompute these loads after the preferences change, allowing plans to respond to how other agents have adjusted their selections. One round performs comparably on the scheduling problems but yields a higher mean performance ratio on MACTP, while the second closes this gap. This difference is consistent with MACTP’s longer plan traces capturing multi-step interactions, whereas the scheduling tasks use Hp = 1 . Further rounds provide no consistent improvement, and runtime remains nearly unchanged.
