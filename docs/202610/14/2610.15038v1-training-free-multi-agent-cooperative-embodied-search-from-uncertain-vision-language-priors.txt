# Training-Free Multi-Agent Cooperative Embodied Search from Uncertain Vision-Language Priors 

Fan Pu, Keshu Wu, Jiwan Jiang, Shiyu Shen, Zhiwen Fan, Zhengzhong Tu, Jiachen Li, and Yang Zhou 

Abstract —Real-world Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) re-quires agents to act under partial observability, where map structure, semantic cues, and target locations may be incomplete or incorrectly inferred by foundation models. This paper presents a lightweight, training-free, and uncertainty-aware framework for cooperative multi-agent multi-target search in mapless en-vironments. Instead of learning an end-to-end navigation policy, the framework separates high-level semantic reasoning from low-level execution through a scalable graph-optimization backbone. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) serve only as hypothesis generators, proposing semantic zones, target-location cues, and possible navigable connections. These outputs are treated as uncertain priors and further refined using online observations and graph-context evidence. Cooperative navigation is then formulated as a rolling-horizon multi-agent mixed-integer linear program with a lexicographic objective that first maxi-mizes target-directed reward and then reduces travel distance, uncertain-edge use, and repeated visits. Experiments derived from 100 Matterport3D test cases show that the proposed framework improves target coverage and search efficiency over representative baseline methods, while shared information and joint planning enable more balanced workloads across agents. By operating on an abstract graph without relying on embodiment-specific policy parameters, the framework scales across varying numbers of agents and targets, generalizes to unseen environ-ments, and accommodates different perception modules and low-level controllers without task-specific retraining. 

Index Terms —Multi-agent systems, cooperative search, vision-language navigation, mixed-integer linear programming. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Robots operating in human environments must make de-cisions from incomplete and evolving observations. Planning under partial observability and probabilistic robotics provide foundations for reasoning about uncertain states and environ-ment structure [1], [2], while mapping and SLAM enable navigation in unknown or partially known spaces [3]–[5]. Nev-ertheless, cooperative embodied search remains challenging when multiple robots must locate multiple targets without prior knowledge of the semantic layout of the environment, traversable connections, or target locations.

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN), Object-Goal Naviga-tion (ObjectNav), and their multi-object and open-vocabulary extensions have substantially advanced language-guided nav-igation and semantic object search [6]–[20]. Multi-robot task allocation, path planning, and reward-driven routing further provide mechanisms for coordinating agents under shared constraints [21]–[28]. However, most existing multi-robot for-mulations coordinate agents over known task representations and sufficiently reliable environment models, with task re-wards and graph connectivity largely treated as known during planning. These assumptions are restrictive in cooperative multi-agent multi-target search, where agents may duplicate one another’s exploration, targets compete for limited search effort, and each new observation can alter both target-location beliefs and feasible routes. Semantic exploration methods address part of this challenge by prioritizing frontiers, informative regions, or locations that are contextually related to a target [29]–[37]. Recent large language and vision-language models provide an additional source of commonsense knowledge and have been used to propose semantic subgoals, identify promising search regions, construct topological representations, and connect language instructions with robot actions [38]–[45]. Yet these outputs are not verified physical facts. Foundation models may hallucinate objects, semantic regions, or navigable connections [46], [47], making direct use of their predictions as action commands unreliable in partially observed environments. To address these limitations, this paper employs Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) as structured hypothesis generators for cooperative embodied search. From panoramic observations and accumulated context, the MLLM proposes semantic zones, target-location scores, and possible con-nections beyond the verified neighborhood. These uncertain hypotheses populate a dynamic semantic topological graph and are updated through physical observations. A rolling-horizon multi-agent Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) first maximizes target-directed reward and then minimizes travel distance, hypothesized-edge uncertainty, and revisits among reward-optimal routes. The abstract graph decouples semantic reasoning from embodiment-specific execution, al-lowing different perception modules and low-level controllers without retraining. For each current graph, belief state, and planning horizon, the MILP yields a solver-certified optimum, or an explicit optimality gap when terminated early. > •

We propose a training-free and uncertainty-aware frame-work for cooperative multi-agent multi-target search, in which MLLMs generate semantic and topological hy-arXiv:2610.15038v1 [cs.RO] 10 Oct 2026 2

II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Navigation, Mapping, and Embodied Search 

Planning under partial observability and probabilistic robotics provide the foundations for decision-making and state estimation under incomplete information [1], [2]. In mobile robotics, real-time navigation and map building, SLAM, and modern spatial mapping systems have enabled robots to op-erate in unknown or partially known environments [3]–[5]. However, object search requires more than geometric mapping: robots must also reason about semantic regions, likely object locations, and the value of exploring uncertain areas. VLN and ObjectNav provide two major embodied-navigation formulations related to this work. VLN focuses on following natural-language instructions in visually grounded environments [6]–[10], [48], whereas ObjectNav asks an agent to search for a specified object category or instance in an unfamiliar scene [11]–[15]. Recent multi-object, lifelong, and open-vocabulary navigation methods further extend these set-tings to longer horizons and broader target categories [16]– [20]. Nevertheless, most of these studies focus on a single agent and do not directly address cooperative multi-agent multi-target search with uncertain map connectivity and dy-namically changing target-location estimates. 

B. Multi-Agent Planning and Semantic Exploration 

Cooperative search couples task allocation, route construc-tion, and information acquisition. Multi-robot task allocation methods assign robots to tasks under coupled utilities and con-straints [21], [22], while multi-agent path-finding and coverage planning construct coordinated routes over shared spatial rep-resentations [23], [24], [49]. Team-orienteering formulations optimize reward collection under travel budgets [25], [26], but typically assume known rewards and graph structure. The difficulty increases under partial observability because one agent’s observations can change the value and feasibility of the plans assigned to other agents, consistent with the broader complexity of decentralized planning under uncertainty [50]. Frontier-based and information-gain-based exploration ex-pand unknown environments by prioritizing unexplored or informative regions [29], [30], and coordinated multi-robot exploration reduces redundant coverage by jointly considering information value and travel cost [31]–[33]. Semantic-search methods use object-place relations, semantic maps, memory, and contextual priors to prioritize locations likely to contain a target [13], [14], [34]–[37]. Vision-language frontier maps further guide exploration by ranking frontiers according to their visual-semantic relevance to the target [51]. In contrast to these approaches, our work coordinates multiple agents over a graph containing both physically verified transitions and uncertain MLLM-hypothesized connections. 

C. Foundation Models for Navigation and Object Search 

Pretrained language and vision-language models have in-creasingly been incorporated into robotic navigation and decision-making. Existing approaches connect language in-structions with feasible actions [38], combine language rea-soning with visual navigation [39], transfer web-scale vision-language knowledge to embodied control [40], [41], and use large models to propose semantic subgoals, promising search regions, or topological representations [42]–[45]. These meth-ods demonstrate that foundation models can provide useful high-level guidance without environment-specific training. However, hallucination remains a persistent limitation of both language and multimodal foundation models [46], [47]. In embodied settings, hallucinations can appear as incorrect target-location predictions, non-existent semantic regions, or infeasible spatial connections. The proposed framework there-fore differs from methods that directly translate foundation-model outputs into actions. It treats MLLM outputs as uncer-tain graph hypotheses, revises them using physical observa-tions and graph-context evidence, and delegates coordinated action selection to an explicit optimization model with great generalizability. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION 

We consider cooperative embodied search in partially ob-served, mapless environments, where a team of robots Q

must locate multiple targets K without prior knowledge of the environment topology, semantic regions, traversable connections, or target locations. As illustrated in Figure 1, the proposed framework follows a closed-loop hypothesize– verify–optimize–execute process. At each decision step, the robots acquire local panoramic observations and feasible one-hop actions; an MLLM then uses these observations, the task descriptions, and the accumulated graph context to pro-pose uncertain semantic and topological hypotheses, including target-location scores and possible unobserved connections. These hypotheses are then incorporated into a dynamic graph that distinguishes physically verified edges from ungrounded MLLM-proposed edges. A rolling-horizon MILP selects coor-dinated routes for the team, of which only the first grounded transition is executed before new observations are collected, the graph is updated, and the problem is solved again.

F. Optimization Model Design 

We formulate cooperative multi-agent search as a lexico-graphic Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) that jointly determines which agent should pursue each active target at candidate viewpoints and constructs the corresponding agent routes over the current hypothesis graph. The routing com-ponent follows a network-flow formulation, where binary arc-selection variables and flow-conservation constraints construct an open path for each agent [52]. A lexicographic objective enforces strict priority among optimization criteria, ensuring that the primary target-search objective is optimized before secondary routing costs are considered [53]. We consider multiple agents Q searching for multiple target objects K in a shared hypothesis graph G1: t. All agents share the same graph state, while each agent constructs its own open path from its current viewpoint. The optimization is solved in a rolling-horizon manner: the model plans a full path over the current hypothesis graph, but only the first physically executable transition is implemented before the graph is updated and the problem is solved again. In the optimization model, paths are constructed only over viewpoint nodes because executable navigation is represented by edge connectivity. Semantic regions are retained in the hypothesis graph for viewpoint assignment, semantic context, and target-location reasoning, but they are not routing nodes. Therefore, we define Nt := V1: t as the routing-node set. In particular, each agent’s current viewpoint nq ∈ Nt serves as the fixed origin of its planned route. Since the hypothesis graph stores edges as undirected edges, each edge {i, j } ∈ E1: t is converted into two directed arcs

We evaluate the proposed framework and comparison meth-ods in the Matterport3D Simulator [6]. The evaluation con-sists of 100 test cases drawn from five indoor scans in the Matterport3D dataset [55]. For each scan, target objects are manually selected and annotated with a textual description and the indices of viewpoints whose panoramas contain the target. These visibility annotations are used exclusively by the offline Oracle and for performance evaluation. The test cases span team sizes of {1, 3, 5} agents and target-set sizes of {1, 4, 8} objects, with randomized initial agent locations and target selections. Each online method is run for at most 30 decision steps and uses the same GPT-5.4 model [56], configured with medium reasoning effort, to detect targets from panoramic observations. 

A. Baseline Methods a) Offline Perfect-Knowledge Oracle : The Oracle is given the complete navigation graph, exact edge distances, agent start nodes, and detectable-viewpoint set Di,k for every target k and test case i. It first computes shortest paths among the agent start nodes and all target-detectable viewpoints. An exact per-agent dynamic program then determines the minimum-distance walk associated with each attainable target-coverage set. A second dynamic program combines these per-agent walks to cover all targets while minimizing the total distance traveled by the team. Targets visible from interme-diate nodes along a route are also counted as covered. The Oracle is unavailable to the online methods and provides the performance upper bound. 

b) VLFM-G : VLFM-G is our multi-agent extension of Vision-Language Frontier Maps (VLFM) [51]. It ranks observed, unvisited frontier viewpoints by target-conditioned visual-semantic relevance and coordinates agents over the online grounded partial graph. For each frontier, eight direc-tional panorama views are scored against target descriptions using the BLIP-2 image–text contrastive head [57], then fused with angular-confidence weighting to maintain its semantic value [51]. Let ¯Stv denote the normalized maximum seman-tic value of frontier v over all active targets, and let ¯dtqv 

denote the normalized grounded shortest-path distance from agent q to frontier v. For a nonnegative distance weight β,VLFM-G assigns agents to distinct frontiers by maximizing 

U tqv = ¯Stv − β ¯dtqv using the Hungarian algorithm [58], with dummy assignments representing wait actions. Conflicts in the agents’ first hops are resolved by retaining the highest-utility assignment and reassigning the remaining agents. Only the first grounded hop is executed before the next replanning step. The distance weight β is calibrated and selected using 20 held-out episodes that are disjoint from the test set. 

c) Decentralized No-Sharing Variant (Dec-Graph) :

Dec-Graph is a controlled variant of the proposed frame-work in which agents maintain separate graphs and select actions independently, while retaining the same target detector, hypothesis-update mechanism, and optimization model. Each agent q ∈ Q independently uses MLLM to construct and update a private graph G1: tq from only its own observation history and then solves the single-agent specialization of (26) with Q = {q}. For a fair comparison across methods, the target-found status is the only information shared among agents: whenever an agent detects and claims a target as found, this status is communicated to all other agents, which subsequently also mark that target as found and remove it from their active target sets. Apart from this synchronized target-found status, no graph, observation, hypothesis, route, or action information is exchanged among agents. Consequently, target assignments and first-hop choices are not jointly coor-dinated: different agents may pursue the same active target or simultaneously occupy the same viewpoint while retaining separate observation histories and graph states. Dec-Graph coincides with the proposed method when |Q| = 1 and is therefore reported only for the multi-agent cases.

V. C ONCLUSION 

This paper presented a training-free and uncertainty-aware framework for cooperative multi-agent multi-target embodied search in mapless, partially observed environments. MLLMs are used to generate structured hypotheses over semantic regions, target locations, and navigable connectivity, which are progressively refined in a shared multi-resolution graph. A rolling-horizon MILP then converts these uncertain hypotheses into coordinated search decisions, balancing target discovery, travel efficiency, uncertainty, and repeated exploration. The framework was evaluated on 100 Matterport3D [55] test cases using multiple MLLM backbones [56], [59], [60] and compared with VLFM-G (a multi-agent VLFM [51] adap-tation), Dec-Graph (a decentralized baseline), and a perfect-knowledge Oracle. The results show consistent improvements over the benchmark methods in search progress and travel efficiency, with the advantage becoming more pronounced in multi-agent settings. The target-location results further show that MLLM reasoning quality affects how strongly the predicted probability mass is concentrated near the true targets in geodesic distance, particularly in cooperative search. More-over, comparison with Dec-Graph demonstrates that shared graph information and joint planning substantially improve coordination, especially by balancing workloads and enabling more effective parallel exploration.
