# Red-Teaming of Vision-Language-Action Models via Quality Diversity Environment Generation 

Shihan Zhao 1∗, Henry Chen 1, Ya-Chuan Hsu 1, Minjune Hwang 1, Siddharth Srikanth 12 

Freddie Liang 1, Akanksha Saran 3, Aaquib Tabrez 4†, Stefanos Nikolaidis 1†, Daniel Seita 1†

Abstract — A long-standing goal in robotics is to develop generalist robots that can perform tasks across a wide range of settings, flexibly specified with images and text. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models offer a promising path toward this goal by training on large collections of robot demonstra-tions. However, while VLAs perform well in-distribution, they often fail under minor test-time shifts, such as changes in object positions, lighting, textures, and camera poses. To address this, we propose Quality Diversity for Diverse Environment Generation (Q-DEG), a method for improving VLA robustness by generating adversarial environments and fine-tuning the VLA on its own successful rollouts. Q-DEG uses Quality Diver-sity (QD) optimization to find a set of high-failure environments, while promoting diversity using policy embeddings extracted from the VLA. These environments expose vulnerabilities that can be mitigated through self-imitation fine-tuning, thereby improving the VLA’s robustness. In simulation, we conduct experiments across three representative VLA models, and our results indicate that Q-DEG improves robustness to out-of-distribution (OOD) adversarial environments compared to the base VLA and VLAs fine-tuned using data collected from two red-teaming baselines. Furthermore, we demonstrate that improving VLA robustness through Q-DEG facilitates sim-to-real transfer. Our anonymous project website is at: https: //qd-env.github.io/qdeg/ .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models [1]–[7] have emerged as a promising mode for robot control. By leverag-ing Vision-Language Models (VLMs) pretrained on internet-scale data, VLAs have access to cross-domain visual and se-mantic knowledge, and generalize across multiple tasks and robot embodiments. However, despite strong in-distribution performance, VLAs frequently fail when test scenarios differ from training data in peripheral ways such as object posi-tions, textures and colors, lighting, and camera poses. Prior work has characterized this vulnerability by analyz-ing VLA failure modes in OOD environments [8], [9], but these environments are hand-designed for benchmarking and not designed to elicit failures in a specific VLA and improve its robustness. Fine-tuning VLAs using reinforcement learn-ing objectives can improve their robustness end-to-end [10]– [12], but these methods do not expose model vulnerabilities by identifying failure-inducing environments. Red-teaming methods probe policy-specific weaknesses through genera-tive visual edits [13] and video world model rollouts [14], but

Prior work established Quality Diversity (QD) optimiza-tion as a framework for testing robotic systems by discov-ering diverse scenarios [15]–[19]. We extend QD to VLA environmental red-teaming and propose Quality Diversity for 

Diverse Environment Generation (Q-DEG). Q-DEG searches for environment parameterizations that cause a target VLA to fail frequently while inducing diverse initial policy em-beddings, extracted from the VLA’s pre-action-layer acti-vations. The search prioritizes environments that still yield occasional successful rollouts and caches these trajectories for subsequent self-imitation fine-tuning to improve VLA robustness. Q-DEG organizes the generated environments into a heatmap (Figure 1) that maps policy embeddings to success rates, facilitating analysis of model vulnerabilities. It uses episode-level task success as its performance feedback and policy embeddings for diversity, requiring no action log-probabilities and thus supporting both autoregressive and flow-based VLAs. To summarize, our contributions are: (1) Q-DEG, a method that formulates VLA environmental red-teaming as a Quality Diversity search over environment parameterizations; (2) a self-imitation [20] procedure that improves VLA robustness by fine-tuning on the VLA’s own successful rollouts saved during QD search; and (3) an evaluation of Q-DEG across three VLAs in LIBERO [21], showing advantage over base-lines in environmental diversity, robustness improvement, and sim-to-real transfer. II. R ELATED WORKS 

a) Vision-Language-Action (VLA) Models: VLAs aim to provide general-purpose robot control by mapping visual observations and language instructions directly to robot ac-tions. Examples of such VLAs include OpenVLA [1], the 

π family [2]–[4], SmolVLA [5], and MolmoAct models [6], [7]. These models are trained on broad robot datasets for language-conditioned manipulation. Techniques such as fine-tuning [22] and steering [23] further improve their success rate and adaptability on downstream tasks. However, making VLAs robust remains challenging because even large robot datasets [24], [25] cover only a limited subset of possible ob-ject poses, lighting conditions, camera viewpoints, textures, and scene layouts. Consequently, VLAs remain brittle [8]– [10] under minor environment shifts even when they do not alter the semantic task. b) Quality Diversity Optimization: Quality Diversity (QD) methods search for collections of high-quality solu-tions that differ along specified measures, often by dis-cretizing the measure space into an archive of cells to be filled through iterative evolution [26]. The search efficiency may be improved with covariance matrix adaptation [27], [28], gradient-based optimization [29], and in-context learn-ing [30]. Compared with pure optimization, QD’s diversity incentive makes it well suited to generating scenarios and inputs that elicit diverse responses from a policy. QD has been used to generate scenarios that probe collaborative strategies in multi-agent systems [16] and expose failures in robot-assisted work settings [15], [31]. See [17] for a review of QD-based scenario generation and testing. These works use QD primarily to characterize a policy’s behavior and lim-itations with respect to user-specified diversity measures. In contrast, Q-DEG searches for diverse, challenging scenarios to generate training data for improving a VLA’s robustness, using the VLA’s policy embeddings as diversity measures. Recently, QD has also been applied to instruction red-teaming for foundation models. Rainbow Teaming [18] gen-erates prompts that elicit unsafe responses from an LLM, and Q-DIG [19] generates instructions that describe the task accurately but still cause a VLA to fail. Q-DEG complements these works by extending QD to environmental red-teaming for VLAs, searching over environment parameters that in-duce VLA failures while keeping the instruction fixed. 

c) Environment Design: A complementary line of work develops simulated environments and benchmarks for evalu-ating language-conditioned robot policies. Benchmarks such as LIBERO [21], LIBERO-PRO [9], and RoboCasa [32] provide valuable testbeds for measuring generalization across tasks, scenes, and physical reasoning challenges. However, these benchmarks are mainly designed for evaluation, with environment variations specified by benchmark designers rather than searched to target a VLA’s failure modes, and the resulting failures are not directly converted into fine-tuning data. Other works on unsupervised environment de-sign automatically generate challenging tasks or curricula for reinforcement learning agents [33], [34] but do not target modern VLA policies. Complementary works study geo-metric red-teaming for robot manipulation [35], generative simulation for robot learning [36], and world simulators for policy evaluation [14]. Within this landscape, Q-DEG searches over interpretable simulator parameters, using QD to promote diversity among the VLA policy embeddings induced by the resulting envi-ronments. III. Policy Embeddings: For evaluating an environment’s di-versity , we use π’s pre-action layer activations at the start of each rollout, which we refer to as policy embeddings .Intuitively, policy embeddings represent the VLA’s “first impression” on each environment, and our goal is to diversify policy embeddings so that our adversarial environments induce diverse experience on the VLA. We denote the projected embedding with Aψ + b, with A being the principal component projection matrix and b being the centering offset. See App. I for more details.

If a candidate undergoes MIQP repair, we subtract its edit distance from its CMA-ES feedback to discourage the generation of invalid environments. When CMA-ES stag-nates (i.e. no sampled environment has positive improvement score), we sample an occupied cell from the archive and restart CMA-ES by centering its distribution on the cell’s highest-failure environment parameterization. This resumes QD search from a “stepping stone” environment known to be the most adversarial within an embedding space region. 

D. Self-Imitation Fine-Tuning 

Q-DEG also improves the robustness of the base VLA π

to failure modes uncovered during QD search. It does so by fine-tuning π to imitate its own successful trajectories on generated adversarial environments. Specifically, for each environment in A satisfying 0 < ˆP fail < 1, we retain the shortest successful trajectory among its k rollouts to con-struct the fine-tuning dataset D. Environments with ˆP fail = 0 

are not adversarial, whereas those with ˆP fail = 1 provide no successful trajectory to imitate. We use only the short-est successful trajectory per environment while excluding longer trajectories, as multiple different demonstrations on the same environment can lead to action divergence, which undermines imitation learning [44]. To mitigate forgetting, we add to D 10% of π’s original training data for the default tasks [45]. We then fine-tune π on D using standard behavior cloning. Notably, the environmental diversity promoted by Q-DEG translates into trajectory diversity within D, helping prevent behavior collapse during self-imitation [46]. Alg. 1 summarizes our method. V. B. Simulation Experiments 

We address the first two questions in simulation us-ing LIBERO-Spatial [21], which consists of 10 language-conditioned pick-and-place tasks. We choose LIBERO-Spatial due to its prevalence as a VLA benchmark and its compatibility with our environment parameterizations. To demonstrate Q-DEG’s application toward multiple VLA ar-chitectures, we experiment using two autoregressive models, 

π0-FAST [3] and OpenVLA-OFT [1], [22], and one flow-matching model, π0.5 [4]. 

Evaluating red-teaming: We compare the methods using two metrics computed over the environments they generate under the same VLA evaluation budget ( 3,200 rollouts): (1) the number of feasibly adversarial environments satisfying 

0 < ˆP fail < 1, and (2) the average pairwise DINOv2 embed-ding distance . For the latter, we pass the first-step overview image from each environment through dinov2 vitb14 

and compute the average pairwise cosine distance among the resulting embeddings. We use DINOv2 because its se-mantically structured representation space allows embedding distance to capture visual-semantic dissimilarity [47]. 

Evaluating robustness: Following Sec. IV-D, we fine-tune the base VLA on successful trajectories collected from each method’s rollouts. We train for 10 ,000 steps using behavior cloning with rank-16 LoRA and a batch size of 

32 . For each method, we reserve 20% of its adversarial environments for evaluation and exclude their trajectories from the corresponding fine-tuning dataset. We evaluate the base and fine-tuned VLAs on the default LIBERO-Spatial tasks and the held-out environments from each method’s evaluation set, and report the success rate for every VLA ×

evaluation set combination. Success rates on the 10 LIBERO-Spatial tasks are evaluated over 50 trials each, whereas success rates on held-out environments are evaluated over 

4 trials each due to the large number of environments. 

C. Real-World Experiments 

To investigate whether Q-DEG improves VLA sim-to-real transfer, we construct a real-world test setting in which the VLA controls a Kinova JACO Gen2 arm to either grab the bowl from table center (Task 1) or grab the bowl next to the plate (Task 2). We modify LIBERO to create simulated digi-tal twins of both tasks. To obtain a base VLA with reasonable success rates, we fine-tune the pi05 base checkpoint on 

40 manually collected simulated demonstrations per task. We train for 10 ,000 steps using behavior cloning with a batch size of 32 and rank-16 LoRA. For each method, we then red-team the base VLA in simulation under a budget of 

832 VLA rollouts and fine-tune for another 10 ,000 steps on successful trajectories to improve VLA robustness. The base model is also fine-tuned for another 10 ,000 steps on the original demonstration to even the amount of training. We then evaluate the base and fine-tuned VLAs on the two tasks in the real world, and report success rates over 20 trials on each VLA-task combination. A trial is successful if the robot navigates its gripper around the rim of the correct bowl within 3 minutes. We interleave trials between the two tested VLAs to control biases from external factors [48]. VI. R ESULTS

VII. C ONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

In this work, we present Q-DEG, a method that red-teams VLAs by using Quality Diversity optimization to search for diverse, feasibly adversarial environments, and improves robustness by fine-tuning the VLA on its own successful rollouts in those environments. Across three representative VLA models, Q-DEG finds more diverse adversarial environ-ments than baselines and improves VLA robustness to OOD environments in both simulated and real-world manipulation tasks, outperforming the base VLA and VLAs fine-tuned on baseline data. A limitation with Q-DEG is that it fine-tunes only on successful rollouts, and does not use failed trajectories despite their information about actions to avoid. Future work could incorporate these failures by forming pairs of successful and failed trajectories for preference learning methods [49]. A second limitation is that Q-DEG uses PCA to reduce the dimensionality of policy embeddings before the discretization-based CMA-MAE search, potentially dis-carding task-relevant information. Recent discretization-free QD algorithms, such as Soft QD [43], could obviate this dimensionality reduction and enable diversification in full embedding space. APPENDIX IPOLICY EMBEDDING EXTRACTION AND COMPRESSION 

We extract embeddings from π0-FAST, OpenVLA-OFT, and π0.5 as follows: 

π0-FAST: During autoregressive action decoding, the final transformer block preceding the action head in π0-FAST produces a 2048 -dimensional hidden state at each FAST-token decoding step. We average the hidden states across all decoding steps prior to the EOS token, yielding a 2048 -dimensional policy embedding. 

OpenVLA-OFT: The final transformer block preceding the action head outputs hidden states of shape Rda×dc×4096 ,where da and dc denote the action dimension and action chunk size. We average over the first two dimensions to obtain a 4096 -dimensional policy embedding. 

π0.5 : Unlike the π0-FAST and OpenVLA-OFT, π0.5 incor-porates robot state information as part of its action expert during conditional flow-matching. To ensure that the policy embeddings capture all information relevant to action gener-ation, we extract activations from the final layer before ve-locity prediction at the first Euler integration step, yielding a 

1024 -dimensional policy embedding. Because flow-matching is initialized from a standard Gaussian source distribution, the resulting activations can exhibit stochastic variation un-related to the observation. To obtain more consistent embed-dings, we perform a separate flow evaluation initialized from the zero vector rather than a Gaussian sample. This auxiliary evaluation is used solely for embedding extraction and does not affect the actions generated by the policy. Discretizing the high-dimensional raw policy embedding space would require a prohibitively large number of QD archive cells to achieve adequate coverage [42], [43]. Follow-ing prior work [50], we therefore fit PCA to the raw policy embeddings and project them onto the low-dimensional subspace spanned by the top M = 2 principal compo-nents. Although this projection discards substantial infor-mation, we find that the first two principal components consistently capture a large fraction of the variation among policy embeddings collected from sampled environments
