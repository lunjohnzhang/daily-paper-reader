## ABSTRACT 

Large-scale applications of lifelong multi-agent path finding (LMAPF) call for computationally inexpensive movement decisions under changing traffic condi-tions and across varied layouts. We propose a navigation policy in which a con-volutional network computes a shared spatial field over the map once per timestep and a compact goal-conditioned operator derives agents’ movement preferences. The complete policy is trained end-to-end using average-reward reinforcement learning with throughput as the reward, without planner demonstrations or a sep-arate online path planner. For structured and unstructured layouts, we train one policy per class on mixtures of small maps and deploy each without retraining on larger, unseen layouts and agent populations. Across six layouts with 10 ,000 

agents, our policies achieve throughput comparable to or higher than the strongest evaluated baselines. The proposed method has an average execution time below 10 ms per step, more than an order of magnitude faster than several established planners. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Lifelong multi-agent path finding (LMAPF) coordinates collision-free movement of agents that re-ceive new goals after completing their current ones. Throughput is the average number of goals completed per timestep (Ma et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021). Applications include large robotic logistics systems that continually transport goods and real-time strategy games in which many units move to-ward changing objectives. Large populations increase the computation needed to update movement decisions, while real-time applications limit the time available for each update (Silver, 2005). Reuse across facilities or game maps also requires handling varied obstacle and passage geometry (Stern et al., 2019). We therefore seek policies that maintain throughput across layouts and populations with low per-step computation cost. Existing methods use different forms of coordination within this computation limit. RHCR solves windowed planning problems (Li et al., 2021), while WPPL refines windowed plans within an avail-able time budget (Jiang et al., 2024). PIBT resolves conflicts through local priority rules (Okumura et al., 2022); Traffic Flow Optimisation adds congestion-aware guide paths (Chen et al., 2024). Learned policies can replace part of this online planning. SILLM combines per-agent neural infer-ence, spatial communication and global guidance to coordinate 10 ,000 agents (Jiang et al., 2025). When agents’ local observations overlap, per-agent neural inference can repeatedly process the same map and traffic information. We use a shared spatial field to reuse this computation across agents. A convolutional network processes the map and current traffic once per timestep, producing scores for movement and waiting at each location. Agents use these shared scores in a goal-conditioned route computation. Neural computation is performed over the map, while each agent’s route computation is limited to a fixed number of departures (Fig. 2). A soft shortest-path distribution derived from the map and each agent’s goal supplies a geometric reference that favours shorter routes while retaining alternatives. ## 3 APPROACH 

The centralised policy computes a shared spatial field, goal-conditioned route probabilities and a destination allocation (Fig. 2). A convolutional network processes the scene once per timestep. The route operator uses its output and each agent’s goals to compute action probabilities, which allocation adjusts for competing demand for cells. All learned parameters are shared across agents; their number is independent of population size and map dimensions. Appendix A.6 specifies the example in Figure 2. 3.1 SHARED SPATIAL FIELD 

Let Ot = O(st) be an aggregate spatial representation of the full state st, containing map geometry, agent positions and previous moves, current and queued goal information, and aggregates of refer-ence routes. The convolutional network WθW computes the shared field Φt = WθW (Ot) once per 3Preprint. Under review. timestep. Its outputs supply scores for movement and waiting. For a continuation of H departures, 

h ∈ { 0, . . . , H − 1} indexes the departure depth. A base action-score vector is shared across depths, with additive corrections for later departures. Scores enter the route probabilities exponentially, so higher accumulated scores favour the corresponding continuations. All depths use the current scene; the field is refreshed at the next timestep. The network has 39,615 parameters, with six further scalars in the route operator and allocation. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate throughput and per-step execution cost on unseen layouts, then examine transfer as agent populations change. We use the structured and unstructured policies from Section 3.4, with the terminal averaged checkpoint from each of three training seeds. Unless stated otherwise, results average cases within each seed and then weight the seed means equally. The six-layout benchmark uses 10 ,000 agents, occupying 17–26% of free cells. Throughput counts the entire episode; map descriptions, case counts and rollout lengths appear in Appendix C. Instances, task streams and rollout lengths follow SILLM’s released evaluation protocol for every method. 

Baselines. SILLM (Jiang et al., 2025) combines learned action preferences and PIBT with shortest-distance heuristics (BD), fixed directional edge costs (SG), or TrafficFlow’s congestion-aware guide paths (DG). OnlineGGO (Zang et al., 2025) instead learns traffic-dependent edge costs for PIBT. We use publicly released checkpoints for SILLM and Follower (Skrynnik et al., 2024a), and train OnlineGGO once per configuration following its released recipe. For our policies, we re-port variability across three independent training seeds per class. Table 1 summarises training and reuse. Non-learned baselines comprise PIBT, RHCR/WPPL, LLLG (Arita & Okumura, 2026) and TrafficFlow (Chen et al., 2024). All entries in Table 2 are reproduced measurements. ## 6 RELATED WORK 

Large-scale lifelong MAPF. Planning methods balance coordination over future steps against the computation available for each decision. RHCR solves windowed MAPF problems and reports lifelong experiments with up to 1000 agents (Li et al., 2021). PIBT uses priority inheritance and backtracking for single-step coordination, with runtime experiments reaching 10 ,000 agents (Oku-mura et al., 2022). WPPL refines PIBT-initialized plans using large-neighborhood search and eval-uates 10 ,000 agents in lifelong MAPF with rotations (Jiang et al., 2024). Traffic Flow Optimi-sation supplies congestion-aware guide paths to PIBT, reporting lifelong experiments with 12 ,000 

agents (Chen et al., 2024). LLLG combines local guidance with LaCAM in a receding-horizon planner, evaluated with up to 10 ,000 agents (Arita & Okumura, 2026). PRIMAL and PRIMAL2 combine reinforcement and imitation learning, reporting evaluations with up to 1024 agents in one-shot MAPF and 2048 in lifelong MAPF, respectively (Sartoretti et al., 2019; Damani et al., 2021). PRIMAL2 introduces corridor observations and movement conventions. Fol-lower learns to follow planned paths and reports lifelong evaluations with up to 256 agents (Skrynnik et al., 2024a). SILLM combines imitation from windowed plan refinement, spatial communica-tion, global guidance and PIBT-style collision resolution, with population sweeps reaching 12 ,000 

agents (Jiang et al., 2025). 

Spatial guidance and shared representations. Spatial guidance and our shared field both as-sociate movement preferences with map locations or directed moves. Direction maps (Jansen & Sturtevant, 2008) and highways (Cohen et al., 2015) encode preferred traffic directions. GGO op-timizes guidance graphs, including through convolutional update models, with lifelong evaluations reaching 6000 agents (Zhang et al., 2024). Online GGO adapts their costs to recent traffic and reports 

12 ,000 -agent evaluations with guided PIBT (Zang et al., 2025). These costs are used by planners or guide-path calculations. SJRL learns environment guidance and communicating subgoal poli-cies, with Causal PIBT resolving subgoal conflicts; its lifelong experiments with rotations reach 320 agents (Jiang et al., 2026). RAILGUN predicts per-cell action distributions with a map-based con-volutional policy trained on planner-generated trajectories, with reported city-tile experiments using up to 256 agents (Tang et al., 2025). 

Differentiable allocation. Sinkhorn layers provide continuous relaxations for learning latent per-mutations (Mena et al., 2018). Sinkhorn Policy Gradient uses such a layer in an actor–critic policy over permutations (Emami & Ranka, 2018). Here, sparse allocation coordinates next-move propos-als within a navigation policy trained from throughput. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We presented a reinforcement learning approach to large-scale LMAPF that learns traffic guidance as part of an end-to-end navigation policy. A shared spatial field guides goal-conditioned route choices, and differentiable destination allocation coordinates competing moves. The policy is trained directly from throughput without planner demonstrations and executes without a separate online path plan-ner. One policy per layout class, trained on small-map mixtures, transfers to larger, unseen layouts and populations. Across six benchmark layouts with 10 ,000 agents, the policies attain throughput comparable to or higher than the strongest evaluated baselines, with execution below 10 ms per step. These results show that reinforcement learning from throughput can yield fast, transferable policies competitive with planning and imitation-learning approaches. AI USE STATEMENT

Destination allocation supplies the proposal probabilities πi used for action selection (Section 3.3). Accelerated updates change how these probabilities are computed; iterative action selection reuses them to reconsider moves cancelled by conflicts. Both operate at evaluation with the same learned field, route operator and trained parameters. 

Accelerated updates. Destination allocation adjusts the route probabilities qi for competing de-mand for cells (Section 3.3). In each of its two passes, plain Sinkhorn iterations alternate row normalisation, which assigns unit mass to each agent, and destination normalisation, which bal-ances demand against unit cell capacity (Appendix A.5). We accelerate these updates at evaluation with temperature scaling (Schmitzer, 2019) and over-relaxation (Thibault et al., 2021). Tempera-ture scaling begins with a higher entropy weight and gradually returns to the learned temperature 

τp; over-relaxation extrapolates each ordinary potential update. For an allocation pass with K ≥ 2

iterations, iteration k ∈ { 0, . . . , K − 1} uses 

τp,k = τp κmax {1−k/ ⌊K/ 2⌋, 0}, fi ← (1 − ω)fi + ω efi. (27) Here κ ≥ 1 is the initial-temperature multiplier and ω is the relaxation factor. We fix κ = 8 and 

ω = 1 .6 in these evaluations; the temperature reaches τp halfway through each pass and remains there. The value efi is the ordinary row update in Eq. (23), evaluated at τp,k ; the same relaxation applies to the slack potential fd and destination potentials gx. Row and slack potentials are updated before computing the destination update. Both allocation passes start from zero potentials and use this schedule; the reverse-edge repricing rule is unchanged. Setting κ = ω = 1 recovers plain scaling. At the final temperature τp, the relaxed updates have the same fixed-point equations as plain scaling. Learned parameters remain fixed, and all checkpoints were trained with 48 plain Sinkhorn iterations per pass. 

Iterative action selection. At deployment, each agent proposes its most probable action under 

πi (Section 3.3). POGEMA’s movement rules (Appendix C) cancel conflicting moves to WAIT. Iterative action selection lets agents whose moves were cancelled reconsider alternatives using the already computed allocation probabilities. First apply the movement rules provisionally and reserve every assigned next cell, including each waiting agent’s current cell. In each round, agents whose original moves were cancelled and who still wait choose, in parallel, their highest-scoring action among WAIT and valid moves to unassigned cells. Comparisons use the fixed logits in Eq. (25) and the original action order for ties. For each proposed non-WAIT destination, the lowest-index proposer is accepted; its destination stays reserved and its current cell becomes available in the next round. This preserves feasibility while permanently adding moves. Repeat until a round accepts no additional move, then advance the simulator once 23 Preprint. Under review. with the resulting joint proposal. The learned field, route probabilities and Sinkhorn allocation are each computed only once per timestep. In the long-horizon comparison of Figure 8, iterative selection changes mean throughput by less than 0.5% on warehouse, sortation and both random layouts, while improving it on Berlin and Paris.

We identify all 172 narrow passages from the walls alone: 84 horizontal passages with three interior cells and 88 vertical passages with two. For neighbouring occupied cells, let qi(j) be agent i’s probability of moving into agent j’s cell after route reweighting and before allocation. Opposing demand is the mean of qi(j)qj (i) over occupied internal passage edges and time steps. We first compute this rate within each case, then average equally over the eight cases and three training runs. It measures incompatible route preferences, not executed collisions; outward movement by both agents is valid.
