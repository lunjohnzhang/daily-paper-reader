# Persistent LLM Orchestration of Heterogeneous Robot Teams via Partial Delegation to Multi-Agent Protocols 

Maria G. Mendoza 1,∗, Victoria M. Tuck 2,∗, Zachary Ravichandran 2, Shankar Sastry 1, and George J. Pappas 2

Abstract — Natural language specifications let non-expert users task robot teams, and large language models (LLMs) have recently proven effective at grounding such semantic specifica-tions into plans for heterogeneous teams. Existing approaches assume missions that terminate and environments that, while unknown, do not change, but both assumptions fail in many emerging applications. For example, a disaster-response mission does not terminate, and its environment changes continuously as hazards spread and evacuees move. We identify three ways an LLM orchestrator breaks in this setting: its prompt grows without bound, its reasoning degrades as agents and targets accumulate, and it stops actively monitoring after covering the search space once. We build on the successful framework of grounded generative intelligence, and address each issue with a classical idea. A Markovian state summary, rebuilt from the semantic map at every invocation, replaces the growing interaction history. Partial delegation lets the orchestrator assign a subset of robots to an autonomous multi-agent protocol and reserve its reasoning for generating subtasks that require detailed parameters and careful ordering. Lastly, observation staleness, surfaced explicitly in the prompt, together with task-and event-triggered re-prompting, keeps the team vigilant. In a simulated urban search-and-rescue mission with spreading fire and moving evacuees, our framework rescues 98% of civilians versus 89% for the strongest baseline while using six times fewer tokens. Additional ablations isolate the contribution of each mechanism. 

I. INTRODUCTION Multi-robot approaches offer several advantages over single-robot systems: they scale to larger environments, provide redundancy against individual failures, and let sim-pler robots combine for complex tasks. These properties are useful across a range of application domains including infrastructure maintenance, healthcare robotics, and disaster response, where specifically the danger of the scenario makes offloading tasks to robots especially valuable. Consider evac-uation during a wildfire: rapid response is required, resources are limited, the environmental state changes continuously, humans may act irrationally, and, critically, the mission has no terminal “done” signal—it is continuous. The first respon-ders directing the team are not roboticists, and their needed tasks cannot be enumerated in advance, For example, ”check whether anyone is still on Main Street” is not a coverage objective or a fixed task list. These considerations lead to our motivating question: 

For continuous, semantic missions in unknown, time-varying, and time-critical environments, how do we efficiently and persistently coordinate a heterogeneous team of robots? 

Our answer is to let the LLM orchestrate both robots and algorithms. We build on prior work in grounded LLM-based task planning and allocation for heterogeneous teams [3], and introduce three ideas aimed at efficiency and persistence. First, the orchestrator is provided a Markovian summary of the state of the system and its tasks, updated from the map at every iteration, instead of the typical full history so that prompt size does not grow unbounded with time. Second, through partial delegation the orchestrator assigns a subset of robots to an autonomous multi-agent protocol such as ergodic coverage [6], which runs continuously without further prompting, allowing the LLM to reserve reasoning for the semantically challenging tasks of subtask generation, parameter choice, and determining task precedence. Third, to invoke vigilance in the time-varying environment, the Markovian state exposes observation staleness explicitly, and the orchestrator is re-invoked on task completion, task failure, and relevant map changes.

II. RELATED WORKS 

Multi-robot coordination in time-varying environments 

Effectively coordinating multi-robot teams in dynamic envi-ronments is an outstanding challenge. While there is exten-sive work on dynamic multi-robot task allocation [4]–[6], the combined problem of dynamic task arrival and time-varying environments is limited. Along with the dynamic nature of the environment, other factors may be considered such as uncertainty, temporal constraints in the mission specification, or payload constraints [7]–[10]. 

LLMs for Multi-Robot Coordination. Recent work uses LLMs to translate natural-language mission specifications into multi-robot coordination. Some approaches use LLMs to frame a reinforcement learning problem that solves some mission specification [11]. Other approaches directly use the LLM as a high-level planner by providing the model with a set of high-level robot behaviors (e.g., for navi-gation, manipulation), and then tasking it with providing a plan for each robot [1], [12]. Such approaches may operate in a closed-loop manner, where plans are updated as robots gather feedback [13]. The LLM-as-coordinator approach has also been adopted for heterogeneous robot teams, which require the LLM to reason about robot-specific capabilities [14], [15]. One challenge of using LLMs in-the-loop for planning is their tendency to hallucinate. Several works address this challenge with architectures that combine LLMs with non-learned components, such as optimization-based task allocation or conformal prediction for uncertainty quantification [16]. These approaches ground LLM outputs in verifiable representations, which all require plans to be vetted before execution [3], [17]–[19]. Moreover, the exist-ing literature suggests that centralized planning approaches exhibit better scaling properties in complex environments, as compared to decentralized approaches [3], [13], [14], [19]. 

Closed-loop planning with LLMs Effectively incorporating feedback is crucial when planning in dynamic and time-varying environments [20]. Event-driven planning mecha-nisms, wherein robots provide feedback from onboard per-ception on task execution to a central LLM planner, have been proposed in [21]. Semantic graph updates have also been shown to be an effective source of feedback for planning in partially known environments [22]. PROBLEM DESCRIPTION We focus on the problem of multi-robot task planning and allocation for a continuous mission in a time-varying environment. An LLM planner has access to M robots with possibly heterogeneous types and a semantic description of the mission. Each robot type has a set of capabilities, and some or all capabilities may overlap between different robot types. The environment may have regions that are restricted to certain robot types, such as a dense forest that cannot be traversed by a ground robot but can be viewed by an aerial vehicle. We assume the LLM knows the bounds of the environment but little about the structure or contents, time-varying or static. The LLM planner is provided with textual descriptions for a set of task primitives that can be assigned to agents as well as for a set of multi-robot protocols to which it can delegate a portion, all, or none of the robots. Each of the task primitives and protocols requires specific robot capabilities. For feasibility, we assume each of the regions can be viewed by at least one robot with sensing capabilities. IV. B. Shared Semantic Map 

We model the world via a topological graph-based se-mantic map where nodes are regions or objects and edges are connections between regions denoting traversability or a connection between a region and an object denoting the existence of the object within that region. Each region is tagged with the time it was last observed. Each traversal edge includes a set of robot capabilities that allow traversal. The planner and agents have access to a partial initial semantic map called so because the objects and regions can carry semantic information such as ”the tree is in the path”. During operation, this semantic map may not always be accurate due to the addition of noisy observations.

D. Subtask Generation 

A foundation model, the orchestrator , decides at each invocation how many and which robots to delegate to a multi-agent protocol and which subtasks the remaining robots should complete. Note that allocations are not done by the orchestrator directly and will instead be allocated algorithmi-cally. The prompt provided to the orchestrator has a structure as detailed below; the prompt contains a pre-determined, fixed portion and a variable portion that changes with each invocation. As part of the prompt, the orchestrator is asked to a) return the set of subtasks as well as their dependencies in a directed acyclic graph (DAG) structure and b) employ Chain-of-Thought (COT) reasoning; these requirements, as well as the final validation and feedback step, are motivated by the approach of [3]. 

Prompt structure. Each prompt combines fixed instruc-tions and the key information of the current state. The fixed instructions contain: a mission description in natural lan-guage; objectives, including competing priorities and rules; a team specification of each robot’s platform and capabilities; task primitives with their required arguments; multi-agent protocols and their descriptions; rules for grounding subtasks in the map and reasoning about a changing environment; and an output schema requiring the orchestrator to return its reasoning, the robots assigned to protocols, the subtasks grounded in map regions, and their dependency graph. The state is rebuilt at every invocation and contains: the semantic map, a short description of each region’s relevant contents built from the team’s observations, its time of last observation, and a summary of observation age across the map; robot state (location, role, current task); task failures since the previous invocation; and critical information like pending tasks, the number of unobserved regions, and stal-eness statistics (latest visit times, average data age, and the oldest-visited regions). Each prompt is therefore self-contained, and it does not grow by the accumulation of invocation history. 

DAG structure. The orchestrator returns a list of robots delegated to protocols and a set of subtasks Tk. Each subtask is a task primitive grounded in regions of the semantic map, and edges of a returned directed acyclic graph encode task precedence. 

Chain-of-thought reasoning. The output schema requires the orchestrator to write its reasoning before its decisions: which regions are relevant, pending tasks, and the reason behind the chosen role split and subtasks follow. 

Validation and feedback. Each subtask is parsed and checked against the task primitives and the current map: the primitive must exist, its arguments must match, and every region it names must exist in the semantic map. The dependency graph is checked for references to rejected, undefined subtasks, and cycles. Rejected outputs are returned to the orchestrator with a reasoning, allowing the planner to try again, up to a fixed number of attempts.

F. Task and Event-Triggered LLM Prompting 

The orchestrator is re-invoked on several conditions, so that a plan written under partial observability stays valid in a time-varying environment. The signals are described below. 

1) Task Failure: A robot cannot complete an assignment. 

2) Change in Semantic Map: The semantic map changes for an object of interest, such as new evacuees appearing for search-and-rescue. The user declares which kinds of objects are relevant and which events should trigger a response. 

3) Task Completion: All robots have finished their sub-tasks. This allows for allocation of a pending subtask or re-delegation to multi-agent protocols.

EXPERIMENTAL SETUP We evaluate the framework in simulated search-and-rescue scenarios for disaster response, modeling spreading hazards, groups of people who must be evacuated, congestion, and blocked areas. Fire ignites regions randomly and spreads to neighboring regions over time. Blocked and burning regions cannot be entered by ground robots, and congestion temporarily slows them. However, aerial vehicles can view these areas from above, creating a heterogeneous mix of capabilities and information asymmetry. Groups of people appear, move along the street network, and must be escorted to a safe zone before a deadline or fire reaches them. We describe the setup of our experiments and comparisons below; results are discussed in the next section, Sec. VI. To test the high-level planning capabilities in complex missions with hazard spread, large search region, and robot team size, we test this in simulation only. Prior work suggests this method can bridge the sim-to-real gap [3]; we defer such a study to future work.

A. Comparison to Baselines 

We compare against two approaches with similar mission profile and architecture, SPINE-HT [3], which our frame-work builds on, and CoMuRoS [21]. While both provide examples of search and rescue operations, neither oper-ates continuously in a time-varying setting. Our larger but physics-extracted setting does not connect to ROS as these methods are implemented for, so we do not directly use their code and instead port the mechanisms that distinguish them from our approach: history representation, invocation (trigger) mechanism, and the absence of multi-agent proto-cols. An additional exploration task primitive is provided that allows the LLM to specify a sequence of regions to explore. CoMuRos also has capabilities such as LLM-determined relevance of observations and a direct chat interface, which we remove for direct comparison in this scenario. CoMuRoS takes a hierarchical approach in which a cen-tralized Task Manager LLM allocates subtasks, and each

In [21], an event callback is triggered by an observation that is determined by this robot’s local LLM to be relevant to the mission. Relevant events, task failures, and human instructions trigger replanning with the chat history supplied as context. This combination is ineffective in our setting where per-sistent search is necessary. Qualitatively, we see in the runs that CoMuRoS often chooses to allocate the faster aerial vehicles to any needed escort, temporarily neglecting exploration in favor of lower rescue time. While event-based triggering is faster at handling change, unexpected scenarios, and new data, it lacks the opportunity that switching on task completion provides for role adjustment. This greedy action devolves into a system that never updates because it is not proactively searching for relevant changes. In the Chain-of-thought rea-soning, we consistently see it state that no exploration is needed because all regions have been observed once. Even with the alternative prompt “There is an active fire spreading in the area. Humans are evacuating their houses and need to be escorted to the triage point.”, performance was still poor. 

B. Ablation on Use of Multi-Agent Protocols 

The purpose of providing access to multi-agent protocols is to allow the LLM to delegate some agents to a well-defined group task that can operate autonomously, potentially continuously, freeing up LLM reasoning to focus on fewer tasks and agents. LLMs have been shown to degrade in capabilities when overloaded with too much information, requiring mechanisms to filter relevant information [1], so this approach allows for more resources to be allocated to the semantically challenging pieces of the mission that the LLM is more specialized to handle. C. Impact of Structure and Frequency of Prompting 

In this set of analyses, we study how to adjust the prompt and prompt timing to improve outcomes. In previous work, the full prompt history or a sliding window [1] is provided for context at each iteration. The length of the full history increases with call number, which loosely correlates with time step, and a sliding window risks leaving out relevant information. We show reduced token usage by instead providing a summary of history without degrading performance. It has been successful because we can represent the system with a Markovian state representation. For the same triggering scheme, ”Summ.” uses much fewer tokens than ”Full” with similar or better levels of success (see Fig. 6). Additionally, the LLM struggles to infer the importance of revisiting regions or collecting new data in the time-varying setting. We find that after providing observation statistics including average age and oldest region observations, the LLM does decide to continue exploring. Fig. 7 shows a case study of this impact. D. Effect of Full History vs State Summary 

We observed that appending the full history costs 1.16 e6

tokens per run with a rescue success of 83 .1% ; summarizing using our approach reduces the cost to 0.41 e6 with a much higher success at 98 .1% (Table III). We have previously discussed how a longer context may confuse instead of inform, which is supported by this data. Fig. 6 shows a further breakdown of token distribution. With full history the run is dominated by uncached input whereas without history, specifically the ”thinking” portion of output tokens dominates, and thinking is proportionally more of the no history output usage. We interpret this switch as the orchestrator using more of its budget on reasoning about the mission instead of just re-reading the prompt. 

E. Model Variations 

All base experiments use Sonnet 5.0. To test whether the architecture can work across multiple models, we run our approach using GPT-5.6 Luna and GPT-5.6 Terra, holding scenario, seed, mission, and prompt fixed. We see a small degradation with Luna with percent success still above 90%. All three stay within one standard deviation of each other on time to rescue (Table III), suggesting compatibility across models with reasonable reasoning capabilities. VII. CONCLUSION In this work, we introduce a framework for multi-agent orchestration in time-varying and continuous settings that leverages the advanced capabilities of grounded generative intelligence. Our approach improves scalability by manag-ing prompt length and allowing the LLM to delegate to autonomous multi-agent protocols, freeing up reasoning. We incentivize persistent operation by improved re-prompting and feedback strategies. Together, these methods reduce to-ken usage while improving the number of successful rescues and rescue speed in a search-and-rescue scenario. Opportu-nities for future work include orchestration using a distilled model as large, online models may not be available when infrastructure access is limited due to damage from a disaster. Locally centralized operation as a mix between our central-ized approach and decentralized multi-agent algorithms may be necessary to scale to large distance with short-range robot communication. Finally, supporting human-provided input and mid-mission changes would broaden practical use.
