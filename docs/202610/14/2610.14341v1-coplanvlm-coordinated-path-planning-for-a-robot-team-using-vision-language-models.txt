# CoPlanVLM: Coordinated Path Planning for a Robot Team using Vision Language Models 

David R. Martin 1, Jared Paquet 2, Mohammad Abdullah Al Faruque 1, Pramod Khargonekar 1,Kyle Volle 3, J. Humberto Ramos 2, and Scott Nivison 4

Abstract — We present CoPlanVLM , which uses a Vision Language Model (VLM) to translate a single natural-language instruction and image into coordinated paths for a team of two robots. While VLMs offer a flexible, natural-language interface for commanding robot teams, their weak spatial reasoning has greatly limited VLM-based path planning. CoPlanVLM im-proves the VLM’s spatial reasoning and path planning abilities through three components: (1) an image overlay marking both free space and obstacles, (2) task classification that selects the most appropriate low-level planner and reasoning prompt for each instruction, and (3) task-specific chain-of-thought reason-ing that prompts the VLM to identify key objects, allocate roles, and verify routes are collision-free before committing to a plan. Together, these components enable the team to complete complex coordinated maneuvers requiring both robots and divide large tasks between robots to increase efficiency. 

CoPlanVLM also supports detailed maneuvers involving specific routes, loops, and coverage of large areas, as well as tasks requiring directional reasoning such as north/south or left/right. We validate CoPlanVLM in both Gazebo simulation and on physical Turtlebot 4 hardware. CoPlanVLM attains an average success rate of 84.4% compared to 50.0% for the closest baseline. On trials where both methods succeed, CoPlanVLM ’s paths are 27% shorter on average. Project code is available at 

github.com/AICPS/CoPlanVLM .

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Multi-robot teams have shown great promise in diverse ap-plication domains such as warehousing [1], UAV swarms [2], and household tasks [3]. Through collaboration, teams are able to complete complex missions which may be impossible for individuals [3]–[5]. However, planning for a collaborative team presents a significant challenge that requires under-standing mission intent, allocating roles, and planning each robot’s motion. Large Language Models (LLMs) have been proposed to address these challenges by enabling robotic systems to interpret complex human instructions, coordinate multiple robots, and adapt to diverse contexts [6]. Vision Language Models (VLMs) can additionally use camera im-ages from the robots or workspace as context to inform planning. LLMs and VLMs have been proposed to generate high-level team plans [3], [4], and navigate individual robots directly from natural-language instructions [7]–[9].

However, VLMs often exhibit poor spatial reasoning [10], which limits their ability to generate long-horizon, collision-free paths. Consequently, many approaches do not construct paths with the VLM directly, instead using it to identify goal locations and/or regions to avoid, which form a cost map over which a classical planner routes to the target [8], [11], [12]. While this reduces reliance on VLM spatial reasoning, it reduces the problem to reaching a goal at lowest cost, and struggles to express more complex behaviors. Many approaches to Vision Language Navigation repeatedly query a VLM for the next action [7], [9], [13], [14], or use Vision-Language-Action models trained for next-action prediction [15], [16]. However, these methods do not produce full long-horizon plans and require frequent querying of large models. The authors of [7] and [17] plan paths by prompting a VLM to select ordered waypoints, but both target single robots, with [7] navigating only to a predefined destination and [17] providing no mechanism to ensure obstacle-free paths. To our knowledge, no prior work generates long-horizon paths for a collaborative team directly from natural-language instructions and supports complex maneuvers be-yond point-to-point navigation. 

To address this gap, this work introduces CoPlanVLM ,which leverages a commercially available VLM to plan coordinated maneuvers for a collaborative team of two robots following a single natural-language instruction. 

It is tested on tasks explicitly requiring both robots (e.g. approaching the same target from two different directions), tasks where coordination improves efficiency (e.g. dividing a region to patrol efficiently), and tasks requiring no coor-dination. Additionally, CoPlanVLM enables the performance of detailed maneuvers involving specific routes, loops, and surveying large areas. Experimental results in both simula-tion and real-world scenarios demonstrate that CoPlanVLM 

attains an average success rate of 84.4% compared to 50.0% for the closest baseline [7], and on trials where both methods succeed, CoPlanVLM ’s paths are 27% shorter on average. A. LLM Task Planning For Robot Teams 

Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are increasingly proposed for collaborative robotic systems due to their ability to interpret natural-language instructions and generalize across a wide variety of tasks [6]. A common paradigm employs a centralized planner LLM that decomposes a mission and assigns natural-language subgoals to individual robots, each equipped with a local LLM that reasons about how to accomplish its assigned subtask [3], [4], [18]. However, coordinated path planning using language models remains largely underexplored. The authors in [19] and [20] employ a VLM to coordinate a robot team’s exploration of an unknown region in search of a target object, but the VLM only selects the most promising frontier to explore next, limiting the system’s ability to generalize to other tasks. The authors in [17] use a VLM to plan a path for a single robot, but the proposed method does little to ensure path feasibility and does not coordinate its actions with any other robots. CoPlanVLM expands the generalizability of language models to long-horizon coordinated path planning for a team of two robots. 

B. Vision Language Navigation 

Vision-Language Navigation (VLN) is the problem of converting a free-form natural-language instruction into a sequence of movement decisions that produce a matching path [21]. One common approach repeatedly queries a VLM for the next action the robot should take [7], [9], [13], [14], and more recently, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have been trained to output navigation commands directly [15], [16]. However, both strategies produce only the immediate next action rather than a long-horizon plan, which hinders coordination between robots and limits their ability to execute long, complex maneuvers. Another common approach uses a VLM to identify target locations and regions to avoid, producing a cost map over which a classical planner computes a path [8], [11], [12]. This yields efficient, long-horizon paths, but a cost-map planner always returns the shortest, lowest-cost route to the goal, which limits the system to instructions whose solution is simply the most direct path. It cannot express behaviors where the route itself matters, such as patrolling a region, looping around an object, or passing on a specified side of an obstacle (e.g., staying to the right of a rack of boxes). In this work, the VLM instead builds a complete route by selecting as many intermediate waypoints as the task requires, enabling these more complex behaviors.

The overall system architecture is depicted in Figure 2. The overhead image is first preprocessed with CLIPSeg [22] to produce an occupancy map and a marked image for prompting the VLM. A classifier LLM then categorizes the instruction and selects the corresponding VLM prompt and low-level planner. A. Image Pre-processing 

The overhead image is preprocessed by CLIPSeg [22] to detect obstacles and construct both an occupancy map and a marked image to prompt the VLM. 

1) Semantic Segmentation: CLIPSeg [22] is a vision-language model that uses CLIP as a backbone to perform prompt-based image segmentation. Given an image and a text prompt p, CLIPSeg produces a dense segmentation score C(q, p ) for each pixel q, indicating the degree to which that pixel corresponds to the concept described by 

p. CoPlanVLM is provided a set of traversable prompts Ptrav 

describing navigable terrain, and any pixel whose confidence falls below 0.50 for all such prompts is conservatively treated as untraversable. In this work, all testing is in indoor environments, motivating the use of a single traversable prompt, Ptrav = {“the floor” }. Any pixel with confidence at least 0.50 for “the floor” is treated as traversable and all others as untraversable. 

2) Occupancy Map Construction: To construct an oc-cupancy grid for planning, each CLIPSeg pixel coordinate 

(px, p y ) is converted to a world position (x, y ) using a pinhole model of the overhead camera together with its intrinsics and extrinsics. Assuming a flat floor at constant height h below the camera, the projection is 

x = (px − cx) hfx

, y = − (py − cy ) hfy

, (2) where (fx, f y ) are the camera’s focal lengths, (cx, c y ) is the principal point, and h is the height of the camera above the floor. The projected obstacle locations form an occupancy map at 0.05 m resolution (Figure 3, Top), to which several corrections are applied. Because CLIPSeg is unreliable near the image boundary, detections within 65 pixels of the edge are discarded, obstacles are inflated by 0.45 m for the robot footprint, and cells within 0.25 m of a map edge are marked untraversable. To prevent robots from being detected as obstacles, any detections within a 0.65 m radius ( 0.2 m robot radius + 0.45 m obstacle inflation radius) of each robot’s pose are cleared before inflating the obstacles.

3) Mark Overlay Construction: A mark overlay (Figure 3, Bottom) is constructed by superimposing an 8 × 14 grid of labeled points onto the image. Each point is assigned a chessboard-style alphanumeric label (e.g., “C5”; letter for column, number for row) that encodes its grid position symbolically, allowing spatial relationships to be expressed through text. To remove directional ambiguity, the prompt fixes a mapping between cardinal directions and image axes, associating north, south, east, and west with up, down, right, and left, respectively. Free points are rendered as blue dots and blocked regions as red “x” marks, with the executive VLM prompted to select navigation waypoints from the free points. Finally, each robot is labeled with its name at the image location corresponding to its current pose, enabling the VLM to distinguish between them. 

B. Task Classification 

Each task instruction is first categorized by a classifier LLM into one of three categories. The descriptions provided to the LLM are as follows: 

Nav2Point : “Best for tasks where each robot drives to one or more goal locations in order. The destination(s) matter, but the exact path taken does not.” 

Coverage : “Best for tasks that involve patrolling or sweep-ing an area. The planner selects high-level regions to cover rather than a specific path, and a low-level controller ensures all selected regions are visited efficiently.” 

Maneuver : “Best for tasks where the specific route matters, not just the destination. Used when the instruction constrains 

how the robot travels, such as loops, specified routes, or avoidance, ...etc. This category also functions as a catch-all category for tasks that do not fall neatly into the Nav2Point 

or Coverage categories.” 

C. Executive VLM Prompting 

A unique prompt is designed for each of the three task categories listed in Section IV-B and is sent to the execu-tive VLM which selects waypoints from the marks on the overhead image. Full prompts for each of the three classes are available in the project’s GitHub repository. Each prompt sent to the VLM is assembled from six components, in order: 1) Preamble – robot descriptions, image context, and global rules shared across all tasks. 2) Map Overlay Description – an explanation of what the markings on the overlaid image represent. 3) Controller Explanation – a per-category block describ-ing how the controller works, how to select points, the output schema, and a worked example. 4) Operator Instruction – Natural-language instruction L.5) Chain-of-Thought Scaffold – a per-category reasoning scaffold guiding the VLM’s reasoning. 6) Marked Image – the marked overhead image. 

D. Chain of Thought Scaffold 

Before selecting a route, the executive VLM outputs a reasoning trace in text. The CoT reasoning scaffolds differ between the task categories, but follow a similar structure: 1) Grounding - Locates each robot, identifies key objects in the instruction, labels the object at each red “x” marker. 2) Task Allocation - Divides the task between the robots by identifying which robot is best suited for each part. 3) Leg Decomposition (Maneuver only) – Splits each robot’s assigned task into an ordered sequence of legs (e.g., travel to chair, loop around it, travel to person). 4) Route Construction - Lists waypoints for each robot. 5) Verify - Verifies no selected point is blocked, replacing any that are with a nearby unblocked point. E. Low-Level Planners 

Low-level path planners are used to convert the VLM chosen waypoints into efficient paths that avoid blocked regions in the occupancy map (Section IV-A.2). 

1) A* Path Planning: For Nav2Point and Maneuver tasks, the executive VLM outputs an ordered list of waypoints for each robot: a single goal or a few goals for Nav2Point , and a longer sequence specifying a precise route for Maneuver .The A* [25] algorithm then plans collision-free paths through these waypoints in order, beginning from each robot’s initial position. The image marking and chain-of-thought reasoning are designed to steer the VLM away from blocked regions, but in the case a blocked waypoint is selected, the planner first attempts to project that waypoint to the nearest free cell within a 1m radius before dropping it if none exists. This ensures safety while attempting to preserve VLM intent although poor VLM selections can still lead to task failures. 

2) Coverage Path Planning: For Coverage tasks, the robot’s sensing footprint is assumed to match the marking resolution of the overlay, so every marked point within the specified region must be visited, though the visiting order is unconstrained. The VLM outputs an unordered set of regions for each robot, and the planner computes an efficient visiting order by solving an open-path traveling salesman problem anchored at the robot’s initial pose, using nearest-neighbor construction [26] refined by 2-opt [27]. Edge costs are shortest-path distances on the inflated occupancy grid, computed with Dijkstra’s algorithm [28].

B. Offline Testing 

Because large-scale evaluation on physical robots is time-intensive, the comparison against baseline methods and the ablation study were conducted offline. In this setting, each method is prompted with an overhead image of the workspace and produces a planned path for the robots. With-out executing the plan on the robots, each path is evaluated directly for whether it completes the task instruction and avoids obstacles. Each of the 18 prompts was run ten times per method with results reported in Table I and Figure 4. 

C. Performance Metrics

D. Comparison to Existing Methods 

Because no prior work has investigated coordinated path planning from natural language for the types of complex be-havior investigated in this work, CoPlanVLM was compared against three representative approaches for VLM-based path planning that were adapted to the two-robot setting. 

Grid Overlay: The closest work to our problem setting is [17], which uses a VLM to plan paths for a single robot executing complex behaviors described in natural language. A labeled grid is overlaid on the overhead image, and the VLM selects an ordered list of cells whose centroids serve as a sequence of target locations for a PID controller. We adapt this approach to our multi-robot setting by labeling each robot with its name and prompting the VLM to plan routes for both. Because [17] relies on the VLM alone to select obstacle-free routes, initial testing showed very high collision rates. We therefore strengthen this baseline by enforcing obstacle avoidance with the A* planner of Section IV-E.1 for a fairer comparison of the marking and prompting methods. 

CoNVOI Marking: CoNVOI [7] performs single-robot navigation from an ego-centric viewpoint. It uses a VLM to identify the robot’s context (e.g., indoor vs. outdoor) and select appropriate navigation rules. CoNVOI marks only obstacle-free regions of the image with numbers from which the VLM selects navigation waypoints. CoNVOI is adapted to our setting by fixing a single rule set across all prompts, since our scenes are all similar indoor environments. It is extended to two robots by labeling each with its name and prompting the VLM to select a list of waypoints for each to follow. The marking scheme is replicated identically by placing marks only in free cells in the occupancy map. 

Pixel Selection: An alternative to selecting from a set of marks is to have the VLM select waypoints as pixel coordinates on an unmarked image as in [9], [31]. Although these works target single robots, we adapt pixel-coordinate selection to our problem by prompting the VLM to output a sequence of pixel coordinates (px, p y ) for each robot on a normalized 1000 ×1000 scale as in [31]. These are projected to world coordinates (x, y ) and used as waypoints in the same A* planner introduced in Section IV-E.1. Table II reports overall and per-category average success rates and makespan. Figure 4 shows per-prompt success rates. CoPlanVLM achieved the highest overall success rate of 84 .4% with CoNVOI second with 50 .0% . CoPlanVLM ’s strong performance on Coverage tasks likely reflects its dedicated coverage planner, highlighting the benefit of adap-tive planner selection. CoPlanVLM also outperforms the baselines on the other two task types, indicating a supe-rior VLM prompting format. Successful paths produced by 

CoPlanVLM were on average 27% shorter in makespan than CoNVOI marking (1.00 vs. 1.37), largely because 

CoPlanVLM assigns each subtask to the best-suited robot and divides large coverage tasks between the two. In contrast, the baselines frequently sent a robot to a goal closer to its teammate or assigned the same goals to both.

E. Ablation Testing 

Three different configurations of CoPlanVLM isolated the contribution of each component. CoPlanVLM W/O Obs. Marking does not mark detected obstacles with the red “x” symbol as described in Section IV-A.3 and the reasoning step where the VLM names the objects marked by each red “x” is accordingly skipped. CoPlanVLM Maneuver Only retains the same marking, but does away with the task categorization and instead only uses the Maneuver planner which was chosen as the single planner best capable of completing all tasks since the Nav2Point planner is only used to navigate to a few set positions, and the Coverage planner cannot specify an order for the points to be visited. Finally, CoPlanVLM No CoT Reasoning retains the original marking scheme and task categorizer, but removes the CoT reasoning trace, instead prompting the VLM to directly output waypoints. The ablation study (Table III) indicates that each of the three components contribute significantly to the over-all success rate. Removing the marked obstacles reduced the Nav2Point and Maneuver success rates by −40% and 

−18 .3% respectively, largely due to the VLM selecting inaccessible regions or missing crucial objects in the scene. Performance on Coverage tasks improved slightly ( +15% )without obstacle marking. We hypothesize that because the coverage planner drops any unsafe points, the VLM can safely select whole regions without reasoning about individual obstacles, so the additional obstacle-labeling step may introduce noise rather than useful information for these tasks. This supports extending the task specific prompting to adaptively select the best image overlay for each task. Using only the Maneuver prompt and planner for all tasks resulted in −13 .3% SR and −55% SR on Nav2Point and Coverage tasks respectively along with a higher makespan indicating that relying on a single planner for all tasks reduces solution quality. Removing the CoT reasoning had the greatest overall effect with an average of −35 .5% SR over all tasks.

F. Robot Deployment CoPlanVLM was deployed on two Turtlebot 4 robots in an indoor robotics laboratory equipped with a motion capture room for robot pose estimation and an overhead camera. GPT-4o was used as both the classifier LLM and the execu-tive VLM. A lab computer was used to perform all calcula-tions, perform API calls to the VLM, and communicate linear and angular velocity commands to the Turtlebots through ROS2 Humble. All nine prompts tested offline on real-world images from the AVL were evaluated twice on hardware with fresh overhead images and newly generated plans for each trial. Hardware deployments (Table IV) demonstrated similar success rates to offline testing: 83 .3% vs. 81 .1% indicating that CoPlanVLM transfers smoothly to physical hardware. The three recorded failures during testing resulted either from missed obstacle detections (prompts 6 and 18) or incomplete coverage (prompt 11) and were visible in the plan before execution. Figure 1 (Bottom) illustrates the paths planned and actual paths for three tasks. VI. C ONCLUSIONS , L IMITATIONS , AND FUTURE WORK 

CoPlanVLM is a method for leveraging VLMs to per-form long-horizon path planning for a collaborative team of two robots. CoPlanVLM improves the spatial reasoning, coordination, and path-planning capabilities of a base VLM through image preprocessing, task categorization, and struc-tured chain-of-thought reasoning leading to improved success rates and more efficient paths compared to baselines. Several limitations remain. Although CoPlanVLM ’s 84.4% success rate outperforms existing approaches, reliability re-mains insufficient for mission-critical deployment. One com-mon failure mode arises when CLIPSeg fails to detect small obstacles: an undetected box drove the 50% success rate on prompts 6 and 18, suggesting CoPlanVLM could benefit from complementary sensing (e.g., LiDAR) to refine the occupancy map. CoPlanVLM also assumes a top-down view of the workspace, access to a cloud-hosted VLM, and was evaluated on only two robots. Future work will address these limitations by fine-tuning VLMs for waypoint selection, investigating edge deployment, and scaling to larger fleets.
