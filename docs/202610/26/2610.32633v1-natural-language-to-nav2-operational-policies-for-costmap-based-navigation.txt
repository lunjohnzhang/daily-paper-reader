# Natural-Language to Nav2: Operational Policies for Costmap-Based Navigation 

Jiyeong Chae, Hyunkyo Seo, Heonjae Lee, and Kyung-Joon Park 

Daegu Gyeongbuk Institute of Science and Technology (DGIST) 333 Techno Jungang-daero, Hyeonpung-eup, Dalseong-gun, Daegu, 42988, Republic of Korea Jiyeong Chae and Hyunkyo Seo contributed equally. Corresponding author: Kyung-Joon Park (kjp@dgist.ac.kr). 

Abstract 

Autonomous mobile robots support material transport in warehouses and industrial facili-ties, where navigation must adapt to changing operating conditions. Sensor observations reveal physical obstacles, but do not by themselves specify temporary access rules, route preferences, or exceptions known to operators. This paper presents a natural-language policy interface that brings this knowledge into ROS 2 Nav2. A local large language model converts instructions into JSON policies specifying destinations, spatial constraints, and temporal or event conditions. Deterministic checks govern mission acceptance, and three costmap adapters project accepted policies into the existing navigation stack without changing its planner or controller. On 150 researcher-authored English commands, four local models produce 107–142 correct accepted poli-cies, compared with 44 for a rule-based parser, with 3–20 incorrect acceptances per model. Fault injection records rejection of eight endpoint/JSON faults and 29 validator-stage faults. Navi-gation experiments use correct task policies to evaluate keepouts, zone costs, person-dependent regions, and event-conditioned passage in simulation and on a TurtleBot3. For Tasks 1–3, mean travel times are 18.4–24.5% lower in simulation and 23.1–35.0% lower on the physical testbed than with the baseline lacking those policies. A native KeepoutFilter comparison and two-robot demonstrations examine basic keepout behavior and shared policy projection. The interface con-nects operator instructions to explicit navigation policies while retaining Nav2’s planning and control components. 

Keywords: mobile robot navigation; natural-language policies; large language models; ROS 2 Nav2; costmaps. 

# 1 Introduction 

Autonomous mobile robots (AMRs) support material transport in warehouses and industrial fa-cilities [1, 2]. Their navigation must account for changing workspace conditions as well as the destination of each delivery [3]. Some of these conditions are operating rules: an aisle may be reserved for loading, a corridor may be closed for a shift, or an emergency may permit an otherwise restricted passage. Sensors can detect an obstruction, but its expected duration and permitted exceptions require additional information. Operators need a way to make this knowledge available to the robot when planning a mission. ROS 2 Nav2 provides planners and controllers for mobile navigation [4]. Layered costmaps combine spatial information [5], and native keepout filters can receive updated masks during oper-ation [6]. These mechanisms provide a means to apply constraints once they have been expressed in a form the navigation stack can use. An instruction such as “avoid Zone A for the next thirty minutes” also requires the system to identify the zone, retain the restriction, and determine when it expires. The problem addressed here is to connect such instructions to Nav2 with explicit rules for policy acceptance, mission replacement, and restriction lifetime. 1arXiv:2610.32633v1 [cs.RO] 23 Oct 2026 We introduce a natural-language policy interface that represents destinations together with con-straints on reaching them. A locally hosted large language model (LLM) translates each instruction into a structured policy. The bridge checks the complete candidate against the schema and execu-tion context before accepting it. Accepted state supplies waypoint goals and inputs to three costmap adapters for keepouts, zone costs, and object-avoidance regions (Figure 1). The same policy-derived updates can be sent to independent robot instances. The LLM operates when a policy is submitted, outside the navigation control loop. NavFn computes the global path and DWB controls local motion. The intermediate policy makes the requested destinations, constraints, and conditions available for inspection. Structural acceptance and semantic correctness remain distinct: a candidate may satisfy the interface contract while naming a waypoint the operator did not request. The evaluation measures both. The work makes three contributions. (1) A policy schema represents destinations together with spatial constraints and temporal or event conditions, providing a common interface between language interpretation and navigation. (2) Transactional acceptance checks the complete can-didate against the schema and execution context before replacing the mission snapshot; rejection preserves the preceding mission, while retained restrictions are managed separately. (3) Nav2 pro-jection translates accepted state into waypoint goals and keepout, soft-cost, and object-avoidance inputs for the existing navigation stack, including shared policy updates to independent robot in-stances. The evaluation follows this architecture. A 150-command benchmark measures conversion accu-racy and accepted errors, and fault injection examines rejection and state preservation. Simulation and physical trials examine route selection under task policies, including a separate comparison of event-conditioned passage permissions. 22 Related Work 

Language as a robot interface. ROSGPT maps natural-language requests to structured ROS 2 instructions using an application ontology and prompting [7]. Our interface extends the language-to-structured-command workflow with explicit management of mission state, restriction lifetimes, and event-conditioned permissions. Code as Policies generates programs that process perception outputs and invoke control APIs, including reactive and waypoint-based behaviors [8]. LLM-A* uses language-model guidance in heuristic search [9], and 3P-LLM studies language-model-based path planning [10]. In our system, the model selects registered destinations and policy parameters; Nav2 computes geometric paths. 

Context in costmaps. Layered costmaps combine spatial information from different sources [5]. Dynamic-obstacle extensions include the ROS 2 layer approach of Cen Cheng et al. [2] and the velocity-obstacle costmap of Chen et al. [11]. Sani et al. use pixel-level image classification to revise local traversal costs for agricultural navigation [12]. Our adapters project accepted operator policies into this costmap representation. 

Nav2 and operational management. Nav2 separates planning and control from costmap gen-eration [4]. Its native KeepoutFilter accepts occupancy-grid masks and processes new masks at runtime [6]. Industrial studies address fulfillment operations [1], knowledge-based material handling [3], and multi-AGV collision and deadlock resolution [13]. Our policy-sharing experiment distributes com-mon restrictions to independent Nav2 instances.

# 3 From an Instruction to a Navigation Policy 

The system treats an operating instruction as a proposed change to a mission. Figure 1 follows that change from language input to robot motion. Interpretation produces a candidate, acceptance establishes the new mission state, and projection converts the applicable rules into Nav2 inputs. All LLM inference runs on an on-premises workstation connected to the robots through the local network. 

3.1 A schema for destinations and operating rules 

An operator publishes an instruction on /nl_command . A frozen local LLM receives the sentence, the registered zone and waypoint context, an output schema, and example conversions. No model 3weights are fine-tuned. Waypoint labels resolve to stored poses and zone labels resolve to map-frame geometry. This avoids asking the model to invent region coordinates. Table 2 groups the policy fields by execution role. For example, “Go to waypoint 6 via Zone B, but do not use Zone A” requests a destination, a required transit zone, and a keepout. “Set the cost of Zone A to 200 and Zone B to 160, then go to waypoint 4” adds relative traversal costs. Temporal exclusions and conditional rules carry the lifetime or event scope that would otherwise be lost when reducing a command to a destination alone.

3.2 Acceptance depends on the candidate and the current context 

An endpoint failure or malformed JSON is rejected before a policy object reaches the validator. A parsed candidate is checked for required structure, data types, registered identifiers, admissible values, and conflicts among fields. A zone cannot simultaneously be a required transit region and an exclusion. Conflicting costs for the same zone also invalidate the candidate. The entire update is rejected if a check fails. Valid fields are not applied selectively, and invalid numbers are not clipped. The schema admits person and forklift object rules with finite radii from 0.1 to 10 m. Du-rations must be positive and no greater than 1,440 minutes; absolute intervals require ordered endpoints. Conditional rules use default , fire_alarm , or low_battery and the actions forbid ,

allow , or allow_shortest . Default rules only forbid. Equal-priority conflicts, incompatible actions, and event priorities that fail to exceed a default prohibition are rejected. Keepout and soft-cost assignments to the same zone are checked for conflict. A structurally admissible candidate can still conflict with a restriction already in force. The execution-context check tests required transit zones against effective keepouts, including permanent restrictions, retained timed restrictions, and applicable event permissions. It rejects an incompatible candidate without deleting the transit requirement. These checks establish structural and contextual consistency; they cannot detect every requirement omitted from the instruction. The bridge sends goals through /follow_waypoints and supplies three global-costmap adapters. Each adapter uses the global map frame and costmap resolution. In the source code, KeepoutLayer and SoftCostLayer are named KeepoutCommandLayer and ZoneSoftCostLayer . Policy costs shape the global path; they are not supplied to the local costmap, so this configuration does not enforce policy restrictions as hard constraints on local-controller trajectories [14]. 

KeepoutLayer. The bridge evaluates active zone restrictions and publishes the resulting list on 

/forbidden_zones_update . The layer assigns lethal-obstacle cost 254 to the corresponding grid cells. Expiry or an event permission removes that policy contribution. Sensor obstacles remain active. Native KeepoutFilter offers the equivalent basic keepout mechanism through occupancy-grid masks [6]. 

SoftCostLayer. Integer values on /zone_cost_overrides assign traversal costs from 0 to 252. Higher values discourage traversal without introducing a lethal region. Nav2 reserves 253 for in-scribed obstacles, 254 for lethal obstacles, and 255 for unknown space. NavFn treats 253 as an obstacle, so it is excluded from the soft-cost range. Invalid direct cost messages are rejected while the preceding valid costs remain in effect. 

ObjectAvoidanceLayer. YOLOv8 detections and RGB-D depth provide object locations trans-formed into the map frame. The layer receives them on /object_world_positions and applies cost 254 within the policy radius supplied through /object_avoidance_radius . Detection persis-tence and expiry govern removal of the contribution. The experiments use person detections. The configured radius sets the spatial extent of the policy cost region. 3.4 Time, events, and more than one robot 

A timed keepout remains separate from ordinary mission replacement. Wall-clock endpoints are mapped to the node’s ROS clock and activated or expired through ROS timers. Simulation shares 

/clock with Nav2; the physical setup uses a common system clock. An event rule can permit passage through a normally forbidden zone. Its activation removes the applicable policy keepout while retaining sensor costs and restrictions configured to disallow overrides. Shared policy-derived updates are distributed through ROS 2 topics to each robot’s adapters. Every robot retains its own localization, sensor layers, planner, and controller. Matching policy re-gions can therefore coexist with different complete costmaps and paths. The two-robot experiments examine this common-policy projection. 

# 4 Interpreting Instructions and Containing Invalid Updates 

The interface evaluation measures conversion into the intended policy, including correct acceptance and accepted semantic errors. Fault injection tests rejection and preservation of the active mission. 54.1 Commands, models, and scoring 

The benchmark contains 150 English commands built from ten policy intentions, five phrasing levels, and three variants per intention–level combination. The intentions cover keepouts, ordered destinations, numeric zone costs, required transit, person avoidance with default or explicit radii, timed restrictions, event overrides, combined keepout/transit requirements, and multi-field policies. L0 is direct wording, L1 uses workplace phrasing, L2 redistributes or reorders clauses, L3 includes corrections or distracting content, and L4 expresses the requirement indirectly. Four frozen local models are evaluated: Llama 3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3.5-9B. Each uses the same schema prompt and 15 input–output demonstrations, with temperature 0, 

top_p =0, and seed 42. A deterministic keyword-and-slot parser provides the comparison. It extracts the same policy categories through fixed patterns. None of the demonstration sentences appears verbatim in the benchmark. The research team developed the prompt, demonstrations, parser, reference policies, and AI-assisted command set. A correct policy must match the reference destinations and order, transit zones, exclusions and timing, costs, object radii, and event rules without adding an executable element. Correct accep-tance compares the full normalized policy with the reference after admission by the validator. Raw accuracy evaluates the generated policy before validation, retaining additional or malformed entries (Supplementary Material, Section S7). Every command has one processing outcome: correct accep-tance, incorrect acceptance, interpreter rejection, parsing rejection, or validator rejection. Explicit interpreter rejection is counted first and is not counted again at the validator. Five fixed-setting calls per command produce unchanged aggregate counts. The analysis uses 150 commands as its denominator. Nominal Wilson intervals describe command-level proportions but do not model shared intentions, paraphrase dependence, or development bias. Latency measures candidate generation from submission on an AMD Ryzen 5 5600X workstation with 32 GB RAM and an RTX 3060 with 8 GB VRAM.

# 7 Discussion 

An operating rule has a lifetime beyond the sentence that introduced it. The interface makes that lifetime explicit through mission replacement, retained restrictions, timers, and event state. This is particularly useful when the next instruction arrives before a previous restriction expires. The context check can reject a required transit zone that is currently closed without silently changing the requested mission. The observed missing keepouts, omitted stops, and added goals motivate previews that place the instruction beside the proposed policy. Confirmation of critical restriction changes, targeted clarifi-cation of numeric roles, and authorization checks for persistent restrictions are candidate extensions for operator review. The benchmark, prompt, parser, and reference policies share a development process; evaluation with independently authored commands is needed to assess generalization. The navigation experiments examine operating policies in two fixed layouts. The baseline lacks operating information that the policy-enabled condition receives. The navigation gains reflect the in-corporation of this information into Nav2’s costmap. Small run-to-run SDs describe these conditions. Additional layouts, obstacle behaviors, and instruction sources are needed to assess generalization. The configured radius specifies the costmap region; achieved clearance depends on perception and navigation execution. 12 8 Conclusion 

We developed a policy interface that connects operator instructions to Nav2 through structured policies, deterministic acceptance, and explicit mission-state handling. The language benchmark quantifies both successful conversions and accepted semantic errors. Simulation and physical ex-periments demonstrate route changes under operating policies. In the direct keepout comparison, both implementations applied the tested prohibition, release, and reapplication updates success-fully. Further evaluation should jointly measure mission completion and instruction compliance from natural-language input through robot execution.

21 S7 Policy scoring and accepted-error analysis 

S7.1 Raw and post-validation policy accuracy 

Raw accuracy compares the generated policy with the reference before validation. The comparison retains all destinations and transit entries, including additional coordinate goals and malformed objects. A policy is incorrect if it omits a required element or adds or changes an executable element. Relative restriction durations are compared in minutes. Post-V compares the complete normalized policy with the reference after acceptance. Destina-tion order is preserved; unordered rule lists are compared independently of serialization order. Both scores check destinations, transit zones, static and timed restrictions, zone costs, object radii, and event conditions, actions, and priorities. Relative durations and radii use three-decimal precision. Interpreter and validator decisions remain those recorded during the experiment. The comparison covers all 3,750 stored results: five methods, 150 commands, and five calls per command. Per-command outcomes agree across the five repetitions. Llama 3.1-8B has 106 raw matches and 107 correct acceptances; Qwen2.5-7B has 122 at both stages. The accompanying scoring script and case records provide the command-level results. 

S7.2 Qwen2.5-7B: malformed transit entry 

Command N03-L0 requests: “Go to WP3 with traversal cost 5 for A and 200 for B.” The output contains the requested destination and costs but adds the following transit entry:      

> "via_zones": [{"dest": {"x": 0, "y": 0, "yaw": 0}}]

The reference has an empty via_zones list. The candidate is incorrect before validation and is rejected with field ’via_zones’ contains a non-string zone . This outcome occurs in all five repetitions. The rejection concerns zone parsing; the requested costs, 5 and 200, fall within both source versions’ cost ranges. 

S7.3 Llama 3.1-8B: additional and repeated destinations 

For N09-L4-V3 , the instruction requests WP5 through B, a keepout in C, cost 40 in A, and a 2 m person-avoidance radius. The accepted policy appends an unrequested coordinate goal at (0 , 0, 0) 

after WP5. It is incorrect at both scoring stages. In N09-L3-V3 , an additional destination with null coordinates is also incorrect before validation and is rejected by the validator. This normalization accounts for Llama’s increase from 106 raw matches to 107 correct acceptances.
