# Dynamic Conflict Clustering with lightweight Learning and Strategic Repair for Distributed Constraint Satisfaction 

Raziyeh Moghaddas a, Fabio Caraffini a, 

> aDepartment of Science and Engineering, Swansea University, Swansea, UK

Abstract 

Distributed Constraint Satisfaction Problems (DCSPs) model distributed decision-making in multi-agent systems, where agents assign values to local variables while satisfying shared constraints. However, many DCSP solvers still rely on broad, repeated peer-to-peer communication and are largely reactive, with lim-ited support for strategic coordination or learning. This increases communication overhead, can lead to stagnation, and reduces the success of hard instances. Hence, we propose Dynamic Conflict Clustering-Incomplete (DCC-Incomplete), a repair-based solver that combines conflict-centred decomposition, structured coordination, strategic repair, and lightweight learning for stagnation recovery. DCC-Incomplete groups the unsolved part of the problem into conflict clus-ters and restricts communication via a coordination layer to agents within the same conflict region. It also introduces memory-guided restart, enabling agents and clusters to pause, revise, and adapt repairs based on neighbouring conflict states. A thorough evaluation of DCC-Incomplete on distributed graph-colouring benchmarks with planted satisfying assignments shows that its memory-guided restart improves the success rate from 37.50% to 61.11%, reduces median runtime from 0.565s to 0.367s, and cuts median communication from 12.493 to 6.503 messages per constraint. These results suggest that dynamic conflict clustering, combined with lightweight strategic and learning-guided repair, improves both communication efficiency and robustness in incomplete DCSP solving. 

Keywords: Distributed constraint satisfaction problem, Distributed constraint Optimisation problem, AI Agents, Multi-agent systems

1. Introduction 

Distributed Constraint Satisfaction Problems (DCSPs) provide a formal framework for modelling distributed decision-making tasks in which multiple agents control local variables and must collectively satisfy constraints involving one another [1]. This formulation is well-suited to Multi-Agent System (MAS) settings, where problem information, decision authority, and computational responsibility are distributed among autonomous agents [1, 2]. Historically, DCSP and related distributed constraint reasoning models have been applied to several decentralised coordination tasks in sensor networks [3], low-power embedded devices for environmental sensing and surveillance [4, 5], scheduling [6, 7], and other engineering domains [8]. Recent impactful applications include smart-home device scheduling, where appliances and sensors across multiple homes are coordinated as a multi-agent optimisation problem [9]; decentralised intersection traffic management using distributed constraint optimisation to coordinate vehicles [10]; smart distribution-grid restoration and microgrid coordination [11]; and sensor-based dynamic environments where dynamic Distributed Constraint Optimisation Problems (DCOPs) are solved by sensor-actuator agents [12]. Distributed constraint reasoning has also been used for scheduling, such as multi-agent train scheduling in single-track railways [7]. Together, these applications highlight the need to solve constraint problems without a single central controller with full access to all variables, constraints, and decisions. Since the formalisation of DCSPs – we follow the well-established formulation in [1] (Definition 1) – several families of distributed constraint-solving algorithms have been proposed. Complete search methods guarantee a solution when one exists but often require substantial coordination and messaging. Examples include Asynchronous Backtracking (ABT) from the early DCSP formalisation [1], Asynchronous Weak-Commitment Search (AWC) [13], and Asynchronous Partial Overlay (APO), which uses cooperative mediation over overlapping subproblems [14, 15, 16, 17]. Related DCOP methods include ADOPT, an asynchronous complete optimisation algorithm [18], and DPOP, a dynamic-programming method based on pseudo-tree utility propagation [19]. In contrast, incomplete 

and local-search methods trade completeness for scalability and responsiveness. Examples include the Distributed Stochastic Algorithm (DSA) [3], the Maximum Gain Message algorithm (MGM) [20], Distributed Breakout Algorithms (DBA) [21], Max-Sum message passing [4], and the Generalized Distributed Breakout Algorithm (GDBA) [22]. These methods advance distributed constraint reasoning but also show that communication design and agent behaviour remain central to solver performance. 

Definition 1 (Distributed Constraint Satisfaction Problem) . A DCSP is defined as the tuple DCSP = ⟨A, X, D, C, own ⟩, where: 

• A = {a1, . . . , a n} is a finite set of agents; 

• X = {x1, . . . , x n} is a finite set of variables; 

2• D = {D1, . . . , D n} is the set of finite domains, where xi ∈ Di;

• C = {c1, . . . , c m} is a finite set of constraints; 

• own : X → A maps each variable to the agent that owns it. 

Technically, many distributed constraint solvers use fixed interaction struc-tures, neighbour-to-neighbour communication, or repeated message exchange over the constraint graph. In incomplete constraint solving, repair-based meth-ods start from a complete assignment and iteratively modify (repair) conflicted variable values to reduce violations [23, 24]. This repair requires communication to maintain consistency and coordinate decisions between related agents, which can become costly when only a small part of the problem is in conflict. Often, locally consistent agents still engage in checking, messaging, or synchronisation even though the difficulty is confined to a smaller conflict region. This motivates a conflict-centred approach, where computation and communication focus on the currently unsatisfied part of the problem instead of repeatedly activating the whole graph. From a MAS-based perspective, many solvers are strong in reactive local repair and distributed communication, but provide limited support for strategic behaviour and learning during search. This is problematic because agents that only address immediate local conflicts may repeat similar repairs, fail to adapt to changes in neighbouring regions, or continue repair attempts that no longer improve the global solving process. As a result, limited strategic behaviour and learning can contribute to stagnation, unnecessary communication, and lower success rates in difficult instances. This work addresses both the technical and behavioural gaps identified above. Technically, it reduces unnecessary communication by dynamically organising the unsolved part of the problem into local conflict-centred structures (referred to as conflict clusters) and by regulating information exchange through a coordination layer. Conflict clusters allow restricting repair activity to agents currently involved in conflicts, while the coordination layer improves communication between agents, leaders, and neighbouring clusters. Behaviourally, the proposed model goes beyond immediate local repair by adding strategic coordination mechanisms and a lightweight learning-guided restart component. Strategic behaviour is used in a broad MAS sense, inspired by the game-theoretic idea that an agent’s decision may depend on the possible actions or states of other decision-makers [25, 26]. Note that this is not a formal game-theoretic equilibrium model; instead, it applies strategic coordination practically by delaying, reconsidering, coordinating, and adapting repair decisions according to the state of neighbouring conflict regions. The learning component follows the principle of memory-based search, using information on recently visited or unproductive states to guide exploration [27]. Here, the memory mechanism is lightweight and local to stagnation recovery; it is not a full Tabu Search (TS) algorithm. To summarise, we propose Dynamic Conflict Clustering-Incomplete (DCC-Incomplete), a repair-based solver for DCSPs that classifies agents according to 3their current conflict involvement, forms dynamic conflict clusters over the active conflict regions, and regulates communication through a dedicated coordination layer. This structure keeps repair local while enabling coordination across clusters when needed. The model also adds strategic behaviours–postponed proposal handling, notification-based coordination, and conditional cluster merging–and a memory-guided restart mechanism that improves stagnation recovery by avoiding repeated exploration of recently unproductive states. The main contributions of this work are as follows: • It proposes a dynamic conflict-centred decomposition mechanism for in-complete DCSP solving, in which repair is focused on currently violated regions of the constraint graph. • Introduces the novel construct of conflict clusters and an additional coordi-nation layer designed to systematically structure inter-agent communication and to minimize superfluous interactions among agents that are not directly participating in the same conflict region. • It incorporates strategic coordination behaviours, including postponed proposal handling, notification-based inter-cluster coordination, and condi-tional cluster merging. • It adds a lightweight learning-guided restart mechanism for stagnation recovery, inspired by memory-based search. The remainder of the article is organised as follows. Section 2 presents the proposed DCC-Incomplete solver and its coordination mechanisms. Section 3 describes the benchmark design, the compared methods, and the evaluation metrics. Section 4 presents and discusses the experimental results. Finally, Section 5 concludes the article and outlines future work. 

2. The proposed DCC-Incomplete solver 

DCC-Incomplete is designed as an efficient repair-based solver for DCSPs. It is based on three key principles, namely dynamic conflict clustering, structured coordination, and localised repair mechanisms. Instead of repeatedly activating the entire constraint graph (Definition 2), this solver focuses on the parts of the problem that are currently involved in conflicts. To achieve this, a preliminary module classifies agents, followed by the construction of a conflict subgraph, which ultimately enables the formation of dynamic conflict clusters governed by a dedicated coordination logic (as illustrated in Figure 1, blocks 1, 2, and 3).

Following Montanari’s constraint-network representation [28], later standardised in constraint processing [29] and adopted in DCS [1], the constraint graph of a DCSP is an undirected graph G = ( V, E ),where V = X and ∃c ∈ C | { xi, x j } ⊆ scp( c), ∀(xi, x j ) ∈ E (i.e., two variables are connected by an edge if they appear together in at least one constraint).

2.1. Agent classification 

Agents are classified as active or passive based on their current involvement in conflicts. At a generic iteration t, we define active agents as those that participate in at least one violated constraint. In contrast, passive agents are locally consistent with the current assignment and are therefore excluded from the repair process unless they subsequently become involved in a constraint violation. We can therefore partition the set of agents as A = AA(t) ∪ AP (t), with AA(t) ∩ AP (t) = ∅, where the subscripts A and P denote the subsets of active and passive agents, respectively (Definition 7). We define the “local conflict cost” of an agent in Definition 5 as a measure to identify which agents are currently involved in violated constraints and should therefore participate in repair. 

Definition 5 (Local Conflict Cost) . Given an assignment α and a candidate value v ∈ Di for the variable xi owned by agent ai, the local conflict cost is 

LCost i(v) = ∣∣{ c ∈ Ci | c is violated under α[xi ← v] }∣∣.

This, in turn, enables the introduction of the score associated with a given agent (Definition 6), which provides a more operational characterisation of active and passive agents (Definition 7). 

Definition 6 (Agent Score) . The score of agent ai under its current value vi is defined as 

Score( ai) = −LCost i(vi).

Note that a score of 0 indicates that the current value of xi violates no local constraint, whereas a negative score indicates participation in at least one violated constraint. Thus, Score( ai) serves as a conflict indicator: values closer to 0 correspond to lower local conflict. 

Definition 7 (Active ( AA(t)) and Passive ( AP (t)) Agent Sets) . At iteration t,

A can be partitioned as follows: 

AA(t) = {a ∈ A | Score t(a) < 0}, AP (t) = {a ∈ A | Score t(a) = 0 }.

Classification is carried out dynamically in every iteration, and the active-agent partition serves as the basis for constructing the conflict subgraph.

2.6. Repair and Coordination Procedure 

The repair procedure of DCC-Incomplete works at the conflict cluster level. Each conflict cluster is an unsatisfied region of the problem, and the coordination layer supplies the information needed to coordinate repair without centralising 10 solving. The leader coordinates the repair cycle, while agents still evaluate candidate values in a distributed way. This preserves agent autonomy, avoids centralising each conflict cluster, and prevents the leader from becoming a computational bottleneck. At each repair step, the leader selects as target the agent whose current value contributes most to the cluster’s conflict load. The leader sends this agent a repair request and the relevant context from the coordination layer, including the current values of variables needed to evaluate candidate repairs and any required boundary-neighbour values. The target agent evaluates its candidate values locally. For each v ∈ Di,it computes the local conflict cost LCost i(v) and returns a repair proposal to the leader. The leader does not compute all candidate values centrally; it only coordinates the decision by comparing the proposal with the current cluster state and checking whether it affects boundary consistency. A proposal has three possible outcomes. First, if it improves the cluster state and does not create a boundary conflict, it is accepted and committed. Second, if it is locally useful but conflicts with a neighbouring conflict cluster, it is deferred and stored in its queue Qkt . Third, if no candidate value improves the state, the cluster records a no-improvement event, which supports stagnation detection and may later trigger the memory-guided restart mechanism in Section 2.7. 2.6.1. Boundary-Conflict Handling 

Boundary conflicts arise when a candidate repair within a conflict cluster affects a constraint involving a variable outside that cluster. These cases matter because accepting the repair may improve the current cluster but worsen a neighbouring one. DCC-Incomplete therefore checks boundary consistency before committing to a proposed value change. Let Kkt be the current conflict centre and let ( xi, x j ) ∈ Boundary kt be a boundary edge, where xi ∈ V kt and xj /∈ V kt . If changing xi to a candidate value 

v∗ 

> i

would violate a boundary constraint involving xj , the proposal is treated as boundary-conflicting. The leader then stores the proposal in Qkt rather than accepting it immediately. This allows the solver to preserve a potentially useful move while avoiding premature decisions that may create unnecessary inter-cluster disruption. Boundary-conflict handling constitutes one of the primary mechanisms through which the model achieves strategic coordination. Rather than re-sponding solely to immediate, localised improvements, each cluster evaluates the impact of a prospective move on adjacent conflict regions. This facilitates more regulated information exchange and mitigates the occurrence of recurrent local adjustments that merely propagate conflicts across cluster boundaries. 11 2.6.2. Deferred Proposal Handling 

A deferred proposal is a candidate repair that might improve the current cluster but cannot be safely committed under the current boundary context. When a proposal is deferred, it enters Qkt , and the leader stores the candidate value, its local cost, and the boundary dependency that blocked immediate acceptance. Deferred proposals are reconsidered only when boundary context changes, usually after a neighbouring conflict cluster commits a value and sends an update. The leader then re-evaluates the proposal: if it no longer conflicts at the boundary and still improves the cluster state, it may be accepted; otherwise, it is discarded. When a cluster commits any accepted change, it clears its deferred queue ( Qkt ← ∅) because the local context has changed and earlier proposals may no longer match the cluster’s current state. 

2.6.3. Postponed Merge-Request Handling 

When a boundary conflict cannot be resolved by local repair or by waiting for a deferred proposal, the current cluster may request a merge with a neighbouring cluster. However, if the neighbour still has internal repair options, the merge can be postponed. This avoids unnecessary cluster growth and lets each cluster resolve its own conflicts before merging. A merge request is handled conditionally. If the neighbouring cluster is still improving, it replies POSTPONE , and the requesting cluster waits for a later update and may reconsider. If the neighbouring cluster cannot improve further, it replies ACCEPT , and the two clusters may merge to solve the shared dependency jointly. This mechanism enables controlled cooperation between clusters. They merge only when local repair and deferred coordination are insufficient, so DCC-Incomplete keeps repair local when possible and widens its scope only when necessary. 

2.6.4. Conditional Cluster Merging 

Conditional cluster merging is used when two neighbouring conflict clusters are strongly coupled through unresolved boundary conflicts. When a merge is accepted, the clusters are replaced by a larger conflict cluster whose members are the union of the originals, and the conflict subgraph and coordination layer are rebuilt for the merged region. Let Kkt and Kht be neighbouring conflict clusters. If the merge is accepted, the new cluster is Kkh t = Kkt ∪ Kht . The latter receives an updated member set, boundary set, leader, context, and conflict load. Repair then continues within the merged cluster. Conditional merging lets the solver match the repair scope to the current conflict structure. If conflicts are local, clusters stay separate. If they span a boundary, the solver temporarily enlarges the scope so dependent variables are repaired together. 12 2.6.5. Local Reconstruction 

After each accepted value change, DCC-Incomplete updates the local conflict state. This is necessary because a single repair action can alter active agents, violated constraints, and conflict-cluster connections. Instead of rebuilding the entire problem from scratch, the solver reconstructs only the affected conflict structures using the updated assignment. The reconstruction step updates agent scores, active/passive agent sets, the conflict subgraph and clusters, cluster leaders, boundary sets, and coordination records. Solved clusters are removed. Repairs that create new conflicts can introduce new active agents or conflict edges. When the conflict structure changes, clusters may split, merge, or be replaced by new connected components. Local reconstruction keeps the model dynamic by ensuring repair targets the current conflict state rather than an outdated view of the problem. This process is implemented as in Algorithm 4. 

2.6.6. Stagnation Detection 

DCC-Incomplete tracks stagnation (Definition 15) using NoImproveCount kt

in the coordination layer record. If a repair attempt does not improve the cluster state, the counter increases; when an accepted improvement is committed, the counter resets. 

Definition 15 (Stagnation) . A conflict cluster is stagnating when repeated 2

repair attempts fail to reduce its conflict load. 

Stagnation detection is crucial because a repair-based incomplete solver may repeatedly revisit similar local states. If no improving move exists, continuing the same repair pattern wastes computation and communication. Thus, when NoImproveCount kt reaches a predefined threshold, the solver triggers a stagnation-recovery procedure. In the final DCC-Incomplete version, this is done via the lightweight learning method described in Section 2.7. Overall, the repair procedure combines decentralised value assessment with structured, cluster-level coordination. Individual agents evaluate their own candidate values, while designated leaders manage proposal selection, boundary checks, deferred processing, merge requests, reconstruction, and stagnation monitoring. This division of responsibilities preserves the agents’ distributed behavior, reduces unnecessary communication, and enables more informed repair decisions.

A purely random restart can help the solver escape stagnation, but it may also restart the cluster into recently explored or unproductive local assignments. DCC-Incomplete overcomes this problem through a lightweight memory-guided restart mechanism. Memory-guided restart does not perform an exhaustive memory-driven search like TS [27]; instead, it borrows TS ideas to limit cycling and repeated moves in local search by avoiding recently ineffective assignments during stagnation recovery. Thus, it serves as a short-term, learning-guided restart mechanism, local to the affected conflict cluster. In practice, each conflict cluster Kkt has a memory slot M kt that stores recently visited or unproductive local assignments αkt = {xi = vi | ai ∈ Members kt }. If the cluster fails to improve for a predefined number τ of repair attempts, its current local assignment is added to memory, M kt ← M kt ∪ { αkt } ( τ = 2 in this study). The restart is applied only to the stagnating conflict cluster, not the whole problem, keeping recovery local and avoiding changes to passive agents or pro-gressing clusters. During restart, the solver generates a temporary pool of ‘p’ candidate local assignments for variables in the stagnating cluster; 3 these are restart alternatives, not a training dataset. 3. Experimental Design 

We use a distributed graph colouring benchmark with planted satisfying assignments, which enables the evaluation of the solver on satisfiable DCSP instances while systematically controlling the graph size, constraint density, 17 and initial conflict structure. We first perform a functionality assessment of the proposed algorithm and then we evaluate it, along with one of its variants, and conduct a comparative analysis against three well-established distributed constraint reasoning baselines. 

3.1. Benchmark Problem: Distributed Graph Colouring 

The benchmark problem in this study is graph colouring [31], where colours are assigned to vertices so that adjacent vertices receive different colours. This can be formulated as a DCSP, where each vertex is represented by one agent, and each agent controls the colour of its corresponding variable. Formally, let 

G = ( V, E ) be an undirected graph with vertices V = {x1, . . . , x n}, where each 

xi is a variable owned by agent ai. Each variable has domain Di = {1, . . . , q }

of available colours. For every edge ( xi, x j ) ∈ E there is a binary constraint 

xi̸ = xj requiring adjacent variables to have different colours. An assignment α

is a valid colouring if α(xi)̸ = α(xj ) for all ( xi, x j ) ∈ E.Within the DCSP framework, a violated edge is equivalent to an unsatisfied binary constraint. This property facilitates the identification of conflict regions, as the connected components formed by violated edges inherently represent localised conflict structures. These structures can subsequently be exploited to analyse conflict cluster formation, perform repair operations, manage boundary coordination, and support reconstruction processes. The benchmark instances are satisfiable distributed graph-colouring problems with a planted solution: a valid colouring is generated first, and the graph is then built so that this colouring remains valid. Thus, every instance is known to be satisfiable. An unsuccessful run only means the solver failed to find a satisfying assignment within the time limit, not that the instance is unsatisfiable. We control three main factors: graph size, graph density, and random seed variation. Graph size is set by the number of variables n, density by four graph families, and random variation by the initial-assignment seeds (see Table 1 for details).                        

> Table 1: Benchmark graph families used in the evaluation.

The instance family identifier “S” denotes sparse instances, “M” medium-density instances, “D” dense instances, and “XD” extremely dense stress-test instances. The XD family is included to test solver behaviour under challenging connectivity, not to represent the main operating setting. Regarding the edge 18 factor, it determines the number of edges generated in the graph. A higher edge factor results in a greater number of constraint edges, thereby increasing the density of the benchmark instance and typically making it more difficult to solve. For each graph size and family, multiple graph seeds generate different structures, and multiple initial-assignment seeds generate different starting assignments for the same planted solution. This separates the effects of graph structure from those of the initial conflict configuration. Overall, the benchmark comprises 72 instances: 3 graph sizes across 4 families, with 3 graph seeds and 2 initial-assignment seeds per configuration. Planted satisfying assignments are crucial for interpreting the results: since every instance has at least one valid solution, the success rate reflects the solver’s ability to find a satisfying assignment within the given search budget. 

3.2. Selected algorithms for testing and comparison 

We compare 5 algorithms, including the DCC-Incomplete solver under two configurations and 3 state-of-the-art baselines. A list with algorithmic details is provided below. • DCC-Incomplete-random : the proposed solver configured with a ran-dom local restart for stagnation recovery.This facilitates assessing the contribution introduced by the “memory_guided” module. • DCC-Incomplete : the proposed version using memory-guided restart for stagnation recovery. • Distributed Stochastic Algorithm (DSA) [3]: an incomplete dis-tributed local-search algorithm in which agents probabilistically change values according to local improvement. • Maximum Gain Message algorithm (MGM) [20]: an incomplete distributed local-search algorithm in which agents exchange “gain” infor-mation and move only when they have the highest local gain. • Generalized Distributed Breakout Algorithm (GDBA) [22]: abreakout-based distributed method that adapts constraint weights to escape local minima. These baselines were chosen as established and incomplete distributed constraint-reasoning methods based on different working mechanism. DSA and MGM are classical distributed local-search baselines, while GDBA is a more advanced breakout-style baseline. 19 3.3. Evaluation Metrics 

We consider both solution-quality and efficiency metrics. A run is successful if the final cost is zero, indicating a satisfying assignment in the graph-colouring DCSP. If the final cost is greater than zero, the run is unsuccessful under the given stopping limit. The evaluation metrics are • Success rate (SR): percentage of instances with final null score. • Runtime (ρ): the wall-clock time (in seconds) required to terminate. • Iter. : the number of repairs that occurred before the problem was resolved or the maximum allowed number of iterations TM AX was reached. • Messages (NM ): the total number of explicit communication events recorded during solving. • Messages per variable (NM/V ): the total number of messages normalised by the number of variables. • Messages per constraint (NM/C ): the total number of messages nor-malised by the number of constraints. • Number of Restart (NR): the number of restarts triggered during a run. • Final cost (FC): the number of unsatisfied constraints at termination. • Solved cases (NSC ): the number of instances solved successfully. For outlier-sensitive metrics (runtime, iterations, messages, messages per variable, and messages per constraint), median values are reported, since unsuc-cessful or difficult runs can sometimes produce unusually large communication or runtime values (outliers). Final cost (FC) is also reported using the median in the main comparison tables. For the external baselines, DSA, MGM, and GDBA are each executed 10 times over the complete benchmark. Within each execution, success rate is calculated over the corresponding benchmark cases, while the remaining numerical metrics are reported as medians. The final baseline values are then obtained by averaging the 10 execution-level results. When specifically discussing the internal effect of memory-guided restart (Table 13, and 14), mean FC (denote as F C ) is additionally reported because it summarises the average remaining conflict level across all runs and highlights the reduction in residual violations. The number of restarts, denoted by NR, and the number of solved cases, denoted by NSC , are additionally reported where relevant to the internal DCC-Incomplete analysis. 20 3.4. Experimental settings and considerations 

All methods are evaluated on the same generated benchmark instances where applicable. For the DCC-Incomplete variants, we use identical graph structures, planted satisfying assignments, and initial assignments, allowing a direct comparison of the restart mechanism by evaluating the random-restart and memory-guided variants under identical conditions. DSA, MGM, and GDBA are executed using the pyDCOP framework 4. The same graph-colouring instances are converted into the required distributed constraint-reasoning format. A run is successful when the final cost is zero, matching the graph-colouring DCSP setting where zero cost means no inequality constraint is violated. We run DSA, MGM, and GDBA 10 times per problem to assess reliability (standard deviation). The comparison is interpreted cautiously because the proposed solver and the baselines use different internal execution models. DCC-Incomplete relies on repair iterations over a dynamic conflict cluster, whereas the other methods use solver cycles defined by their own algorithms and frameworks. Thus, iteration and cycle counts are reported for transparency but are not the primary criteria for assessing solver quality. Message counts are interpreted cautiously. DCC-Incomplete records explicit messages from its coordination and repair procedures, while the chosen state-of-the-art methods implement different mechanisms that may alter exact counts. Therefore, communication results are analysed mainly via overall trends and normalised measures such as messages per variable and per constraint. DCC-Incomplete, DCC-Incomplete-random, DSA, and MGM were evaluated using a stopping budget of 150 iterations or cycles ( Tmax = 150), where applicable. GDBA was evaluated using a strict 10-second timeout, because the installed pyDCOP implementation does not support stop_cycle for GDBA. We remark that the complete source code used to generate these results is made publicly available in [30].

We emphasise that GDBA does not support a cycle-based stopping mechanism and instead relies exclusively on a strict timeout criterion (10 seconds in our experiments 6). For this algorithm, the maximum iteration count is therefore not applicable. Although this is not an issue for many instances – since the algorithm often terminates successfully before completing 150 iterations – it may lead to slightly unfair comparisons in certain cases. Furthermore, the DCC repair iterations are not directly comparable to the cycles counted by pyDCOP.

Overall, our numerical results confirm DCC-Incomplete as a promising repair-based approach for solving DCSPs, featuring a substantially lower communication overhead than established traditional and state-of-the-art algorithms. It is highly competitive, consistently achieving the highest overall success rate and lowest median final cost among the algorithms in this study. Our experimentation verifies its correct functioning and proves that all of its novel modules contribute positively in terms of performance. It is interesting to note, that despite being less effective without memory-guided restarts, the DCC-Incomplete-random variant still outperforms established algorithms such as DSA, GDBA, and MGM in overall success rate, demonstrating that this new framework is highly effective and provides a strong foundation for future research. 

5. Conclusion and Future Work 

Our results demonstrate that the proposed algorithm constitutes an efficient, incomplete, repair-based solver that overcomes two major limitations affecting current distributed constraint-solving approaches, such as: (i) high commu-nication overhead arising from broad or redundant message passing, and (ii) insufficient support for strategic coordination and learning during the repair process. 42 When evaluated on controlled distributed graph-colouring benchmarks with planted satisfying assignments, the solver demonstrated its efficiency. In par-ticular, its memory-guided variant outperformed the random-restart variant by increasing the success rate from 37.50% to 61.11%, reducing the median runtime from 0.565 seconds to 0.367 seconds, and decreasing the median com-munication overhead from 12.493 to 6.503 messages per constraint. An external comparison further indicated that DCC-Incomplete achieved the strongest overall performance among the evaluated methods, exhibiting a higher success rate and substantially lower communication overhead than state-of-the-art algorithms such as DSA, MGM, and GDBA on the tested benchmark set. In this study, we additionally demonstrate that the integration of dynamic conflict-based decomposition with structured coordination mechanisms and lightweight, memory-guided recovery strategies can enhance the performance of incomplete DCSP solving. Furthermore, we show that the proposed solver preserves distributed agent-level repair while adding cluster-level organisation to reduce unnecessary communication and improve robustness against stagnation. Future work includes: (1) evaluating the solver on broader benchmarks, such as varied domain sizes, non-binary constraints, heterogeneous agent structures, and real-world distributed constraint problems; (2) conducting a more systematic parameter-sensitivity analysis of the memory-guided restart mechanism (memory length, candidate pool size, scoring weights); (3) performing additional ablation studies to isolate the impact of each component—conflict cluster formation, boundary coordination, merge handling, and memory-guided restart; and (4) extending the incomplete solver to a complete or hybrid variant that combines conflict clustering with stronger search guarantees.
