# Collective Learning for Unified Robotic Manipulation via Predictive Dynamics Adaptation 

Shaocheng Liu 1, Yuan Meng 1, Zhenshan Bing 2,†, Alois Knoll 1

Abstract — Learning a collective policy for state-based Carte-sian manipulation across diverse robotic embodiments presents a fundamental challenge due to embodiment-dependent tran-sition dynamics. Collective learning aggregates multi-robot experience to discover shared task structure, yet naive aggre-gation can average incompatible action modes when different embodiments realize the same Cartesian command through different transition behavior. This leads to strategy ambiguity and performance collapse on dynamically outlying embodi-ments. To address this, we propose PACE, a two-stage collective learning framework that separates shared task-space intent from embodiment-dependent transition dynamics. The first stage employs a representation layer pre-trained on offline trajectories to extract task-level context from collective data. The second stage introduces a decision layer equipped with a predictive dynamics adapter. By forcing the latent state to predict future transitions, this adapter acts as a latent regularizer that grounds the policy in the transition behavior of the executing robot while preserving real-time inference without online planning. Evaluations across simulated embodiments demonstrate that our method prevents performance collapse on distinct robots. It achieves over 93% overall success and 89% on dynamically out-of-distribution zero-shot simulated robots, outperforming the Transformer behavior cloning (82%) and diffusion policy baselines (66%). Physical KUKA deployment further validates the feasibility of the full state-based pipeline under zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Developing generalist robotic agents capable of mastering diverse manipulation tasks across heterogeneous embodi-ments remains a central objective in embodied AI. Recent large-scale robot learning efforts show that policies trained on collective data from many robots can acquire reusable task structure and improve transfer across platforms [1], [2]. Such collective data enables a policy to acquire reusable task struc-ture beyond the experience of any individual robot. However, cross-embodiment collective learning also introduces hetero-geneous sensing modalities, kinematic structures, and phys-ical execution behaviors [3], [4] Achieving robust control across such heterogeneous embodiments therefore requires learning what can be shared across robots while preserving embodiment-specific factors that affect execution. Recent works increasingly train shared policies on collec-tive robot data to overcome the limitations of isolated single-robot training and to discover reusable structures across embodiments [5], [6]. Training a single policy on such diverse dynamics leads to strategy ambiguity. Because the optimization process minimizes the expected error by averaging conflicting actuation modes, it ultimately generates suboptimal actions [4]. Fully realizing the potential of collective learning thus requires bridging these low-level dynamic gaps by isolating shared task se-mantics from specific hardware physics. Dynamics-aware collective learning benefits from sep-arating what should be shared across robots from what should remain embodiment-aware. To achieve this, we pro-pose a two-stage collective learning framework illustrated in Figure 1. The learning pipeline begins with a diverse multi-embodiment dataset covering various tasks and robot morphologies. The core collective network processes this data through two functional stages. First, a representation layer pre-trained on offline data extracts an embodiment-agnostic task context via masked trajectory reconstruction and contrastive learning [7], [8]. Second, a decision layer employs a predictive dynamics adapter as a latent regularizer to enable precise low-level control [9]. By forcing the latent arXiv:2610.19141v1 [cs.RO] 13 Oct 2026 state to predict future transitions, the policy implicitly adapts to the physical constraints of the current robot. This design ultimately yields robust cross-embodiment generalization, allowing the policy to control unseen simulated robots and physical hardware. Furthermore, it avoids the computational latency associated with the online simulation of traditional world models [10], [11]. As a result, the system executes in real time while achieving an overall success rate of over 93% and maintaining an 89% success rate on challenging robots with out-of-distribution dynamics, effectively preventing the severe performance collapse observed in the Transformer behavior cloning (73%) and diffusion (66%) baselines. The main contributions of our study are three folds:  

> •

We formulate dynamics-aware collective manipulation under a shared state-based Cartesian interface, where multi-robot data provides shared task structure while embodiment-dependent transition behavior creates strat-egy ambiguity.  

> •

We introduce a dual-objective latent regularizer that utilizes reward prediction to anchor the representation in task semantics and applies auxiliary next-step prediction to enforce adaptation across diverse manipulators.  

> •

We conduct extensive empirical evaluations demonstrat-ing that the proposed method prevents performance collapse on outlier robots, outperforms standard be-havior cloning baselines, and enables zero-shot transfer capabilities on previously unseen physical hardware. Recent studies rely on collective and fleet learning to train models across diverse robotic platforms [5], [6], [12]. Early large-scale parallel data collection systems, which used arrays of identical manipulators to autonomously learn shared grasping skills, established the foundation for this ap-proach [13]. Modern frameworks expand on this concept by aggregating experiences across multiple hardware instances, allowing robots to learn generalizable manipulation skills without isolated training. Recent cross-embodiment models, for example, train generalist agents by processing extensive visual observations combined with natural language instruc-tions [1], [2]. In parallel, hardware-centric approaches use universal interfaces to standardize the physical control space [14]. To handle structural differences within multi-robot datasets, current architectures align disparate state spaces into a shared latent space [15], [16] or apply heterogeneous pre-trained transformers [4], [17]. Although these methods provide a strong basis for semantic generalization across robot fleets, they frequently struggle to solve the low-level dynamic discrepancies that arise when transferring policies to hardware with significantly different inertial properties. To model action distributions, recent studies adopt se-quence modeling and predictive architectures. Masked tra-jectory models reconstruct sequences of states and actions to learn representations applicable to control [7]. Advanced autoregressive and diffusion frameworks further refine the generation of trajectories [18], [19], while contrastive learn-ing disentangles task intent from morphological noise [8]. Concurrently, model-based reinforcement learning captures transitions of the environment through explicit predictive world models [10], [11], [20]. Although explicit world mod-els can capture system dynamics, they typically rely on itera-tive internal simulation for online planning. This mechanism introduces substantial computational latency during execu-tion and requires active environmental interaction to adapt to new physical properties. Consequently, the application of explicit world models is fundamentally restricted in zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer, where policies must execute in real time from strict offline demonstrations. The proposed framework avoids this limitation by utilizing predictive dy-namics exclusively as a latent regularizer, thereby enabling real-time physical adaptation without the computational over-head of online planning. B. System overview 

This two-stage framework (Fig. 2) distills heterogeneous expert policies into a single dynamics-aware collective agent. Stage 1 extracts task-level context from collective trajec-tories, capturing the shared structure that should transfer across embodiments. Stage 2 uses predictive latent dynamics to preserve the embodiment-dependent transition behavior needed for reliable control. This design allows the policy to exploit multi-robot data while avoiding the action averaging that causes strategy ambiguity in pooled behavior cloning.

While the Representation Layer extracts a semantic con-text ct, it lacks the explicit physical grounding required to control diverse embodiments (e.g., differentiating high-inertia industrial arms from lightweight cobots). The De-cision Layer bridges this gap using a Predictive Dynamics Adapter to refine the latent state zt.Unlike standard World Models [11] [10] that use learned dynamics for planning or synthetic rollouts, we use the predictive model strictly as a representation regularizer. By minimizing the next-step prediction error Ldyn in latent space, we force zt zt to encode robot-specific transition dynamics within a latent world model framework. This allows the policy πϕ(at|zt, c t) to remain model-free during inference, avoiding online planning latency while retaining the benefits of physics-aware representations. Our Predictive Dynamics Adapter operates alongside a re-ward prediction module. The reward model ensures the latent space retains task-critical semantic features. The dynamics model simultaneously adapts the representation to specific physical transition delays. This dual structure prevents the dynamics objective from overshadowing the core manipula-tion intent.

This research introduces a two-stage collective learning framework to resolve dynamic heterogeneity in multi-robot manipulation. The system combines a representation layer for extracting task intent with a decision layer containing a predictive dynamics adapter, effectively decoupling semantic objectives from physical transition rules. Empirical evalu-ations show this predictive regularization prevents perfor-mance degradation on kinematic outliers and enables zero-shot transfer to physical hardware like the KUKA manipula-tor. Although a Cartesian action space prioritizes workspace geometry, this abstraction bypasses joint-level constraints and kinematic singularities, occasionally causing execution stalls. Future work should integrate joint state feedback directly into the predictive dynamics adapter to ensure joint-level compliance. Finally, broader quantitative testing in multi-tasks will help establish a rigorous understanding of system boundaries and deployment capabilities. VI. A CKNOWLEDGMENT
