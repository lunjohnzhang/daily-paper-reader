Robotic swarms must coordinate across diverse missions, yet existing con-trollers often require redesign, retuning, or retraining when objectives change. Here we show that probabilistic energy modeling provides a unifying repre-sentation for versatile swarm control, in which diverse missions emerge from compositions of shared interaction principles rather than task-specific control formulations. By exploiting the correspondence between Gibbs random field en-ergy minimization and structured reward maximization, we encode fundamental swarm behaviors as reusable energy terms within a unified probabilistic objec-tive. Decentralized policies trained within this representation are composed into a single controller through lightweight policy gating, without retraining. An in-terpretable action-attention mechanism captures non-uniform local interactions, while dimensionless normalization supports transfer across swarm scales and plat-forms. Demonstrated on aerial swarms, the unified policy generalizes zero-shot across six mission classes, 10–100 agents, two hardware platforms, and indoor clut-tered and GPS-denied woodland environments. Large-scale simulations achieve 0.96–1.00 task success rates with 1–10 ms per-agent inference latency, while phys-ical deployments confirm zero-shot transfer to real platforms and unstructured

# Introduction 

Biological swarms coordinate collective motion while switching fluidly among flocking, forag-ing, and navigation through local interactions ( 1–5 ). This functional flexibility has long motivated robotic swarms, which combine multiple autonomous vehicles for exploration and monitoring and offer potential advantages in robustness, scalability, and efficiency over single-robot systems ( 6–9 ). A substantial gap nevertheless remains between the heterogeneous demands of multi-phase mis-sions and current swarm systems, which are largely organized around task-specific designs ( 6, 7 ). Controllers are typically developed and evaluated around a single coordination objective, such as optimized flocking ( 10, 11 ) or formation control ( 12, 13 ), and their reuse across heterogeneous missions is often limited. Onboard resource constraints, together with uncertain sensing and com-munication, further complicate coordinated motion across environments ( 7, 14, 15 ). The central challenge is therefore not to improve any single-task controller, but to identify a shared mathemat-ical representation in which diverse mission objectives can be expressed and reused, and to train a reusable decentralized policy that retains real-time execution. Existing swarm control approaches face a persistent trade-off among coordination perfor-mance, computational demand, and cross-task reuse. Rule-based methods, including Reynolds-style interaction primitives, velocity vector fields, and artificial potential fields, provide decen-tralized scalability and low computational cost ( 10, 16–18 ), but their heuristic structure usually requires scenario-specific tuning and can degrade when operating conditions depart from de-sign assumptions ( 9, 14, 16 ). Optimization-based frameworks, particularly Model Predictive Con-trol, explicitly incorporate vehicle dynamics, safety constraints, and task objectives and achieve strong performance in specialized settings ( 11,12,14,15,19–21 ). However, online optimization and neighbor-trajectory exchange must fit limited onboard resources and real-time execution require-ments ( 15, 19, 22 ). Learning-based swarm control, including multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), shifts much of the computational burden offline and enables high-speed neural inference during deployment ( 23–28 ). While learning-based flocking control and its performance trade-offs 2have been established in prior work ( 29 ), learned policies are typically optimized for individual tasks: extending them to new objectives often requires redesigning rewards, retraining policies, or deploying separate controllers. Thus, despite advances in efficient decentralized control and policy learning, a common representation that supports systematic reuse across heterogeneous swarm objectives remains less explored. The present work moves beyond single-task learned flocking toward a unified representation that supports multiple mission classes, modular policy integration, cross-platform deployment, and interpretable interaction analysis. This cross-task reuse bottleneck sits within a broader trend toward modular and compositional policy architectures in artificial intelligence ( 30–33 ). For swarm systems, it must be reconciled with structural coherence during real-world deployment and sim-to-real transfer under environmental uncertainty ( 9, 14, 27 ), while retaining a simple interface for high-level human or task-level guidance ( 34 ). Here we show that probabilistic energy modeling provides a unifying representation for this versatility. We build on Gibbs Random Fields (GRFs), in which swarm coordination objectives are expressed as a common probabilistic energy composed of reusable unary terms for robot-to-environment interactions and pairwise terms for robot-to-robot interactions. Cohesion, collision avoidance, velocity alignment, formation regulation, and task-specific guidance are encoded as en-ergy terms within one shared objective rather than as independent rewards designed for each task. We leverage the direct correspondence between GRF energy minimization and reward maximiza-tion: minimizing the Gibbs energy corresponds to maximizing a structured reward, so the same energy formulation yields consistent reward functions across missions. These energy-based rewards train decentralized specialist policies with MARL, and an action-attention mechanism enables in-terpretable, non-uniform weighting of neighboring agents while preserving fully decentralized execution and inference independent of swarm size. Dimensionless observation normalization provides a scale-consistent interface for high-level commands. The result is a single integrated controller, from which distinct mission capabilities arise by composing shared coordination energy terms with task-specific guidance potentials, and new capabilities can be incorporated without mod-ifying previously learned policies. Although demonstrated on aerial platforms, the energy-based formulation is not inherently tied to a specific robotic platform. By unifying diverse objectives within a single probabilistic energy framework and demonstrating zero-shot transfer across tasks, scales, and platforms, this work establishes a representation layer upon which large-scale pre-trained 3swarm policies—analogous to foundation models in language and vision—could be constructed in the future. We validate the framework through large-scale simulation benchmarks and physical deploy-ments spanning four axes of generalization: mission class, swarm scale, hardware platform, and environment. In simulation, the unified policy attains task-execution success rates of 0 .96–1 .00 across traversal settings ranging from sparse 10-agent, 10-obstacle scenarios to congested 100-agent, 50-obstacle configurations, while per-agent inference remains 𝑂 (1) in swarm size at 1–10 ms. Physical deployments on 29-g Crazyflie micro-quadrotors and custom-built outdoor quadrotors evaluate traversal, high-speed racing, persistent patrol, pattern formation, distributed exploration, and an integrated multi-phase outdoor workflow—without retraining or fine-tuning. Experiments span indoor passages, obstacle-dense arenas, and GPS-denied woodlands, with the same policy transferring directly across platforms and environments. Collectively, these results demonstrate zero-shot generalization across six mission classes, swarm scales from 10 to 100 agents, two hardware platforms, and indoor and GPS-denied outdoor environments, supporting reusable de-centralized swarm coordination across diverse operating conditions.

## Versatile swarm control across missions 

Building on these elementary behaviors, we next ask whether the unified policy can support qualitatively different missions under varying demands on motion, spatial organization, sensing, and resilience. We consider traversal and racing in cluttered environments, persistent patrol and pattern formation, and distributed exploration under agent loss and changing swarm scale. Across these tasks, the same learned policy adapts its collective configuration and local interactions to task demands without task-specific retraining.

The swarm deforms its topology in narrow passages while maintaining collision-free motion and centroid tracking (Fig. 3A(ii)). Except for corridor-induced elongation between 20–40 s, the mean inter-agent distance remains close to the 0.5 m equilibrium, with nor-malized variance below 0.1 (Fig. 3A(iii)). Distributed exploration under failures and scale variation. Finally, we test whether the 10 learned coordination layer remains effective under limited sensing and changing swarm composition (Fig. 5). A hierarchical planner supplies cooperative objectives while the coordination layer executes local motion. Each UAV has a 0 .75 m sensing radius, and a location counts as explored only when covered concurrently by at least two UAVs. With eight UAVs, the swarm splits into two subgroups and reaches 97 .7% coverage within 50 s with comparable contributions from the two subgroups (Fig. 5A(i),B–C). The same coordination layer adapts to agent and subgroup loss. When one UAV in each subgroup fails at 𝑇 = 28 .5 s, the survivors redistribute and reach 98 .2% coverage without an evident interruption in cumulative coverage (Fig. 5A(ii),B–C). When an entire subgroup drops out at 𝑇 = 21 .9 s, exploration slows but continues without deadlock, reaching 91.1% coverage with the remaining subgroup (Fig. 5A(iii),B–C). Increasing to 12 UAVs in three subgroups reveals a density–congestion trade-off: denser initial deployment slows early exploration, but the additional sensing capacity ultimately yields 99 .7% coverage (Fig. 5A(iv),B). Overall, these experiments show that the unified policy provides reusable coordination across distinct motion, spatial-allocation, and exploration regimes, with task-dependent collective config-urations emerging without task-specific retraining, within the tested conditions.

Attention thus encodes both spatial organization and kinematic context, not distance alone. Attention peaks at short range and near the reference spacing 𝑑 ref (Fig. 7B,C). As with inferred interaction forces in fish schools ( 36 ), these weights encode information allocation rather than physical force. ## Zero-shot transfer across platforms and environments 

The generality of the learned coordination policy was further tested through zero-shot deployment beyond the training distribution. Without fine-tuning or retraining, the same policy was deployed on custom-built quadrotors across indoor, open outdoor, and GPS-denied woodland environments (Fig. 8), enabled by the dimensionless observation representation that provides a common input across physical scales. Across indoor, open outdoor, and dense woodland environments, the swarm maintains coher-ent and collision-free motion despite changes in geometry, sensing conditions, and aerodynamic disturbances (Fig. 8A,B). The policy also transfers from micro-quadrotors to larger custom-built platforms, which perform formation reconfiguration and subgroup cooperative patrol (Fig. 8C,D). The same policy further adapts to changing mission conditions without modification. In an un-mapped environment, eight UAVs divide into two subgroups for exploration; after detecting a target, one UAV maintains surveillance while the others continue searching (Fig. 8E). Finally, the swarm combines traversal, regrouping, and formation transformation within a single mission (Fig. 8F). Together, these field experiments demonstrate that the unified policy transfers zero-shot across platforms, environments, and mission phases without task-specific retraining.
