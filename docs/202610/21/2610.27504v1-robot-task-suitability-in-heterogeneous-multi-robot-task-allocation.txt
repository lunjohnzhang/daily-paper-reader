# Robot–Task Suitability in Heterogeneous Multi-Robot Task Allocation 

Daniel Weiner 1 and Raj Korpan 2

Abstract — Heterogeneous multi-robot task allocation de-pends on estimates of how suitable individual robots are for candidate tasks, yet suitability can be defined in substan-tially different ways. We compare ten deterministic methods and two LLM-based variants spanning rule-based, similarity-based, requirement-oriented, fuzzy, and semantic approaches. Using common robot and task profiles, we compare how the methods score and rank candidate robots, identify feasibility, and select a top robot, while examining how these outcomes change with team composition, scale, spatial context, capability uncertainty, and task representation. The methods frequently disagree despite operating on identical scenarios. Generic similarity measures can violate properties expected under a one-sided requirement-satisfaction interpretation of suitabil-ity, while LLM-based scoring introduces additional stochastic and linguistic sensitivity. These results show that robot–task suitability should be treated as an explicit modeling choice whose assumptions, robustness, and decision-level consequences should be considered before allocation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION AND RELATED WORK 

Multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) concerns assigning tasks to robots subject to constraints on resources, timing, motion, and team objectives. MRTA has been studied through centralized optimization, market- and auction-based meth-ods, distributed coordination, heuristics, and learning-based approaches [1], [2], [3], [4]. The problem is especially chal-lenging for heterogeneous teams, whose robots may differ in mobility, sensing, manipulation, payload, reach, endurance, communication, and environmental tolerance [5]. Existing heterogeneous MRTA approaches represent these differences through capability constraints, utilities, energy-aware allo-cation, resilience mechanisms, or task-specific requirements [6], [7], [8], [9]. Regardless of the allocation mechanism, however, the system must determine how appropriate each robot is for each task [10], [11]. We use the term robot–task suitability to describe how well a particular robot matches the requirements of a particular task, including considerations such as feasibility, specialization, and expected performance. Constructing suitability values is itself a nontrivial mod-eling problem. Robot capabilities and task requirements may be numerical, categorical, contextual, unstructured, or uncertain, and some requirements are mandatory while others permit partial satisfaction or tradeoffs. Prior work has considered uncertain or changing capabilities and task requirements [14], [15], while adaptive specialization meth-ods model robot efficacy as initially unknown or changing during deployment [11], [8]. Reducing these factors to a scalar therefore requires assumptions about hard vs. soft con-straints, capability weighting, surplus vs. deficient capability, and whether operational context is part of suitability. Different methods encode these assumptions differently. Rule-based methods can explicitly evaluate requirements and reject infeasible pairs; vector-based approaches compare robots and tasks through geometric similarity; requirement-oriented methods measure satisfaction or deficiency; and fuzzy methods allow gradual satisfaction near numerical thresholds. Multi-objective MRTA likewise reflects the need to balance competing aspects of robot suitability [16], [17], [18], [19]. More recently, large language models (LLMs) have been used to reason over heterogeneous robot capa-bilities and natural-language task descriptions [20], [21], [22]. Such methods offer semantic flexibility but intro-duce stochasticity and sensitivity to linguistic representation. These approaches encode different notions of compatibility, making their effects difficult to isolate when they are evalu-ated using different robot, task, and allocation models. This work therefore examines suitability estimation as a distinct component of heterogeneous MRTA rather than proposing a new allocation algorithm. We compare ten de-terministic methods, including rule-based, similarity-based, requirement-oriented, set-based, and fuzzy approaches, to-gether with two LLM-based variants, using common robot and task profiles. We evaluate agreement and robustness under changes in team composition, scale, robot-to-task ratio, spatial scale, and capability perturbations; test five one-sided suitability properties; examine task-dependent behavior; and evaluate LLM repeatability and semantic robustness. The experiments show that suitability method affects feasibility judgments and robot rankings, that several generic similarity measures exhibit behaviors inconsistent with one-sided re-quirement satisfaction, and that semantic scoring introduces additional uncertainty. These findings motivate treating suit-ability estimation as an explicit modeling decision rather than an assumed input to downstream task allocation. II. M ETHODOLOGY AND EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

This further shows that similar score magnitudes do not imply similar robot rankings. IV. D ISCUSSION 

The central implication of these experiments is that suitability methods encode meaningfully different notions of robot–task compatibility. Rule-based and requirement-oriented methods emphasize whether task requirements are satisfied, whereas Euclidean, cosine, and Jaccard characterize broader similarity between robot and task representations. The property tests make this distinction explicit: Euclidean, cosine, and Jaccard penalized task-irrelevant added capabili-ties, while Euclidean and cosine frequently reduced suitabil-ity when task requirements were relaxed. These behaviors are reasonable for symmetric similarity measures but conflict with a one-sided requirement-satisfaction interpretation in which additional capability should not hurt and an easier task should not become less suitable. The rule-based, coverage, fuzzy, and shortfall methods satisfied all tested properties, although this does not establish that they better predict execution outcomes. Suitability measures should therefore be selected according to the semantics and behavioral properties required by the allocation system rather than solely for mathematical convenience or numerical agreement. The heterogeneity and task-specific experiments further show that the consequences of method choice depend on team composition and candidate availability. Adding dis-tinct robot archetypes increased the best available estimated suitability and often made specialist relationships more pro-nounced, whereas increasing variation within an archetype generally reduced compatibility and cross-method agree-ment. Increasing the number of robots similarly improved the best available match while reducing agreement about which individual robot should be preferred, suggesting that method choice becomes more consequential as several plau-sible candidates compete under different capability tradeoffs. Task-level results support this interpretation: agreement was stronger for tasks associated with distinctive specialists and weaker for tasks, such as brick laying, that admit multiple plausible capability strategies. Thus, heterogeneity can both improve the quality of available matches and increase ambi-guity about how those matches should be ranked. The spatial and perturbation experiments motivate adistinction between intrinsic capability compatibility and 

contextual suitability . Methods incorporating distance, en-durance, sensing, or navigation changed substantially with map scale, whereas more static capability-similarity mea-sures changed little. A robot may therefore possess the hardware needed for a task while still being a poor cur-rent candidate because of location or operating conditions, suggesting value in separating relatively static compatibility from dynamic feasibility or execution cost. The perturbation results highlight the importance of evaluating suitability at the level at which it will be used: small score changes can still alter the preferred robot when candidates are closely matched, while larger score changes may leave the allocation decision unchanged. Because suitability is ultimately used to support allocation decisions, ranking margins, top-choice stability, and feasibility changes may be more informative than score correlation alone. LLM-based suitability introduces additional uncertainty from both stochastic inference and linguistic representation. Structured prompting reduced score-level variability relative to direct scalar judgments, suggesting that decomposing compatibility into explicit capability dimensions can regu-larize the generated values, but it did not improve ranking or top-choice stability. Scalar scoring showed higher apparent decision stability, although its much larger number of tied maxima makes this comparison difficult to interpret. Para-phrasing otherwise equivalent task descriptions also changed allocation-relevant rankings, demonstrating that linguistic representation becomes part of the suitability function. Gem-ini did not reproduce any deterministic method, but its rankings agreed more strongly with requirement-oriented balanced, fuzzy, and coverage scoring than with generic geo-metric similarity measures. Although this does not reveal the model’s internal reasoning, it suggests Gemini’s suitability judgments align more with inferred requirement satisfac-tion than feature-space similarity. LLM-generated suitability should therefore be treated as a distinct and uncertain repre-sentation rather than as a deterministic approximation of con-ventional capability matching; operational use may require standardized descriptions, repeated sampling, aggregation, or allocation methods that explicitly account for uncertainty. These conclusions are limited by the absence of execution-derived ground truth. The experiments characterize agree-ment, robustness, and behavioral properties rather than pre-dictive accuracy, so higher estimated suitability cannot be interpreted as improved real-world task performance. The robot and task archetypes are manually constructed and cover a finite set of capability combinations, while the decision-level analysis considers top-robot selection rather than com-plete allocations involving contention, coalitions, scheduling, or other coupled constraints. Hard-feasibility methods also require special treatment when every candidate receives zero suitability, since a nominal arg max then does not represent a meaningful feasible choice. Future work should evaluate these methods against execution outcomes such as success, completion time, energy use, and safety; expand the range and scale of robot and task profiles; and measure how suitability disagreement propagates into complete MRTA solutions. Overall, robot–task suitability should be treated as an explicit modeling choice whose assumptions, context, and uncertainty are explicit and matched to the needs of the allocation system.
