Belief-Driven Search in Connectivity-Constrained Mobile Sensor
Networks with Convergence Guarantees
George J. Ziavras, Ziqiao Zhang, Shreyas Sundaram and Christopher G. Brinton
Abstract— Multi-agent search and coverage is critical to
autonomous exploration, search-and-rescue, and defense appli-
cations. This paper investigates settings where a mobile sensor
network is tasked with coordinating to discover the position
of a stationary target within a bounded region. We develop a
Bayesian update scheme where agents iteratively combine local
uncertainties derived from their sensor measurements through
inverse variance weighting to get a better global understanding
of where the target may be located given some prior belief.
Network agents’ trajectories are governed by a primal-dual
gradient update rule that simultaneously minimizes the ex-
pected sensing uncertainty and enforces network connectivity
among the agents. We prove that the belief converges to
the target under mild, planner-agnostic conditions, with an
explicit exponential rate of false-hypothesis rejection and a
bound on expected search time. Simulation results show that
we can successfully find the target and collapse belief to the
ground truth target location while ensuring connectivity of our
sensor network. They also empirically confirm our theoretical
bounds and revisiting assumption. Finally, we compare against
a lawnmower sweep, showing that our method locates the target
more quickly when the prior is informative.
I. INTRODUCTION
Autonomous multi-agent systems are increasingly vital for
operations in high-stakes, uncertain environments, ranging
from autonomous scientific exploration and environmental
monitoring to search-and-rescue and defense applications.
A common objective in these settings is to locate a target
of interest whose position within some region is uncertain.
We build on related coverage control to establish an active
search algorithm that performs this task. Coverage control
often seeks to distribute stationary agents based on a fixed
importance map to optimize some cost or objective. In
contrast, active search benefits from agents actively learning,
adjusting their beliefs of the environment based on their
sensor feedback, to approach a target’s localization. Fig. 1
illustrates one such use case.
Key Challenges. Multi-robot search under communication
constraints poses two core difficulties:
1. Information gain versus network connectivity. To max-
imize the rate of information gain, uncertainty minimization
incentivizes agents to spread out and cover unvisited re-
gions. Fusing measurements and maintaining a shared belief,
however, requires a connected communication network. 2.
The need for tractable, differentiable constraints. We seek

Main Contributions. Our contributions are as follows.
• Inverse-Variance Sensor Fusion: We develop a sensing
model that replaces Voronoi partitioning with a smoother
global measurement uncertainty, enabling more flexible
gradient-based control. This uncertainty also defines detec-
tion probabilities given some fixed false-alarm rate, driving
an exact Bayesian update of the belief.
• Connectivity-Constrained Planning: We encode network
connectivity as a differentiable constraint, solving the
resulting planning problem with a primal-dual gradient
method, showing empirically in simulation that a team
starting connected remains connected.
• Convergence Analysis: We prove that, for any planner that
does not anticipate future measurements, the probability
of ever confirming a wrong location is explicitly bounded.
We introduce Aggregated Global Identifiability (AGI), a
weaker revisiting condition than full coverage (cells with
non-negligible belief, or the target cell, must be sensed
within each finite window). Under this condition, the belief
concentrates on the target. Belief in false locations decays
exponentially at a rate determined by the sensor’s false-
alarm probabilities and missed-detection threshold, and we
bound the expected search time.

II. RELATED WORKS
Distributed search & coverage control has traditionally
used Voronoi partitions to tessellate the search space [1]–
[4]. Density-Driven Optimal Control (D2OC) instead ap-
plies optimal transport to match agent distributions to non-
uniform priority maps [5], but assumes these maps are static
or prescribed. AI-augmented extensions update them under
uncertain priors [6] at the cost of added computation and
training. Effective coverage control [7] guarantees that every
point is sensed above a capacity threshold, using a strict
maximum sensing radius and a measurement-driven penalty
over a fixed underlying belief distribution. None of these
models the cumulative information gain from sensor overlap
or how that gain alters the localization belief.
We fuse measurements from connected sensors into a
global uncertainty estimate and use Bayesian updates to
model how sensing changes the belief. Close to this, [8]
applies inverse-covariance fusion for decentralized state and
target estimation. We trade fully decentralized covariance
fusion in favor of a simpler differentiable objective built on
a distance-coupled local uncertainty function. This physics-
informed model sums inverse-variances (i.e. Fisher informa-
tion) across overlapping sensors, as is standard for indepen-
dent Gaussian measurements [9], an assumption we make
for our sensing model. Weighted variants of this method
can be used to accommodate heterogeneous sensors, as in
[10]. Bayesian belief updates also drive information-theoretic
search [11], [12]. Unlike these, we model sensor-overlap gain
in closed form and enforce multi-agent connectivity.
Finally, online sampling-based informative path planners
like IA-TIGRIS build candidate trajectory trees through
informed sampling and select trajectories by scoring metrics
like cumulative information gain [13]. They offer strong
global exploration in non-convex environments and resist lo-
cal minima, but scale poorly to joint multi-agent, constraint-
aware state spaces. Our gradient-based planner avoids this
but can stall at saddle points and in flat, low-belief regions,
which we mitigate with perturbed gradients and an adaptively
varied gradient pull region (Algorithm 1).

A. Search Space and Sensing Model
We consider n sensors (agents) in a convex, compact
search space X ⊂Rd, and its discretized mesh Xh of spacing
h with c = |Xh| cells. A single stationary target occupies an
unknown cell p ∈Xh. Time is discrete, t ∈{0,1,2,...}, and
x(t) = [x1(t)⊺,x2(t)⊺,...,xn(t)⊺]⊺∈(X)n collects the sensor
positions, with xi(t) ∈X the position of sensor i. Sensing
uncertainty is modeled by a continuously differentiable, non-
decreasing function fi : R+ →R+, where fi(||q −xi(t)||)
is the variance of sensor i’s measurement at cell q and
∥· ∥is the Euclidean norm. Individual measurement noise
is independent across sensors, cells, and time.
The belief φ(q,t) is a probability mass function on Xh
giving the probability that the target is at q at time t,
starting from a prior φ(·,0). Our goal is to update φ and
the trajectories xi(t) jointly so that φ(·,t) converges to the
point mass φ ∗(q) = 1{q = p} at the target.

IV. ALGORITHM METHODOLOGY
In this section, we detail our optimization algorithm used
to solve (8). To handle the non-convex nature of the objective
function H (x(t)) and the complex connectivity constraint
gconn(x(t)), we adopt the Primal-Dual Proximal Augmented
Lagrangian Algorithm (PPALA) developed in [1], [18]. We
drop the index t when the context is clear. Unlike the static
setting of [1], where PPALA converges to a KKT point of
fixed sensor positions, our objective Ht changes at every step
as the belief is updated, so we do not claim convergence
of the positions. Instead, Section V shows that the belief
concentrates on the target for any planner that satisfies AGI.

V. ANALYSIS
The primal step of Algorithm 1 moves each agent toward
the belief in RG(t), weighted by its share of the fused
sensing at each cell. On this nonconvex, time-varying ob-
jective, the descent can (i) stall at critical points where the
weighted belief balances about an agent, (ii) lose direction
once nearby belief is depleted, while (iii) the connectivity
metric reacts slowly to a single weakening link in a large
group. Algorithm 1 mitigates these with gradient noise, a
pull region that widens when nearby belief is depleted, and
a step-size safeguard with a connectivity radius below the
true communication range. These measures make recurrent
sensing plausible but not provable. We therefore treat the
planner as a black box, state our results under explicit
assumptions on which cells it senses (Assumption V.2), and
verify these assumptions empirically in Section VI.

Three suboptimal behaviors follow. (i) Critical points. (11)
vanishes when the weighted mass φ wq balances about xi(t),
and saddles points are also possible with multiple agents.
Noise may help here, although perturbed-gradient guarantees
[20] assume smoothness, which we cannot assume while
RG(t) changes between steps. These call for an explicit
escape mechanism. (ii) Dead regions. When sensing depletes
nearby belief, the gradient carries no information about belief
outside RG(t). Switching to a gradient over all of Xh would
cause other stall issues, since by (11) a lone agent is drawn
to the centroid of belief, which may be empty. In Algorithm
1, we exploit the discontinuous property by varying radii
for RG(t) when stuck, which showed empirical promise in
treating (i) and (ii) in simulation. (iii) Slow connectivity
response. The det(PTLP) constraint reacts slowly to a single
weakening link within large groups. A safeguard in step-
size that rejects disconnecting moves, and a constraint radius
below the true communication range, avoid this in Algorithm
1. These make recurrent sensing plausible but not provable
which is why we treat the trajectory planner as a black box
moving forward, under certain reasonable assumptions.

A. Simulation Setup
All experiments were run in MATLAB, using the same
world scale, sensor model, and belief update. The prior
φ(·,0) is a mixture of anisotropic Gaussian hot spots of
different sizes and orientations plus a 10% uniform floor,
ensuring every cell has positive prior mass. The n = 10 agents
start in a connected formation near the bottom left corner. We
adopt the sensing cost flocal(∥q−xi(t)∥) = 1
2∥q−xi(t)∥2 +ε,
standard in coverage control [2], [19], where ε > 0 enforces
flocal > 0. We assign ε = 10−6 and the pull region uses
rs = 0.15, γ = 1.1, and εg = 10−12. Agents communicate
within rc = 0.1 (inside the Laplacian we use 0.08 as a safety
margin), with ζ = 1. A cell is confirmed when its belief
reaches 1−η, with η = 0.01. The remaining parameters can
be found in Table I. We run three experiments. (i) Single run:

VII. CONCLUSION
We presented a coordination framework for multi-agent
search that fuses sensor uncertainties by inverse-variance
weighting and keeps the team connected through a differ-
entiable constraint maintained by a primal-dual planner. For
any planner that does not anticipate future measurements, we
bounded the probability of ever confirming a wrong location
and showed that false locations are rejected exponentially
fast in the number of looks. Under AGI, the belief provably
concentrates on the target. In simulation, our planner main-
tained connectivity, satisfied AGI in most trials, and located
the target faster than a lawnmower sweep when the prior was
informative. The measured residual belief, evidence per look,
and false-confirmation rates all agreed with our bounds.
