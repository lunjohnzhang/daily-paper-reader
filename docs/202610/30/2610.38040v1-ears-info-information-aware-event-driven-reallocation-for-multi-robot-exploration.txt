# EARS-Info: Information-Aware Event-Driven Reallocation for Multi-Robot Exploration 

## Kabeh Mohsenzadegan 1, Vahid Tavakkoli 1, and Kyandoghere Kyamakya 1,2

> 1

Department of Smart Systems Technologies, University of Klagenfurt, Austria

Faculté Polytechnique, Université de Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo Corresponding author: kabeh.mohsenzadegan@aau.at Preprint — October 27, 2026 

Abstract. We introduce EARS-Info, an information-aware extension of Event-Driven Ant Reallocation Search for multi-robot exploration on OpenStreetMap-derived grids. It combines marginal sensing gain, feasible relocation, return-aware routing, sensing reservations, and cached shortest paths. Across 168,000 episodes comparing 30 implementations on eight city maps, EARS-Info achieves the highest mean operational score in every evaluation panel. With eight robots and 3% communication dropout, it improves the score over EARS-Safe by 5.43%, increases weighted discovery from 88.88% to 98.46%, and reduces mean energy use by 2.62%. Score differences are positive in all eight cities ( p = 0.0078125; descriptive 95% city-bootstrap interval [0.0159 , 0.0694 ]), with no simulated incidents across 5,600 EARS-Info episodes. These results support the complete controller; individual component advantages remain uncertain. Generalization is limited by reused geography, one training replicate, coarse maps, and adapted comparison methods. 

Keywords: Multi-robot exploration; event-driven reallocation; information gain; ant-inspired search; runtime safety; OpenStreetMap; swarm robotics. 

# 1 Introduction 

Multi-robot exploration distributes spatial search across a team, but additional robots also create overlapping sensing, conges-tion, and avoidable movement. The useful objective is there-fore not simply to move faster or visit more cells. A controller must direct the team toward new information while preserving enough energy to return and limiting coordination overhead. Recent exploration research studies asynchronous decisions, macro actions, reinforcement learning, and frontier-led swarm-ing [1–6]. Our starting point is Event-Driven Ant Reallocation Search (EARS). Its original local-to-global structure uses an ant-inspired local rule most of the time and invokes relocation when observable search degradation occurs. This structure is interpretable, but a local score can revisit already sensed neighborhoods, a relocation goal can duplicate another robot’s sensing, and repeatedly rebuilding shortest-path fields can dominate runtime. Event triggering alone does not resolve these problems: the value and feasibility of the requested route must also be considered. We develop EARS-Info to address this complete decision process. The method scores newly sensed area using the simu-lator’s sensing footprint, compares feasible distant information gain with the best local opportunity, accounts for return travel before accepting relocation, and reserves sensing footprints across robot decisions. It maintains topology-dependent graph fields across steps rather than treating unchanged geography as a new planning problem. The design reads observable un-certainty and visit information; unsensed mission labels are excluded from policy inputs. This paper makes four contributions. First, it specifies EARS-Info as a new member of our EARS controller family, including the preceding EARS-Safe, negative-pheromone EARS-NP, and hierarchical H-MAPPO-EARS variants. Second, it defines matched local-only, periodic-request, and sensing-reservation controls that clarify what the complete-controller comparison can and cannot identify. Third, it reports a single frozen comparison of all 30 implementations on identical map snapshots, start zones, and step-indexed communication schedules, comprising 168,000 episodes. Fourth, it provides city-level inference, all-method rankings, raw task metrics, and artifact checks rather than relying solely on an aggregate rank. The main question is whether the complete information-aware controller improves our preceding EARS-Safe imple-mentation. Additional questions concern team-size/dropout behavior, alternate target realizations, and the evidence for indi-vidual design choices. The eight evaluation cities are excluded from the current training and checkpoint selection, but their identities have appeared in earlier development. Accordingly, this is an exploratory evaluation on reused geography, condi-tional on the frozen models. It is not a claim of untouched geographic confirmation or of reproducing every named exter-nal MARL algorithm. 

# 2 Related Work 

## 2.1 Cooperative Exploration and Bio-Inspired Search 

Recent multi-robot exploration methods include macro-action deep reinforcement learning under communication dropouts [1], asynchronous MARL for real-time cooperative explo-ration [2], reinforcement-learning-based coordination [3], and frontier-led swarming for unknown environments [5]. Dynamic frontier-led swarming extends these ideas to repeated coverage 1arXiv:2610.38040v1 [cs.RO] 27 Oct 2026 in changing environments [6]. Classical swarm intelligence also remains active in task allocation, including multi-objective ant-colony systems [7], event-triggered adaptive allocation [8], learned intention-aware allocation [9], and reactive multicon-straint assignment in uncertain semantic environments [10]. These studies motivate our use of a local swarm controller as a serious algorithmic baseline rather than a weak heuristic. Our AntSwarmSafe differs from generic ant-colony opti-mization in that pheromone is only one observable term in an online cell-level action score. The controller explicitly rewards novelty and uncertainty, suppresses repeat visits, discourages clustering, and passes actions through a safety monitor. EARS asks a narrower question: can global coordination be applied sparsely, only when local search is measurably degrading? Event-triggered mechanisms are not new in themselves; they have been studied for multi-robot allocation [8] and bandwidth-aware MARL communication [11]. The contribution here is the Ant-based architecture, degradation-triggered relocation mechanism, information-aware route selection and caching, persistent sensing reservations, and matched multi-city evalua-tion. 

## 2.2 MARL and Robotic Benchmarks 

Multi-agent PPO (MAPPO) and related PPO-family methods are strong cooperative MARL references [12], while trust-region formulations provide another principled family [13]. Surveys highlight exploration and scalability as persistent MARL challenges [14, 15]. MultiRoboLearn [16], MAR-BLER [17], and NeuronsMAE [18] emphasize that robot-oriented evaluation must represent dynamics, embodiment, transfer, and reproducibility rather than only abstract multi-agent games. Heterogeneous cooperation further stresses gen-eralization [4]. SafeSwarm-CityTwin complements these plat-forms by concentrating on urban map abstractions, explicit runtime checks, interpretable swarm methods, and geographi-cally resolved comparisons. 

## 2.3 Safety, Energy, Mapping, and Digital Twins 

Filtered reinforcement learning and dynamic shielding demonstrate how runtime constraints can be enforced around multi-agent decision policies [19–21]. Energy-aware coverage planning shows that distance alone can be a poor proxy for battery demand [22]. Distributed mapping frameworks such as Swarm-SLAM and DCL-SLAM address the mapping side of robot-team operation [23, 24]; recent work also combines collaborative SLAM with lightweight frontier exploration [25]. Broader coverage-planning surveys continue to document the tradeoff between completeness, motion cost, and environmen-tal dynamics [26]. Digital-twin research increasingly targets safety-critical robotics and smart-city integration [27–29]. SafeSwarm-CityTwin uses this perspective pragmatically: cached map-derived obstacles, restricted areas, bases, target fields, uncertainty, and communication conditions define reproducible city instances. Here, the term city twin denotes a static map-derived abstraction. The system has no live synchronization, calibrated vehicle dynamics, or physical field validation. 

# 3 Problem Formulation and Runtime Constraints 

## 3.1 Partially Observable Search With Shared State 

Let N robots move on an L × L grid, with traversable cells G ,obstacle cells, restricted cells, and a set of bases B. Robot 

i has position xi(t) and battery bi(t). Each action moves one cell north, south, east, or west, or remains stationary. The geometry is known; mission locations and priorities are hidden until sensed. Consequently, this is mission exploration on a known grid, rather than simultaneous localization and mapping in unknown geometry. Policies access a shared observation field o(c), uncertainty 

u(c), pheromone h(c), visit counts v(c), robot positions, batter-ies, and communication status. Only the evaluator and sensing model read unsensed target coordinates and priorities. Per-robot action scoring does not imply a fully distributed imple-mentation: shared fields and team positions are directly acces-sible in the simulator. Communication dropouts update status counters but do not implement bandwidth limits or delayed map exchange.

## 4.3 EARS Family and Scope of Comparison 

EARS-Safe, EARS-NP-Safe, H-MAPPO-EARS-Safe, and EARS-Info-v9 are members of our proposed method family. EARS-NP adds a decaying, diffusing repulsive pheromone field to discourage revisits and congestion. H-MAPPO-EARS uses a repository-trained MAPPO local option within a hierarchy of return, relocation, escape, and exploitation behaviors. It is a hybrid controller rather than a new MAPPO training algorithm. These earlier family members measure the development of the proposed approach and are not independent external competitors. The three additional v9 variants are diagnostic controls of our information controller. EARS-Info-Local-v9 disables relocation while retaining the information-based local rule, graph return logic, monitor, and action ordering. EARS-Info-Periodic16 changes only the event-request schedule to a fixed period of 16 steps after initial eligibility, preserving routing and feasibility checks; accepted intervention counts are measured rather than assumed to match. EARS-Info-NoSensingReservations removes sensing-footprint overlap reservations while preserving collision-related position reservations. All four v9 variants use the same newly selected EARS-Safe checkpoint and the same routing configuration except for the specified intervention.

# 5 Experimental Methodology 

## 5.1 Geography, Observation, and Map Prove-nance 

Training uses Vienna, London, and Berlin; validation uses Amsterdam and Prague. Evaluation uses San Francisco, Paris, Barcelona, Rome, Manhattan/New York, Chicago, Tokyo, and Melbourne. Training start zones are north-west, south-east, and center; validation uses west, east, north, and south; evalua-tion alternates north-east and south-west. The current training and checkpoint selection do not consume evaluation metrics. Because city identities were used in earlier studies, these splits do not erase development exposure to the geography. Each snapshot queries OpenStreetMap around a geocoded center with radius parameter 1.4 km and maps returned features to a 40 ×40 grid [30]. Points or representative points are scaled by the returned feature bounds. Buildings mark obstacles; industrial/railway land use and water/wetland mark restrictions. Mission priorities are 1.00 for emergency/clinical facilities, 0.85 for schools/care/community facilities, 0.75 for transport, 0.65 for tourism, 0.55 for recreation, and 0.45 for shops/offices. Co-located targets retain the maximum weight; mission and base cells override blocked labels. Start preprocessing selects traversable bases near the assigned zone. Geometry and mission provenance must both be OpenStreetMap-derived. Invalid legacy caches are re-freshed; synthetic fallback, empty missions, invalid priorities, out-of-grid cells, and target/blocked overlap are rejected. All methods then use identical frozen snapshots. These checks establish provenance and internal consistency, not fidelity to building footprints or physical mobility. ## 5.2 Training, Selection, and Method Ownership 

The learned GRPO, IPPO, MAPPO, and HAPPO implemen-tations are trained on the development cities, followed by the existing teacher-assisted coordination upgrade. PRISM config-urations and the EARS family are selected using training and validation performance. The initial learning command requests 108 episodes per learned policy, 12 validation episodes and two validation repeats; coordination uses two rounds; PRISM uses eight rounds; EARS shortlisting uses six training episodes and eight validation episodes with two repeats. Commands, selection histories, checkpoint hashes, and elapsed times are archived. These budgets describe the pipeline rather than a claim of equal optimizer steps across methods. One training replicate is available. The comparison includes 26 preceding implementations and four v9 implementations. The earlier EARS methods, the pro-posed v9 controller, and its controls are our family of methods; their progression is shown explicitly. Other implementations provide comparison context. QMIX-Safe, MADDPG-Safe, and MAT-Safe are lightweight policy-style adaptations, not complete published QMIX, MADDPG, or MAT reproductions. Repository PPO-family policies use trained linear/residual models. The comparison therefore concerns the frozen im-plementations, not a general ranking of published MARL al-gorithms. RandomAgent and GreedyAgent are unfiltered ref-erence controls. Heuristics have no learned weights; GRPO ablations share the corresponding trained checkpoint. Native filter and return implementations differ. 

## 5.3 Evaluation Grid and Paired Execution 

Both mission-stop and fixed-budget panels evaluate N ∈{4, 8, 16 , 32 } robots and dropout pd ∈ { 0, 0.03 , 0.15 , 0.30 },with 20 episodes per city/setting/method. Each panel contains 8 × 16 × 20 × 30 = 76 , 800 episodes. Mission-stop ends at complete discovery, all-agent termination, or step 160. Fixed-budget disables mission-completion stopping, but all-agent termination can still shorten a rollout. Consequently, it is a horizon-controlled rather than guaranteed equal-duration comparison. The SWAP panel uses eight robots, 3% dropout, three target seeds (2042, 3051, 4060), and 20 episodes per city/seed/method, giving 14,400 episodes. Total evaluation 6size is 168,000. The primary setting is mission-stop with eight robots and 3% dropout, giving 160 episodes per method and 4,800 episodes across methods. Each episode starts robots with 100 battery units, uses movement cost 0.55, idle cost 0.10, Manhattan sensing radius one, sensed uncertainty factor 0.25, and pheromone decay 0.96. All methods share frozen maps, starts, episode seeds, and communication draws indexed by simulator step and robot. Draw hashes agree within every one of the 5,600 matched scenarios. Method execution is serial with rotated method or-der. An observed-only environment interface hides unsensed mission labels; this is verified through action-parity fixtures. Shared map access nevertheless persists during disconnec-tion, so dropout is not a realistic delayed decentralized map-exchange model.

Weighted discovery rises from 0.888788 to 0.984619, an improvement of 9.583 percentage points. Coverage changes from 0.538550 to 0.542286, a smaller 0.374-point increase. Mean energy falls from 574.3625 to 559.3222 units (2.62%), and complete-target episodes increase from 31.25% to 35.625%. Mean capped completion steps decrease from 138.6875 to 134.0875. These are mission-stop rollout outcomes, not matched-duration energy savings. Runtime is reported separately from J and cannot account mechanically for the score increase. 

## 6.2 Performance Across Evaluation Panels 

EARS-Info-v9 has the highest equal-city mean score in the full mission-stop, fixed-budget, all-city SWAP, and changed-view SWAP panels (Table 4). The overall mission-stop mean averages all 16 team/dropout settings and differs from the primary-setting mean. Comparisons between stopping rules remain separate; pooling their episodes would obscure the operational meaning of energy and completion. Figure 2 displays the measured score across team sizes and dropout settings. These are evaluations of one fixed configu-ration, without retuning for individual test cities or settings. Higher dropout does not amount to full communication isola-tion because shared observations remain accessible. The results characterize robustness to this simulator’s dropout process. 

## 6.3 Geographic Heterogeneity and Sparse-Map Sensitivity 

The primary city contrasts range from +0.000373 in Chicago and +0.000649 in New York to +0.094265 in Rome and +0.092255 in Barcelona (Fig. 3). Tokyo improves by +0.080779, San Francisco by +0.042886, Paris by 

+0.014024, and Melbourne by +0.010451. Thus, the improvement is directionally consistent but varies substantially in magnitude. Excluding Chicago gives a mean score improvement of 

+0.047901 across seven cities. This post hoc sensitivity shows that the primary gain is not created by the sparse Chicago in-stance; it does not justify deleting the city from the original analysis. The small Manhattan effect and near-empty obstacle grids in some snapshots also caution against equating a city label with equivalent geometric difficulty. Comparisons are paired within the exact abstraction rather than across suppos-edly standardized physical urban environments.

## 6.6 Score Dependence 

The composite score is fixed before evaluation and excludes runtime. Raw discovery, coverage, energy, completion, and incident metrics accompany it. The principal improvement is not merely a runtime-weight artifact: runtime has zero weight, weighted discovery increases markedly, energy decreases, and coverage increases slightly. Nevertheless, the ordering depends on chosen operational preferences. We drew 2,000 independent weight vectors by multiplying each original score coefficient by a uniform factor in [0.75 , 1.25 ] and renormalizing the six coeffi-cients (seed 91827). EARS-Info-v9 remains above EARS-Safe and ranks first among all 30 implementations for every tested vector. Its mean-score margin over EARS-Safe ranges from 0.03333 to 0 .05073. These are fixed-data preference sensitivi-ties, not independent samples, additional simulation episodes, or new significance tests. 

# 7 Discussion 

## 7.1 What Changes Relative to Earlier EARS 

The central finding is an improvement of the complete information-aware controller over the preceding EARS-Safe implementation, with positive score differences in each of the eight evaluated abstractions. Earlier EARS uses observable evidence, novelty, and event-triggered global relocation. V9 directly values newly sensed cells, screens routes using graph travel and return distances, coordinates marginal sensing, and reuses graph structure. The large discovery improvement with a small coverage change suggests that where sensing occurs matters beyond the count of visited cells. This interpretation is consistent with the design but is not an independently identified causal decomposition. The comparison among our EARS family members is de-liberately distinct from the comparison with other implemen-tations. H-MAPPO-EARS and EARS-NP represent alterna-tive extensions, not external claims against which novelty is manufactured. The matched v9 controls ask narrower design questions. Other swarm, PRISM, and learned-policy imple-mentations establish broader benchmark context, while adapted QMIX/MADDPG/MAT labels must not be read as full external algorithm reproductions. 

## 7.2 Practical Interpretation 

Within this static, known-geometry benchmark, EARS-Info-v9 offers a favorable measured combination of discovery, energy, and runtime. It is appropriate when the decision system has 9access to shared uncertainty/visit fields and a traversable graph. The reported benefit does not directly transfer to unknown geometry, delayed map exchange, uncertain localization, con-tinuous dynamics, or real battery discharge. Those settings require an observation/communication model and an execution layer beyond this study. 

# 8 Limitations 

The study is exploratory because evaluation cities were previ-ously used during development. Coarse rasterization produces sparse maps, including Chicago’s single-target instance. One training replicate and unequal model capacity and training re-sources limit generalization and external MARL comparisons. Joint design changes prevent isolated causal attribution, and no secondary test survives multiplicity correction. Shared state persists during dropout, execution safeguards differ, and early termination limits energy comparisons. Runtime is platform-specific; zero simulated incidents do not guarantee physical safety. 

# 9 Reproducibility and Artifact Avail-ability 

Code is available at https://github.com/vtavakkol i/SafeSwarm-CityTwin . The frozen eight-city-pub lication-v3 study contains 168,000 episode records cov-ering 30 methods, maps, checkpoints, source snapshots, and training/runtime manifests. All 227 checked hashes matched; independent recalculation reproduced the scores, panel means, and primary p-value. The runtime manifest records software versions, hardware, and single-thread settings; no immutable image digest was supplied. The CSV supports the reported analyses, but the omitted SQLite journal and full diagnostics are required for the original journal-based reporting workflow. Analysis scripts and an artifact inventory accompany the manuscript materi-als. OpenStreetMap data retain their separate attribution and licensing requirements [30]. 

# 10 Conclusion 

EARS-Info extends our EARS method family with marginal sensing gain, graph-feasible event-driven relocation, sensing reservations, and reusable path fields. In 168,000 matched evaluations of 30 implementations across eight OSM-derived city maps, EARS-Info-v9 achieves the highest mean opera-tional score in every reported panel. At the primary setting, its score improves by 5.43% over EARS-Safe, weighted dis-covery increases by 9.58 percentage points, and mean rollout energy decreases by 2.62%. All eight city score contrasts are positive, with exact p = 0.0078125. These findings support the complete-controller improvement in the tested benchmark. Component-level superiority, independent geographic gener-alization, and physical safety remain outside the established evidence. The result is an interpretable and auditable improve-ment with explicit implementation, statistical, and environmen-tal scope.
