# Abstract 

We present an experimental framework for de-termining sufficient communication in robot teams running fixed, independent Behaviour Trees with private blackboards. Within a de-clared policy family, we vary message content and admission frequency and measure traffic in recipient-weighted bits per robot decision. A dense sweep identifies candidate policies for comparison with full-state communication. We retest selected candidates and the reference on fresh, paired scenarios, using one-sided simul-taneous confidence bounds to assess whether success remains within five percentage points of the full-state reference. The bounds also assess whether tested cheaper policies can be ruled out as insufficient. Depending on the evidence, the framework reports an exact minimum, a positive bracket, a sufficient upper bound, or an unresolved boundary. Experiments with eight content policies across four warehouse condi-tions identify zero communication as the ex-act minimum under low contention. In the scaled condition, the no-release policy at gate 

q = 0 .992222 is confirmed sufficient, achieving 96.33% success at an estimated rate of 738.55 bits per robot decision, or 0.544% of full-state traffic. The positive minimum remains unre-solved. These claims apply only to the declared policy family, sampled gate values and operat-ing conditions. 

# 1 Introduction 

Communication allows a robot to learn that another robot has already claimed or completed a task, which can prevent repeated travel and work. However, sending this information is associated with a cost and creates com-munication traffic. Therefore, the challenge is to reduce communication while preserving enough information for the robot team to coordinate successfully. We therefore investigated how much communication is sufficient, for a fixed team controller and operating condition, to keep task success within a chosen loss from full-state communication. We study this question using Behaviour Trees (BTs), which provide modular and reac-tive control structures [Colledanchise and Ögren, 2018; Iovino et al. , 2022]. Each robot runs its own BT instance and maintains private beliefs about tasks and team-mates. Throughout the experiment, the BT structure and task-selection rule remain unchanged. We vary only the communication protocol, which determines what in-formation is shared between robots and how ownership information is maintained. This allows us to isolate the effect of communication while keeping the underlying decision-making process fixed. We vary communication along two dimensions, namely message content and admission probability. Firstly, a content rule determines what information a policy is al-lowed to send and when it is generated, such as task completion, ownership claims, releases, intent, task facts or robot status. Secondly, a gate q ∈ [0 , 1] controls the probability that each eligible message is admitted. The gate therefore controls how often eligible messages are transmitted, but it does not directly measure the amount of communication. Specifically, different content rules generate different numbers and sizes of messages, and messages sent earlier can change later robot decisions and therefore which messages are generated afterwards. We therefore measure the traffic that is actually trans-mitted. For each message, we count its application-level payload once for every intended recipient, then normalise the total by the number of robot decisions. This gives the realised recipient-weighted application bits per robot decision. A policy is sufficient if its success remains within the chosen tolerance of the full-state reference. To claim a positive minimum, we would additionally need evi-dence that every cheaper policy in the declared family is insufficient. We therefore distinguish between a con-firmed sufficient policy, an unresolved boundary, a posi-arXiv:2610.38176v1 [cs.RO] 28 Oct 2026 tive bracket, and an exact zero-rate minimum. Note that our conclusions apply only to the family that we evaluate. Within this scope, our contribution is a reproducible procedure that combines fixed private BT control, measured recipient-weighted cost, exploratory frontier search, and fresh paired confirmation. We ap-ply the procedure to eight content policies across four controlled task conditions and report the strongest con-clusion supported by the evidence in each case. 

# 2 Related Work and Gap 

Early multi-robot research showed that communication requirements depend strongly on the task. Communica-tion can improve performance in some settings while be-ing unnecessary in others, and relatively simple commu-nication can sometimes approach richer schemes [Balch and Arkin, 1994]. Task-allocation research similarly shows that the usefulness of information depends on the structure of the team and the way tasks are coupled [Gerkey and Matarić, 2004; Dias et al. , 2006]. This moti-vates studying a framework to test on specific controller and operating condition, rather than searching for one universal minimum across unrelated robot teams. Behaviour Tree research has developed several ways to coordinate multiple robots. Distributed BT exe-cution, capability-based allocation and communication of robot intent have all been used to improve coor-dination [Heppner et al. , 2023; Heppner et al. , 2024; Hull et al. , 2024]. These approaches mainly change the coordination architecture or the information representa-tion used by the team. In contrast, we keep the local BT structure and task-selection rule fixed and vary only the communication protocol. This allows us to study how changing the information shared between robots affects the performance of the same underlying controller. Furthermore, previous work has used utility-aware message forwarding, learned communication schedules and information bottlenecks to decide what information should be transmitted [Wang et al. , 2020]. Our contri-bution is therefore not simply to reduce communication, but to provide an experimental and statistical proce-dure for determining when communication is sufficient. The resulting frontier relates communication cost to task performance, similar in spirit to rate-distortion analysis. However, it describes only the tested policy family and is not an information-theoretic lower bound.

# 4 Experiment and Implementation 

4.1 Task Environment and Robot Behaviour 

The experiment uses a two-dimensional grid containing stationary service tasks. There are no pickup or delivery stages, obstacles, collisions, deadlines, or battery con-straints. All tasks are available from the start of an episode. Each robot initially knows every task’s identi-fier, location, and required capability. This initial task catalogue is treated as part of the problem setup and is not counted as communication. The experiment focuses on communication used for coordination after the tasks are known. Task locations are sampled independently and uni-formly from cells in the grid, and multiple tasks may occupy the same cell. Capabilities are assigned cycli-cally to robots and tasks, and a robot may only work on compatible tasks. Robot starting positions are fixed in a central 2×2 block; the eight-robot condition reuses these four cells. Perfect local sensing within a Manhattan ra-dius of one grid cell reveals task completion. Teammate status is available only through messages. Table 1 gives the four operating conditions. A robot selects the nearest compatible task that it believes is unfinished and not already held by another active claim. Distance ties are broken using the task identifier. If teammate status information is available, the robot may also defer to a compatible teammate that it believes is closer to the task, has priority under the tie-breaking rule, or is already working on that task. Task selection takes one tick. On later ticks, the robot moves one grid cell towards its selected task, moving along the x direction before the y direction. Once it reaches the task, each work tick contributes one unit of progress. Every task requires two units of work. If sev-eral robots work on the same task during the same tick, only one unit of progress is added, and the remaining actions are counted as duplicate work. The low-contention condition assigns one capability type to each of the four robots. Each task therefore has only one eligible robot, removing competition between robots with the same capability. The scaled condition changes the team size, number of tasks, arena size, and horizon together. It would be interpreted as a different operating condition. 

4.2 Private Behaviour Trees and Tick Order 

Each robot has its own Behaviour Tree instance, black-board, task beliefs, and teammate beliefs. All robots use the same fixed BT structure with three branches: con-tinue an active task; otherwise select a new task; other-wise remain idle. The environment stores the true task state and applies the robots’ actions, but it does not choose tasks for them. Each simulation tick follows the same order. First, messages scheduled for that tick are delivered and local sensing is updated. Each robot then ticks its own BT using only its private information. Robot actions are committed, and outgoing communication is generated afterwards. A packet transmitted at tick t is delivered at tick t+d,where d is the communication delay for that condition. Messages generated during a tick therefore cannot affect another robot until a later tick. This ensures that com-munication timing is determined by the model, rather than by the order in which robots happen to be pro-cessed by the simulator. 

4.3 Communication Policies 

The experiment evaluates eight communication policies. Table 2 outlines each of the eight policies. Each pol-icy determines which types of information may be sent. The underlying BT and task-selection rule remain un-changed. A completion message is generated when a task fin-ishes. A claim is generated when a robot selects a task, and a release is generated when a robot gives up a task because of a competing claim. Some policies use persis-tent claims, while others use leases that expire unless re-newed. Lease duration is eight ticks, or ten in the scaled condition. An active owner renews its private lease each tick; peers learn of renewal only through delivered mes-sages. Note that messages are serialised using a fixed text format. Their exact byte length is measured after se-rialisation rather than assigning one fixed size to each message type. This matters because optional fields and different identifier lengths can change the size of individ-ual messages. 

4.4 Admission Gate and Message Loss 

For every message that is eligible under a policy, the simulator generates a reproducible pseudo-random score 

um ∈ [0 , 1) . The message is admitted when um < q ,where q ∈ [0 , 1] is the admission gate. Thus q = 0 ad-mits no eligible messages, q = 1 admits all of them, and intermediate values admit only a fraction of the available message opportunities. The score for a message depends on the scenario seed and the semantic details of the message, including its type, sender, tick, task, owner, and original claim time. This makes admission reproducible across repeated runs. If the same message opportunity exists at two different gate values, increasing q cannot cause that particular message to change from admitted to rejected. However, increasing q can also change the robots’ later behaviour. For example, admitting an ownership claim may cause another robot to choose a different task, which can remove message opportunities that would otherwise have occurred later in the episode. Therefore, a larger 

q does not simply mean that the final set of transmit-ted messages is a larger version of the set produced at a smaller q. For this reason, q is used only to control message admission. It is not used as the communication cost itself. Instead, we calculate the realised communica-tion rate from the messages that are actually transmitted during the episode. Messages rejected by the gate are discarded. Messages admitted by the gate are transmitted and are then sub-ject to the channel-loss model separately for each recip-ient. The main simulation does not include retransmis-sions, acknowledgements, congestion, or communication-range limits.

4.6 Paired Confirmation and Confidence Bounds 

The final sufficiency decision uses the fresh confirmation scenarios. For policy c and scenario i, define 

di(c) = Yi(c) − Yi(cref ). (8) The paired difference can take three values. A value of 1 means the candidate succeeds while the reference fails, −1 means the candidate fails while the reference succeeds, and 0 means that both have the same outcome. Pairing makes the comparison fair by evaluating both policies on the same scenarios. This reduces the effect of some scenarios being harder than others. If both policies succeed or both fail on the same scenario, that scenario contributes zero to their performance difference because neither policy performed better than the other. Let 

a(c) = Pr {di(c) = 1 }

be the probability that the candidate gains over the ref-erence, and let 

b(c) = Pr {di(c) = −1}

be the probability that it loses to the reference. The success difference is 

∆( c) = a(c) − b(c). (9) We construct one-sided Clopper-Pearson bounds for these two probabilities. Let M be the total number of non-reference policies included in confirmation across all conditions, and define 

η = 0.05 2M . (10) If n+ is the number of paired gains and n− is the number of paired losses, the simultaneous lower bound for the success difference is L(c) = LCP (n+, n ; η) − UCP (n−, n ; η). (11) For 0 < k < n ,

LCP (k, n ; η) = Qη {Beta( k, n − k + 1) }, (12) 

UCP (k, n ; η) = Q1−η {Beta( k + 1 , n − k)}. (13) At k = 0 , the bounds are LCP = 0 and UCP = 1 −η1/n ;at k = n, they are LCP = η1/n and UCP = 1 . With no paired gains or losses, Equation 11 therefore gives 

L(c) = η1/n − 1, approximately −0.0023 for M = 25 and 

n = 3000 .The adjustment by M accounts for the fact that sev-eral confirmation candidates are tested at the same time. Using a union bound gives at least 95% simultaneous coverage for the family of lower bounds, assuming the scenario samples themselves are independent. A policy is confirmed sufficient when L(c) ≥ − 0.05 .

This means that, after accounting for uncertainty and multiple comparisons, the evidence supports the policy being no more than five percentage points worse than the full-state reference. We inspect every policy included in confirmation and report the passing policy with the lowest measured mean communication rate. We also report 95% confidence intervals for absolute success rates and mean communication rates to show the uncertainty in these estimates. 

4.7 Exploratory Frontier Uncertainty 

As discussed previously, the detailed-stage frontier is ex-ploratory and is used to help select candidate policies. To show its uncertainty, we use a paired bootstrap with 2,000 replicates [Davison and Hinkley, 1997]. Each boot-strap replicate resamples complete scenarios, using the same resampled scenario indices for all policies within a condition. For each replicate, we calculate the largest estimation error across the tested policies. These errors are used to construct a common one-sided uncertainty envelope around the exploratory frontier. The resulting shaded region in Figure 2 is used to visualise uncertainty during candidate selection. 

4.8 Secondary Token-Bucket Test 

As a secondary stress test, we also replace probability gating with a token-bucket communication limit. For this test, q = 1 , so all eligible messages are proposed, but a message can only be transmitted when the robot has enough tokens available. The bucket is tested using refill rates equal to 0.9, 1.0,and 1.1 times the proposed candidate’s estimated com-munication rate. Each robot’s bucket starts empty. Ca-pacities are 16,384 or 65,536 bits, doubled in the scaled condition. Messages that cannot be sent immediately may remain pending, while periodic messages are regen-erated. This test is not part of the primary sufficiency analy-sis. Its purpose is to check whether policies with similar average communication rates behave similarly when the timing and burstiness of communication are constrained. 

# 5 Results 

5.1 Content and gate response 

Figure 1 shows that increasing the gate generally in-creases the realised communication rate, but higher traf-fic alone does not guarantee higher success. Performance depends strongly on which information is being commu-nicated. In the baseline condition, completion-only communi-cation achieved 13.00% success, whereas claim–release achieved 97.67%. Mean duplicate work also fell from 14.67 to 0.00 actions per episode. This suggests that learning only when a task has finished is often too late to prevent redundant behaviour. Ownership claims are more useful because they can prevent another robot from travelling towards the same task before the work is com-pleted. Removing release messages still gave 97.67% suc-cess at 354.55 bits per robot decision. These comparisons suggest that claims provide most of the useful coordina-tion information in this condition. The same pattern becomes stronger in the scaled condition. Completion-only communication achieved just 1.00% success, while claim-release achieved 95.67%. Mean duplicate work fell from 75.95 to 0.00 actions per episode. Removing release messages again retained 95.67% success, at 742.68 bits per robot decision. This indicates that, as more robots can compete for the same tasks, communicating ownership becomes increasingly important for avoiding duplicate work. At the same time, the strong performance of the no-release policy suggests that not every ownership-related message is equally important. The lossy condition shows a different communication requirement. Persistent claim-release achieved 92.00% success, lease-heartbeat achieved 96.33%, and leased claims without heartbeat achieved 89.33%. The im-provement from adding heartbeat messages suggests that repeated ownership information can help when earlier messages may be lost. This suggests that useful com-munication policy depends on the operating condition: a sparse event-based policy can work well in a reliable channel, while message loss can make repeated updates more valuable. 5.2 Fresh-seed confirmation 

The exploratory stages identify promising operating points, but final sufficiency claims use new confirmation scenarios that were not used to select those candidates. Table 4 reports the lowest-rate passing arm among the policies retested in each condition. The simultaneous family contains 25 non-reference arms across all four con-ditions, with 3000 paired scenarios per arm. In the baseline condition, the no-release policy at q =1.0000 achieved 95.40% success at an estimated 356.36 bits per robot decision, or 1.133% of full-state traffic. Its simultaneous paired lower bound was −0.0395 , so it passes the −0.05 sufficiency criterion. This confirms that, for the baseline condition, completion and claim messages are sufficient without release messages. The low-contention condition gives a stronger re-sult. The zero-message policy achieved 93.37% success, matching the full-state reference on the paired sample, with a lower bound of −0.0023 . Its communication rate is therefore 0.00 bits per robot decision. Together with the structural argument in Section 5.4, this establishes that communication is unnecessary under this condition: because every task capability has only one eligible robot, the robots do not need ownership messages to avoid com-peting for the same task. The lossy condition required substantially richer com-munication among the retested candidates. Rich status at q = 0 .4989 achieved 93.67% success at 3127.66 bits per robot decision, or 9.902% of full-state traffic, with a lower bound of −0.0461 . It therefore passes the suffi-ciency criterion. Combined with the exploratory heart-beat results, this suggests that when messages can be lost, repeated state information can compensate for miss-ing earlier coordination messages. Note that this result identifies a sufficient operating point, not the minimum communication required under loss. In the scaled condition, the no-release policy at q =0.9922 achieved 96.33% success at 738.55 bits per robot decision. This is only 0.544% of the full-state communi-cation rate, and its simultaneous lower bound of −0.0438 

passes the sufficiency criterion. The result shows that the scaled team can remain within the chosen success tol-erance using a sparse claim-based protocol rather than repeatedly broadcasting full task and robot state. The scaled condition also illustrates why fresh con-firmation is necessary. The exploratory procedure pro-posed the no-release policy at q = 0 .9422 . When retested on fresh scenarios, it achieved 94.70% success at 717.58 bits per robot decision, but its lower bound was −0.0621 ,so it did not pass the confirmation criterion. The selected cheaper challenger at q = 0.9300 similarly achieved 94.70% success at 712.25 bits per robot decision and did not pass. The confirmed operating point therefore moved to the higher gate q = 0 .9922 . This shows that an apparent crossing in the exploratory frontier is not sufficient. 

5.3 Token-bucket stress test 

The scaled token-bucket tests produced mean communi-cation rates between 571.99 and 667.41 bits per robot de-cision, but success remained between 2.30% and 11.40%. These rates are of the same order as the probability-gated candidate, yet their performance is much worse. This result shows that average communication rate alone does not determine performance. The token bucket changes when messages can be sent and may delay or suppress ownership information that is useful at a par-ticular decision point. Two mechanisms can therefore produce similar average traffic while delivering different information at different times. 5.4 Why zero communication is sufficient 

In the low-contention condition, each task has exactly one eligible robot. Messages about other robots’ tasks cannot remove a compatible option, and status-based deferral considers only compatible teammates, of which there are none. Tasks do not interact through collisions or shared resources. The compatible-task trajectory is therefore identical with and without communication, by induction over ticks. Hence p(c0) = p(cref ) for every task layout under these assumptions, where c0 is the zero-message policy, and R∗ = 0 . The paired experiment checks the implementation; identical outcomes in a finite sample alone would not establish population equality. 

# 6 Discussion 

6.1 Answer to the research question 

There is no single communication minimum for all in-dependent BT teams. The quantity is a function of the task condition θ, acceptable loss δ, and policy family C.Within those boundaries, the framework can make rigor-ous statements by holding the controller fixed, searching content and frequency separately, measuring actual bits, and applying paired uncertainty bounds. We identify an exact zero-rate minimum under low contention. In baseline and scaled operation, the no-release policy is confirmed sufficient at q = 1 and 

q = 0 .992222 , respectively. Under loss, rich status at 

q = 0 .4989 passes. Confirmation does not exclude ev-ery cheaper policy, so the positive minima remain unre-solved. For BT engineering, the frontier turns an informal de-sign process into a measurable test. A developer can ask whether adding a message type moves the frontier left or merely adds cost. The ablations also yield concrete do-main findings. Claims are the smallest tested semantic component that removes most duplicate work. Releases are redundant under deterministic task completion. Un-der loss, renewable ownership becomes useful. Because the BT is unchanged, these effects can be traced to infor-mation entering the tree rather than to a hidden change in decision logic. 

6.2 Novelty and limits 

Our contribution is a method for assessing communica-tion requirements with a fixed controller. Prior work designs more capable BT coordination or learned sparse protocols. We ask which content justifies its communi-cation cost, then assess the smallest measured rate the data support. Private BT blackboards provide a con-trolled information boundary, within which we assess the content–frequency frontier using the explicit claim hier-archy. Four limits remain. First, the minimum is only over eight declared content policies and sampled gate values. A better encoding may use fewer bits. Second, all con-ditions are warehouse variants. Adaptability must be tested in a domain with a different reason for commu-nication, such as partial-discovery search and rescue or tasks requiring simultaneous coalitions. Third, full state is an information reference using the same BT, not a cen-trally optimal allocator. Fourth, simulation omits real radio congestion, correlated loss, and timing jitter. Adeployment should preserve application bits for seman-tic comparison and add wire bits, queue delay, and burst loss. Further experiments should target policies cheaper than the confirmed no-release points in baseline and scaled operation, and the rich-status point in lossy op-eration. A failure to confirm sufficiency does not estab-lish insufficiency; fresh paired tests are needed to resolve these cheaper candidates. Each adaptive round needs a new seed block and a predeclared error budget, or an anytime-valid procedure. Sensitivity analyses can repeat the final rule at δ = .02 and .10 , while retaining .05 as the primary margin.

# 8 Conclusion 

We present a method for assessing sufficient communica-tion in independent BT teams. It varies an explicit pro-tocol family and message-admission gate, measures ac-tual recipient-weighted traffic, and separates exploratory selection from fresh paired confirmation. Simultaneous confidence bounds support the listed sufficient policies, while the claim rules prevent a selected frontier cross-ing from being mistaken for a positive minimum. The zero-communication result follows from unique task ca-pabilities and the simulator’s independent task struc-ture. For contested conditions, the reported operating points quantify what the specified controller and pro-tocols can achieve; each new task condition requires its own evidence.
