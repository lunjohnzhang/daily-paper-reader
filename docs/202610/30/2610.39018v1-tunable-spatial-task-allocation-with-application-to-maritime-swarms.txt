# Tunable Spatial Task Allocation with Application to Maritime Swarms 

Vishnudatta Thota [0009 −0003 −1297 −7978 ] ,Tyler Paine [0000 −0001 −6071 −621 𝑋 ] and, Anastasia Bizyaeva [0000 −0003 −0728 −9655 ]

Abstract In this chapter, we explore how a team of autonomous agents can flexibly reconfigure the distribution of its members across many spatially embedded tasks using a task allocation strategy grounded in a generalization of the recently proposed Nonlinear Opinion Dynamics model. Motivated by an adaptive maritime survey application, we couple opinion dynamics directly to spatial movement and examine how the emergent macroscopic allocation is shaped by the interplay between environmental geometry, nature of social interactions, and task demands. We illustrate in simulation how social coupling in the model tunes group cohesion, producing macroscopic allocation patterns ranging from maximally dispersed states, to intermediate semi-dispersed states with a smaller number of distinct subgroups, to maximally cohesive consensus states, in distinct and interpretable parameter regimes. Furthermore, we demonstrate with illustrative examples how a team can reconfigure itself in real time under changing task demands. For allocation scenarios with distance-based task preference and matched number of agents and tasks, we relate numerically the final allocation in the group to the Hungarian optimum, illustrating close-to-optimal performance. We illustrate performance of this method under realistic communication constraints in the MOOS-IvP-Multi simulation environment with simulated autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs). Together, these examples show how a common set of local interaction rules can support a large range of interpretable macroscopic allocation states and flexible transitions between them.

## 1 Introduction 

Allocating members of an autonomous team across tasks flexibly without centralized control is a key challenge in multi-agent autonomy. For example, a team of robots surveying an environment may need to distribute its members across candidate sites, then form subgroups to investigate selected sites where mission-relevant information was located, and finally reallocate robots to other sites as tasks within the initially selected sites are completed. Each of the three mission stages requires individual robots to select specific sites to commit to based on available information, but they each require different macroscopic patterns of collective participation within the group. Furthermore, the evolving mission scenario requires the group to transition between different coalitions as task-relevant information evolves. When tasks are embedded in physical space, as they frequently are in robotic applications, the interplay between the geometry of the task distribution and the agents’ physical motion also plays a key role in the emergent decision-making in the group. These considerations motivate the need for decentralized task allocation frameworks that account for the coupling between decision-making and physical motion, and also provide interpretable mechanisms to shape collective patterns of participation through tunable local interactions. In this chapter, we illustrate how nonlinear opinion dynamics [9] can support such flexible allocation across many spatial tasks, with a social coupling parameter providing an interpretable design “knob” that tunes the degree of cohesion within a group. The multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) problem is commonly modeled as a com-binatorial optimization problem that accounts for task utilities and constraints [20]. Decentralized MRTA approaches prominently include bio-inspired threshold mech-anisms based on local responses to sensory stimulus [1, 26, 12] and market-based approaches in which agents bid on tasks through repeated rounds of auctions [13, 37]. Another class of approaches associates task preferences of each agent with dynamic variables such as opinions or motivations that are exchanged over a communication net-work. This perspective transforms the task allocation problem into a continuous-time dynamical system convergence problem. There is a long precedent for using opinion dynamics models as coordination mechanisms for collective choice; for example, in [30] a majority-rule opinion dynamics model is used for collective decision-making between two alternative travel paths. In more recent works, vector-valued opinion and motivation dynamics are used to coordinate assignments among multiple tasks [38, 44, 5, 47, 48]. The dynamical approach provides a natural framework for studying how decision rules, interactions among agents, and local information shape collective allocation patterns. Recently proposed Nonlinear Opinion Dynamics (NOD) provide a tractable math-ematical framework for relating patterns of collective decisions in a group to values of social interaction parameters, network structure, and influence of external informa-tion [9, 10, 28]. In this framework, each agent’s opinions evolve through a bounded nonlinear response to a combination of self-reinforcement, social influence, and exter-nal inputs. The model form takes direct inspiration from dynamics of influence and decision-making over social networks. The nonlinearity of the social interactions en-ables multistable opinion patterns and gives rise to bifurcations through which the number or stability of these macroscopic patterns changes. this framework, such as the signs of pairwise social couplings and local biases, can then be used to shape the emergent allocation as has been illustrated for two-task de-centralized allocation scenarios [8, 3]. Recently, NOD has been applied to the problem of autonomous teaming within the context of a hierarchical coordination framework [31, 33]. However, most practical scenarios considered in the listed works are still lim-ited to allocation of agents to two or three subgroups. Our goal in this chapter is to illustrate how agents can flexibly allocate themselves across a large number of spatially embedded tasks using the NOD framework as an organizing principle. When deciding between multiple tasks, it is helpful to distinguish between two desirable aspects of the decision process. Within each agent, dynamics of opinions must resolve competition among feasible options, i.e. one opinion must dominate each agent’s preferences to avoid internal deadlocks. Across agents, the interactions must organize resulting preferences into a macroscopic pattern of commitment or allocation, shaped by the nature of social interactions between agents and coupled to the geometry of tasks and local information about them. In the original NOD formulation [9] and its subsequent extensions and applications, nonlinear interactions between agents are modeled as componentwise saturations, with a typical choice of nonlinearity being the hyperbolic tangent function. This is a natural nonlinearity for the description of binary choice between mutually exclusive tasks; however, in multi-task settings, saturation alone does not ensure that one task preference strongly dominates over others. In this chapter, we utilize an NOD formulation with a shifted softmax saturation of social information that more naturally represents choice between multiple mutually exclusive options, and explicitly connects the mathematical structure of the NOD framework with established game-theoretic and neural computation frameworks for decision-making and task allocation, including logit population game dynamics [40, 4, 14] and Potts neural network models [23, 15]. Several previous works have established connections between NOD and game theo-retic frameworks. For example, [34] uses NOD to tune cooperative behavior in social dilemma games through a competition between a payoff function and social influence; [32] uses a game-theoretic formulation to tune a bias in NOD to reallocate a team between two tasks. More recently [43] shows how ring attractor-like internal coupling among an agent’s preferences towards actions in an NOD framework shapes a neuro-morphic best-response mechanism, with an application to a multi-agent coverage game with no explicit communication. Building on these connections, we study how social interactions, spatial movement, and task capacity demands jointly shape allocation across many spatially embedded tasks in a softmax NOD formulation that is coupled to physical motion of agents in the plane. To illustrate how softmax NOD can be leveraged in flexible spatial task allocation scenarios, we study a coupled model of opinion dynamics and physical movement, with an adaptive maritime survey serving as a motivating application throughout the chapter. The chapter is structured the following way. In Section 2 we introduce the motivating scenario. In Section 3 we present the softmax NOD task allocation model, introducing agent-specific task capabilities as a mask on the nonlinearity, and discuss the connection between the resulting model and logit dynamics. We illustrate how decisiveness emerges in a bifurcation of a neutral opinion state. ## 2 Motivating Example: Adaptive Survey of Benthic Fauna 

We consider the class of problems that require dynamic task allocation under significant communication constraints. The following oceanographic survey scenario grounds the theoretical development in a practical application. The challenge we address is locating and quantifying adult benthic fauna that are found on the ocean floor at depths of approximately 100-300 m or in the deep water column near the ocean floor. The specific organism of interest is Pleuroncodes planipes ,or the tuna crab, which is commonly found along the Pacific coast of North and South America [11]. Recently, large swarms of P. planipes were discovered on the ocean floor [36], motivating further research into the behavior of these organisms. 

P. planipes forms a key link in the food chain by connecting smaller phytoplankton to larger predators such as tuna in upwelling-driven ecosystems [2]. These upwelling systems are highly productive fisheries along the coasts of California, Mexico, and Peru. Since they play a central role in the ecosystem, it is important to understand their spatial distribution, particularly that of the adult benthic P. planipes , which are believed to be responsible for the majority of reproduction [29]. Despite their ecological importance, these organisms are difficult to survey. P. pla-nipes migrate as larvae or juveniles suspended in pelagic water column. The migration patterns are driven by ocean currents, and blooms are correlated with upwelling [39]. Both of life phases are affected by processes that are altered by ocean warming. Adults are no easier to observe. Mature adults are mostly found at depths of 100-300 m [11], preventing remote sensing via satellites, while costly trawling surveys and hydroacous-tic mapping provide only an estimate of biomass in the water column [39]. Moreover, adults can swarm together to form tight clusters on the ocean floor, or otherwise exhibit high spatial variability [36]. One viable approach is to use autonomous vehicles that descend into the water column and illuminate an area of ocean floor to capture images and samples [36]. There are several autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) in service that already perform missions of this type [42, 25, 24], but they are often expensive and require dedicated crewed ships to support operations, limiting their ability to perform large-scale, persistent searches. Recently, lower-cost vehicles have become more capable and Tunable Spatial Task Allocation with Application to Maritime Swarms 5

offer an affordable alternative platform for surveying at 300 m [35, 45, 41]. Although these low-cost vehicles are less ruggedized, they rely on more sophisticated autonomy and control to operate at the edge of their relatively limited operating envelope. Their low cost also allows them to be deployed in groups, further improving robustness and efficiency. The search area is simply too large for an exhaustive search. Even with a good prior estimate of the location of the benthic fauna, the search area is on the order of 100 

𝑘𝑚 2[11]. Low-cost underwater vehicles have a range of 4-5 km and carry cameras that image areas on the order of 10 𝑚 2 per frame. In addition, vehicles do not have the communication capacity or computing resources to solve assignment problems using brute-force approaches, especially as team size grows. One way to address this problem is to use uncrewed surface vehicles (USVs) to perform a preliminary search using acoustic backscatter methods. Each USV also carries a complement of AUVs that it can deploy. Both advantages of using USVs stem from energy savings: the USVs can efficiently refine the search area by transiting on the surface, and then they transport the energy-constrained AUVs as close to the estimated sample sites as possible. Although this multi-robot system design relies on largely commercially available hardware, new distributed autonomy techniques are required to coordinate these teams at scale. 3.3 Analysis of softmax nonlinear opinion dynamics for a single agent 

The NOD framework uses a component-wise bounded saturation function such as tanh to encode nonlinearity in the opinion dynamics [9, 10, 28]. This framework works well for task allocation problems involving up to two options [8]. However, when more than two options are considered, the tanh nonlinearity can give equilibria for our application purposes, such as ones where multiple tasks simultaneously have large associated opinion values at equilibrium. This can cause a moving agent to stop between multiple sites instead of converging to a specific sample site. To address this issue, we used the shifted masked softmax nonlinearity given in Eqn. (6). Adding social influence, geometric effects, and prices, effectively tunes the agent’s bias and dynamically unfolds its symmetric bifurcation diagram, selecting a choice that has the most evidence for it as a result of the three combined effects.

4.1 Effect of social coupling 𝜸 

First, we investigate how the macroscopic coalition formation patterns are shaped by the agents’ social relationships under all-to-all communication. The social coupling parameter 𝛾 determines whether agents are cooperative, i.e., reinforce each others’ opinions, or antagonistic, i.e., suppress each others’ opinions. We expect the opinion dynamics to promote consensus choice on a task when 𝛾 > 0 and dispersed allocations when 𝛾 < 0, which we will illustrate in simulation. Our simulations illustrate how by varying just this single parameter, a large set of collective allocation patterns can be reached from identical initial conditions, with predictable trends in group cohesion as agents are made more cooperative or more antagonistic. Consider 𝑁 a = 10 agents and 𝑁 o = 10 sample sites, with the agents following the dynamics given in Eqn. (9) and interacting over a complete graph. Fig. 3 illustrates how increasing 𝛾 from a sufficiently negative value to a sufficiently positive value tunes group cohesion through a sequence of macroscopic states, from a maximally dispersed allocation with one agent at each sampling site, through intermediate allocations with several self-organized subgroups, to consensus with all agents gathered at a single site. This tunable group cohesion observed in simulation illustrates how the same set of decision rules can support different task allocation objectives. In particular, for different choices of 𝛾 , the resulting allocation pattern can be interpreted within the taxonomy of multi-robot task allocation in [20]. If the number of distinct clusters is less than 𝑁 o, then some of the sample sites receive multiple agents, which corresponds to the “single-task robots, multi-robot tasks, instantaneous assignment” (ST-MR-IA) problem, also known as coalition formation [20]. The equivalent optimization problem is the set partitioning problem, i.e., partition the 𝑁 a agents among 𝑁 o sample sites, where each sample site can be assigned multiple agents. In contrast, if the number of distinct clusters is equal to 𝑁 o, then the agents are maximally dispersed, which corresponds to the “single-task robots, single-robot tasks, instantaneous assignment” (ST-SR-IA) problem [20]. This is equivalent to the optimal assignment problem, i.e., we want to distribute 𝑁 a agents among 𝑁 o (= 𝑁 a) options, where each sample site is assigned exactly one agent. ## 5 AUV simulation study 

We evaluate the proposed distributed allocation approach for the motivating scenario in MOOS-IvP-Multi, a marine multi-agent autonomy framework extending the MOOS-IvP open-source autonomy project [6]. Our goal is to examine whether the maxi-mally dispersed allocation behavior observed above persists under communication and vehicle-motion constraints representative of a real-world AUV deployment, thus fur-ther validating the opinion-guided allocation strategy. The simulations focus on the distributed autonomy of the AUVs using the theoretical results developed above. A group of ten AUVs completes the distributed allocation process for ten sample site locations, each with a capacity of one, making the problem a perfect matching. The ten locations are randomly placed within a square 6km region. Optimal placement of the USVs, which act as navigation beacons, is outside the scope of these simulations. Communication range was limited to 3.5km, consistent with the typical ranges reported for the WHOI Micro-Modem [18] used in shelf and offshore environments. Each AUV broadcasts packets to all AUVs within range, with no acknowledgment or retransmission. A linear sound-speed profile was assumed across the region. Packets were broadcast at 30 s intervals. A dedicated IvP behavior implemented the motion update law, expressing preferences over heading and speed. Even with the long broadcast interval, the time-scale of the nonlinear opinion dynamics process was faster than the time-scale of the AUV motion, whose speed was capped at 1.0 m/s, consistent with the performance of low-cost vehicles. Fig. 10 shows three simulation trials with identical sample sites and independently randomized initial AUV locations. In every trial, all ten AUVs converged to distinct sample sites and the track lines are shown in Fig. 10. Discrete updates and limited range reduced performance compared to the idealized scenarios considered in earlier sections, with the optimality gap of the emergent allocation from the Hungarian optimum ranging from 4.5%-16%, consistent with the other numerical simulation studies, e.g. Fig. 4. Overall, these results are encouraging and represent a step toward deployment of the proposed strategy on physical vehicles.
