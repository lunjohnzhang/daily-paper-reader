# Real-Time Human-Adaptive Task Allocation for Multi-Human Multi-Robot Supervision 

Seabin Lee 1†, Sujeong Park 1†, Nayoung Kim 1, Sungjin Park 2, Haechan Jung 1, and Changjoo Nam 1∗

Abstract —We propose a human-factor-aware method of al-locating robot supervision tasks to multiple human operators. In scenarios where multiple operators occasionally teleoperate multiple robots to help the robots overcome difficulties, the allocation of the supervisory control tasks to humans needs to consider the real-time cognitive states of individual operators. However, most existing methods assume fixed supervisory capac-ity per operator and overlook fluctuations in the human factors such as workload and fatigue. As a result, workload distribution can be unbalanced where some operators become overloaded while the others remain underused. Our method dynamically regulates supervisory capacity and allocates tasks in a way that maintains balanced mental workload, prevents overload, and improves overall team performance. The allocation method uses a greedy strategy that minimizes estimated operator workloads with task prioritization. Robots are assigned to operators by reflecting their current supervisory capacity where the required effort depends on the types of tasks. In the user study, the analysis across predefined time intervals shows that the proposed method consistently achieves higher performance and lower behavioral signs of fatigue compared to a baseline method that does not consider human factors. These results highlight adaptive capacity adjustment as an effective preventive mechanism for sustaining operator performance in long-duration, high-demand settings. 

Index Terms —Human-robot interaction, multi-robot systems, supervisory control 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

The deployment of multi-robot systems in real-world envi-ronments continues to expand, aiming toward full autonomy. Multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) increases throughput and coordination by determining which robot performs which task [1]. Although MRTA followed by task and motion planning advances autonomy, current systems still require human supervisors to intervene when robots fail to deal with unexpected events and dynamic changes [2]. Human supervision remains essential because it pairs robotic precision with human judgment and can enhance safety and accuracy. This means that another task allocation is required between human operators and the dynamically emerging supervision tasks.

However, there are only a few studies on multi-human task allocation in the context of supervising multi-robot systems, and the existing ones typically assume fixed throughput of the human operators, overlooking variations in workload, fatigue, and task performance [3]–[5]. This assumption would no longer be valid if a small number of operators supervise many robots, as human cognitive resources are fundamentally limited [2]. During prolonged supervision, workloads tend to increase and performance can decline, requiring the human interface to adapt to the cognitive and physiological states of operators in human-in-the-loop settings [6]. In addition, oper-ators may differ in skill and performance even for the same type of tasks. Therefore, an effective human-task allocation must not only satisfy mission requirements but also account for real-time human factors and technical capacity. We propose Human-factor-Aware Multi-robot Allocation (HAMA), an adaptive method for multi-human multi-robot (MHMR) supervision that maintains robot task performance while considering human states. As illustrated in Fig. 1, arXiv:2610.00897v1 [cs.RO] 1 Oct 2026 2

HAMA estimates human factors such as workload and fatigue and updates the supervisory capacity of each operator (i.e., the maximum number of tasks that can be supervised) at predefined time intervals. We focus on two task types that require supervisory intervention: multi-robot deadlock resolu-tion and docking at charging stations, where failures frequently occur in real-world scenarios. For missions with changing task priorities, HAMA supports reallocation to prioritize more urgent robots over earlier assignments. HAMA also accounts for differences in human factors between task types and across operators by using Area-Of-Interest (AOI) dwell time to estimate visual demand. Then robots requiring teleoperation are assigned to operators based on task priority, visual demand, and current capacity. HAMA introduces dynamic supervisory capacity that adapts to human factors and mission performance, replacing fixed capacity limits used in prior systems. By integrating subjective reports, behavioral indicators, and physiological signals into a unified update rule, HAMA provides individualized operator capacities and enables real-time robot–operator reallocation as middleware. In user studies, HAMA improved team per-formance by about 10% and reduced blink counts by 16% compared to a fixed-capacity baseline, demonstrating that adaptive capacity can increase multi-robot throughput while mitigating fatigue in long-duration, high-demand settings. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Task Allocation Strategies 

The task allocation problem has been widely studied to improve efficiency and mission throughput in multi-robot systems, with more recent work incorporating safety con-siderations such as collision avoidance to enable scalable and reliable coordination [1], [7]–[9]. While most MRTA formulations focus on allocating tasks among robots, human-in-the-loop systems additionally require allocating robots to human operators [10], [11]. Prior studies have shown that the number of robots an operator can manage is closely tied to workload and decision time, exhibiting strong sensitivity to human state [12]. Building on this insight, collaborative human–robot scheduling approaches explicitly model human assignments, availability, and performance constraints within the optimization process [13], [14]. Together, these works indicate that operator capacity should be treated as a variable influenced by human factors, rather than as a fixed constant in the allocation problem. 

B. Human-in-the-Loop Control 

Supervisory control often requires continuous monitoring, managing multiple robots simultaneously, and making rapid decisions in time-sensitive situations. These demands place heavy strain on attention and working memory, which can be depleted during prolonged supervision [2], [15]. As a result, operator workload and fatigue have a direct impact on task performance [16]. To mitigate these effects, prior studies have explored interface-level support strategies such as guid-ing visual attention and adjusting interface transparency [7], [17]. However, such approaches typically intervene only after performance degradation becomes apparent and do not adjust task allocation in response to ongoing changes in operator state. Moreover, even under identical task conditions, operators differ substantially in how quickly they become fatigued or overwhelmed, and these individual differences can affect overall team performance. Motivated by these limitations, recent work has begun to integrate human factors directly into task allocation frame-works [18], [19]. For example, [18] proposed a model that incorporates cognitive workload and affective state when distributing tasks across multiple operators. However, such methods rely on pre-recorded data and trained models, making it difficult to reflect real-time changes or adapt to individual differences during live operation. Despite these advances, empirical research on task allocation frameworks that dynam-ically update operator capacity and adapt task distribution in real time remains limited. Accordingly, the present study examines how real-time estimation of operator state can enable adaptive and balanced task distribution in multi-human–multi-robot environments. 

C. Human Factors in Measuring Cognitive States 

In practical teleoperation settings, operator state cannot be fully explained by a single factor [20], [21], as cognitive workload and fatigue interactively influence overall perfor-mance. For example, even under low workload conditions, accumulated cognitive fatigue can lead to performance degra-dation. In contrast, operators with lower fatigue can maintain performance despite high workload demands. Consequently, relying on a single measure introduces limitations in sensitivity and interpretability. To address this limitation, recent studies [18], [22] have integrated physiological, behavioral, and self-reported ques-tionnaire data to achieve more comprehensive estimation of operator state. Each measure captures a distinct aspect of the human cognitive state [23], and their integration provides a more comprehensive and complementary representation of human performance dynamics [18]. Reliable task allocation in MHMR systems requires accurate estimation of the cognitive state for each human operator. While many previous stud-ies [24], [25] have focused on real-time cognitive workload as a predictor of immediate task demands, prolonged opera-tion also induces cognitive fatigue, which gradually depletes mental resources over time. To maintain consistent supervision performance and prevent overload, it is important to consider both mental workload and fatigue together [26]. 

1) Mental Workload: Mental workload represents the cog-nitive effort required during task execution and can fluctuate based on task complexity, frequency of attention shifts, and decision-making demands [27], [28]. As noted by Wick-ens [29], excessive load on shared cognitive channels can lead to interference and reduce task efficiency. Although NASA-TLX [30] is widely used to measure workload subjectively, it lacks the temporal resolution to track changes continuously during operations. To overcome this limitation, several studies [31], [32] have employed objective indicators such as AOI dwell time obtained 31 2 3

2) Cognitive Fatigue: Cognitive fatigue refers to the grad-ual decline in cognitive efficiency that occurs during prolonged task engagement [34], [35]. It results from the depletion of attentional and mental resources over time, leading to reduced alertness, attention span, and slower decision-making. Accord-ing to the vigilance decrement model [36], sustained cognitive effort without sufficient recovery can diminish performance and reduce operational reliability. In remote environments such as teleoperation [37], supervisory control of autonomous systems [38], or prolonged VR-based monitoring [39], fatigue can gradually accumulate over time. This can undermine human-system coordination and increase the risk of delayed or incorrect responses. While subjective fatigue scales can capture perceived tired-ness, they are often administered periodically and cannot re-flect continuous fluctuations during operations. To complement self-reported measures, several studies employed physiological indicators such as blink rate, which has been shown to increase with cognitive fatigue [40], [41] and reduced engagement [42]. A higher blink rate typically reflects increased cognitive fa-tigue, serving as a sensitive indicator of resource depletion over time [40]. The blink rate is non-intrusive, continuously measurable, and effective in detecting fatigue in real time without interfering with task performance [25]. We consider a warehouse scenario in which multiple mobile robots navigate narrow passages formed by densely packed shelving units (as shown in Fig. 2a). A team of human opera-tors remotely supervise the robots via a monitoring interface. While navigation is largely autonomous, certain events require human intervention, and the system must allocate operators to robots under limited attention. We focus on two representative intervention-triggering states: deadlock , where the moves of a robot are constrained by surrounding robots or obstacles in narrow spaces (Fig. 2b), and manual docking , which occurs when autonomous docking fails near battery depletion owing to state estimation uncertainty (Fig. 2c). We note that docking tasks occur in proximity to the designated docking stations. Although illustrated in a logistics setting, our approach applies broadly to multi-robot systems in which human intervention can efficiently resolve challenging situations. Let R denote the set of robots and O denote the set of operators. Each operator o ∈ O can supervise at most Co ≥ 1

robots concurrently, whose upper bound is determined by the cognitive capability of the operator. As cognitive capability can fluctuate, the system regularly updates Co at predefined time intervals, called sessions . Since the operator supervises the robots through a user interface, Co cannot exceed the number of monitoring windows where each window transmits the ego-view of a robot. In the running example shown in Fig. 2, a VR display with four monitoring windows is used. Each monitoring window displays a single supervised robot, and the operator selects which robot to intervene for among the presented options via keyboard input. In our work, the VR display is chosen to provide an immersive environment as 4

well as measure human state indicators, though the interface is not limited to VR 1. The system maintains a supervision task queue Q ⊆ R that contains robots currently requiring human supervision (i.e., deadlock or manual docking states). The queue is updated continuously as events occur and are resolved. Our objective is to assign robots in Q to operators in O to reduce the mental workload of the operators while satisfying the capacity constraints (i.e., Co for all o ∈ O ). IV. We propose an algorithm that computes a human-factor–aware task allocation using the capacity of operators estimated based on real-time human state indicators (i.e., men-tal workload, cognitive fatigue, and blink counts). It consists of (i) the method for updating the operator capacity from real-time measurements and (ii) the allocation method based on the capacity estimates.

B. Task Allocation Strategy 

HAMA allocates supervision requests greedily in an online manner whenever the priority queue Q is non-empty and at least one available operator is assigned fewer tasks than their current capacity. It assigns the task at the head of Q to the operator whose relative workload would be lowest after the assignment. The relative workload does not need to be measured on an absolute scale, as the allocation only requires identifying the operator with the lowest workload among all operators. The tasks in Q are ordered in descending order of the priority score which is calculated based on task type and urgency. A specific rule for scoring tasks can differ from the mission environments and task types. In our logistics scenario, the maximum possible score of the docking task is higher than the deadlock task as delayed intervention can lead to critical battery depletion. Since all docking tasks occur in proximity to the docking stations, we do not differentiate the importance among them. The scores of deadlock tasks are calculated based on the distance from the goal locations. Robots that are farther from their goal are handled earlier. This strategy encourages robots to be distributed more widely, thereby securing local free space around the deadlock and reducing the likelihood of secondary deadlocks. On the other hand, the workload of an operator is deter-mined by considering the difficulty of the assigned tasks. In human supervision, task difficulty is perceived subjectively and can vary across operators. For instance, one operator may find deadlock resolution more demanding, whereas another may 5

perceive manual docking as more challenging. Because work-load itself is inherently subjective, such individual differences should be reflected in its estimation. To account for this variability, we estimate the relative difficulty of different task types for each operator using their average AOI dwell times measured in the previous session. Specifically, we compute the ratio of average dwell times between task types and use this ratio to scale the base unit workload assigned to each task. For example, if an operator exhibited average AOI dwell times of 30 seconds for docking tasks and 45 seconds for deadlock tasks in the previous session, a ratio of 1 : 1 .5 is applied in the next session. As a result, deadlock tasks are assigned a workload weight of 1.5,while docking tasks retain a unit workload. In the case of deadlock resolution, precedence constraints must be enforced, whereby robots outside a deadlock cluster must first move away to enable inner robots to escape. As the composition of the deadlock cluster evolves during deadlock resolution, the set of robots to be prioritized must be updated dynamically. We identify such outer robots by computing the convex hull of the robots in the deadlock cluster and designating those on the hull boundary as outer robots, as illustrated in Fig. 3. For operators assigned with inner robots during deadlock resolution, HAMA replaces those inner robots with the currently identified outer robots. V. U SER STUDY

D. Experimental Design 

We employed a within-subjects design in which each team experienced both allocation conditions: a baseline strategy and the proposed human-factor-aware method. To mitigate learning effects, the order of conditions was counterbalanced across teams. Operators supervised 20 mobile robots in a warehouse through the VR display and a physical keyboard. Each mon-itoring window in the VR display shows a single supervision task where the operators could choose which robot to intervene using the keyboard (the key mapping is shown in the bottom-left of Fig. 2). Fig. 5 shows the four ego-view monitoring windows in the VR display. The number of monitoring win-dows is determined based on prior studies [45], [46], which indicate that remote operators can effectively supervise up to four robots. A top-view window on the side summarizes the overall warehouse state. We compared HAMA against a non-adaptive baseline in the same task environment, which maintained a fixed operator capacity of four. Additionally, attention-based load estimation was disabled by applying equal weighting to all task types. However, boundary-based reallocation remained enabled. F. Analysis 

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statis-tics with a significance level of 0.05 . A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (RM-ANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effects of the algorithm and session. When significant main effects were observed, post-hoc Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons were conducted to assess differences between conditions. No significant interaction was found between two factors (p > 0.05 ). During the ten experimental sessions, HAMA progressively adjusted the capacity of each operator using the human factors. This process reflected the cumula-tive influence of adaptation over time. Because the adaptive algorithm requires data from the previous session to update operator capacity, adaptation begins only after session two. Accordingly, we conducted RM-ANOVA using data from sessions 3 through 10. VI. R ESULTS

D. Algorithm 

We also summarize the overall effect of the algorithm on key measures and computational cost. Across sessions 3–10, HAMA increased average task performance from 16.22 to 17.86 (about a 10% increase) relative to baseline. Over the same sessions, blink count decreased from 66.39 to 55.92 (about a 16% reduction), indicating lower behavioral signs of fatigue under HAMA. In contrast, NASA-TLX scores changed by less than 1% between algorithms (30.90 to 30.93), and cognitive fatigue ratings decreased by about 7% (5.54 to 5.15) without a significant main effect of algorithm. We also evaluated the computational cost required for allocation decisions. HAMA, despite processing additional human factor data, recorded an average allocation latency of 1.34 ms, compared to 1.26 ms for the baseline. This negligible difference demonstrates that HAMA improves team performance and mitigates physiological fatigue with minimal computational overhead, maintaining real-time responsiveness. VII. D ISCUSSION 

We presented HAMA, a human-factor-aware task allocation algorithm for MHMR supervision. We treat supervisory capac-ity as a controllable variable instead of a fixed constant. The algorithm estimates operator state from behavioral and self-reported subjective indicators to update per-operator capacity at predefined session boundaries and allocates supervision tasks to operators online. As shown in Fig. 6e, HAMA adjusted per-operator supervisory capacity across sessions, whereas the baseline remained fixed. As a result, HAMA improved objective performance while reducing fatigue-related behavior without increasing perceived workload. While HAMA increased the number of successfully completed tasks compared with a fixed-capacity baseline, it reduced blink counts, which indicates lower behavioral signs of fatigue. 

A. Efficiency Gains through Adaptive Allocation 

A key insight is that higher throughput did not require higher individual capacity. In our study, HAMA often as-signed fewer concurrent robots per operator than the baseline. Even with this reduction, performance improved. This result suggests that overload can reduce the quality and speed of interventions in MHMR supervision. HAMA mitigates this risk by redistributing supervisory demand toward operators with stable performance signals and away from operators with rising strain signals. The mechanism aligns with the goal of adaptive automation, which aims to keep human demand within a functional range rather than maximizing assigned volume. 

B. Behavioral Sensitivity Beyond Subjective Workload Ratings 

The results show a gap between behavioral indicators and subjective reports. The blink count changed with the algorithm, while NASA-TLX and fatigue ratings did not. This gap can occur in high-demand monitoring tasks where perceived workload becomes less sensitive once cognitive resources are saturated. In addition, NASA-TLX and single-item fatigue ratings are collected after each session and may miss short-term fluctuations during the session. These findings support the use of complementary measures when evaluating adaptive allocation systems, since subjective reports alone may not reflect changes in physiological or behavioral state. 

C. Limitations 

Several limitations should be considered. The study used a VR interface with a small set of intervention tasks, namely deadlock resolution and manual docking. The participant group had limited prior experience with this supervision setting. In addition, capacity is updated at session boundaries, which prioritizes stable operation and reduces sensitivity to transient fluctuations within a session. If faster adaptation is desirable, a shorter update time can be explored. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

HAMA provides a lightweight and real-time approach for human-adaptive allocation in MHMR supervision. The frame-work improved team performance and reduced fatigue-related behavior with negligible computational overhead. Future work will test longer deployments and more diverse operator pop-ulations. The observed effects across measures open a path toward more principled weighting of the integrated human-factor score, with objective indicators taking a larger role when they show clear algorithm sensitivity and subjective measures serving as complementary signals for robustness. 9
