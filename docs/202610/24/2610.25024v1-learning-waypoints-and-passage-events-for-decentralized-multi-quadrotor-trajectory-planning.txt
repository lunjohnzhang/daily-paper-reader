Abstract —Multi-quadrotor passage planning requires coordi-nated spatial routes and passage times under vehicle dynamics and actuation constraints. Optimizing these decisions jointly couples temporal allocation, nonlinear motion, and inter-vehicle interactions, creating a computational challenge for local trajec-tory generation. We propose a learning–optimization framework that predicts spatial waypoints and ordered passage events for independently solved quadrotor planners. A common physical time grid aligns the vehicles, with intermediate events fixed by the networks and terminal progress optimized locally to permit different completion metrics. Differentiable sequential convex programming incorporates rigid-body dynamics and rotor-thrust bounds, allowing collision and completion gradients to train both networks through the generated trajectories. In a two-quadrotor evaluation across 24 layouts, the adapted policy passes physical feasibility and sampled separation checks in all five held-out layouts. Their mean completion metric decreases by 1.52% relative to the inherited policy. The median forward time is 21.01 ms on the held-out exchange layout with matching numerical trajectory references. These results demonstrate that learned spatial and event parameters can coordinate independent rigid-body planners while preserving local vehicle constraints in the evaluated scenarios. 

Index Terms —Multi-robot planning, quadrotors, differentiable optimization, event timing, sequential convex programming. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Multi-quadrotor passage planning concerns the coordinated motion of several vehicles through prescribed gates toward individual targets. Such tasks arise in swarm racing and navigation through cluttered environments, where vehicles share limited passage space [1], [2]. Successful traversal requires each trajectory to satisfy vehicle dynamics and each pair of vehicles to maintain suitable separation. Spatial route selection and passage timing therefore form a common planning problem for coordinated flight. The central computational difficulty is the coupling between passage timing, vehicle motion, and inter-vehicle separation. Changing an arrival time changes where a vehicle meets its neighbors, and generating the corresponding motion requires satisfying nonlinear attitude and actuation constraints. Solving these decisions jointly introduces temporal variables and interaction constraints into the trajectory optimization. This coupling motivates three complementary approaches: direct optimization, learned motion generation, and hybrid learning– optimization planning. Optimization-based methods provide explicit representations of trajectory objectives and physical constraints. Minimum-snap and MINCO formulations exploit polynomial structure for efficient trajectory generation [3], [4], while complementary progress constraints (CPC) optimize ordered waypoint comple-tion together with motion [1], [5]. Decentralized planners such as MADER and Robust MADER incorporate shared trajectory information into local collision handling [6], [7]. The resulting formulations make passage allocation and inter-vehicle coupling explicit, identifying the decisions that a learned planner can help organize. Learning-based methods offer a direct mapping from ob-servations to motion decisions. Sensor-to-trajectory policies have been trained through privileged imitation learning for high-speed flight [8], and differentiable simulation has supplied analytical gradients for quadrotor control from states and visual features [9]. Their training objectives encode desired behavior in network parameters and support rapid inference. For con-strained multi-vehicle passage, this motivates learning spatial and temporal decisions together with a physical trajectory-generation model. Hybrid learning–optimization methods provide a mechanism for coupling neural predictions to constrained planning. Alloc-Net predicts segment durations for a differentiable trajectory quadratic program [10], Jiang et al. jointly learn waypoints and time allocations [11], and Marino et al. combine learned multi-UAV trajectory parameters with a differentiable correction layer [12]. These approaches show how optimization gradients can shape planning parameters during training. Inspired by these approaches, we learn spatial and event parameters to coordinate independently solved rigid-body trajectories on a shared time grid. This design involves three linked challenges. First, pairwise separation must be evaluated at common physical times even when the vehicles complete their routes at different times. Second, the trajectory layer must incorporate nonlinear rigid-body motion and rotor-level limits while retaining usable derivatives for network training. Third, independent local solves need spatial and temporal decisions that respond to arXiv:2610.25024v1 [cs.RO] 18 Oct 2026 neighboring vehicles. A suitable hierarchy must therefore connect synchronized event allocation, constrained local motion, and inter-vehicle learning within one training objective. We propose a learning–optimization framework that connects these three requirements through spatial waypoints and passage events. As illustrated in Fig. 1, shared networks use the vehicle state, known gate locations, and current relative neighbor positions to predict waypoint centers and ordered intermediate events. Independent differentiable CPC-based planners then generate trajectories on a common physical grid, and the outer objective evaluates collision and completion costs across the resulting trajectories. This hierarchy makes learned event allocation the connection between multi-vehicle coordination and local physical planning. The framework has three main technical contributions: 1) A shared-grid event representation organizes spatial targets and passage timing for independent local planners. Continuous intermediate timestamps parameterize inter-polated passage constraints, and the final CPC progress distribution remains a local decision variable. This con-struction supports common-time interaction evaluation together with vehicle-specific completion metrics. 2) A differentiable sequential convex programming (SCP) trajectory layer incorporates rigid-body dynamics and individual rotor-thrust limits. Local approximations of the dynamics and terminal progress yield convex conic subproblems whose parameterization satisfies disciplined parametrized programming. The layer provides a con-strained trajectory map through which waypoint and event predictions can be trained. 3) An outer coordination objective adapts both networks through independently generated trajectories. Collision penalties act on synchronized vehicle positions, and the completion term links the learned parameters to local progress. This objective makes inter-vehicle behavior part of parameter learning while keeping the trajectory solves independent. The numerical evaluation examines coordination, physical feasibility, and computation within a two-vehicle setting. Gate-perturbation and goal-exchange layouts test spatial and temporal adaptation, a frozen-policy experiment examines thrust-difference regularization, and selected finite differences check the network derivatives. Warm-reference forward timings characterize the solver’s computational cost. Together, these experiments assess the framework through trajectory behavior, local constraint satisfaction, and measured planning cost. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Waypoint Timing and Trajectory Optimization 

Polynomial minimum-snap trajectories provide an effi-cient representation for quadrotor flight through waypoint sequences [3]. MINCO provides a compact polynomial rep-resentation with efficient spatial–temporal deformation and supports geometrical and dynamic constraints through trajectory optimization [4]. CPC models ordered completion through continuous progress variables and jointly optimizes passage allocation and vehicle motion [5]. Shen et al. extend this principle to a swarm racing task [1]. Our formulation retains complementary progress only for the final event and uses learned continuous timestamps for intermediate events, which restrict the feasible trajectories to those compatible with the assigned passage times. 

B. Decentralized Multi-Vehicle Planning 

MADER generates trajectories asynchronously using en-closing polyhedra, optimized separating planes, and collision checks against other agents’ committed trajectories [6]. Robust MADER extends trajectory sharing with delay checks and a two-step publication scheme to accommodate communication delays in dynamic environments [7]. EGO-Swarm combines gradient-based local planning, collision-risk penalties, and lightweight topological trajectory generation for decentralized navigation in cluttered environments [2]. These methods make exchanged trajectory information part of decentralized collision handling. In our formulation, current relative neighbor positions condition the shared networks, and synchronized local trajectories enter the outer collision objective during training. 

C. Learning with Differentiable Planners 

Differentiable MPC learns controller costs and dynamics by differentiating the KKT conditions of a convex approx-imation at a controller fixed point [13]. AllocNet predicts segment durations and differentiates through the fixed-duration trajectory QP [10]. Jiang et al. combine depth-based waypoint prediction, time allocation, and differentiable minimum-snap optimization [11]. Marino et al. predict MINVO control points and a learned collision constraint from point clouds and communicated features, then use an OptNet-based QP layer during imitation learning [12], [14]. These works establish the broader learning–optimization architecture. Our focus is the event representation and the separation of outer inter-agent collision learning from local rotor-level planning. For interacting aerial systems, learned model predictive games project neural reference strategies into dynamically feasible trajectories and train with game objectives [15]. DiffCo-ord differentiates a distributed ADMM–DDP pipeline to learn problem and solver parameters for cooperative trajectories [16]. Our local optimizers interact through shared observations and the outer training objective. This choice reduces explicit coupling in each local solve, while leaving collision avoidance dependent on the learned policy and numerical validation. Learning also connects visual observations to aerial motion. Loquercio et al. train a sensor-to-trajectory policy through privileged imitation learning in simulation for high-speed flight [8]. Heeg et al. train quadrotor control policies using analytical gradients through differentiable dynamics and learned visual-state representations [9]. These studies provide con-text for trajectory-level learning and dynamics-based policy gradients. A. Waypoint Network 

A shared encoder maps each relative neighbor position to a feature vector using a linear layer and a tanh nonlinearity. Mean pooling gives a fixed-size neighborhood embedding, which is concatenated with the own state and gate coordinates. A waypoint head predicts bounded offsets from a smooth geometric reference. For example, the waypoint block in Fig. 1 represents intermediate spatial points whose locations can adjust the route before and after a prescribed gate. These points become event centers for the local planner; their passage times are assigned by the event network.

C. Numerical Forward Backend 

The forward backend reuses the sparse conic template and directly updates Clarabel numerical data [21]. A cached dynamics function supplies the state and control derivatives used for linearization [22]. Adaptive stopping checks the current nonlinear dynamics defect, terminal CPC residual, physical bounds, event constraints, and quaternion consistency. A stored trajectory can initialize the numerical reference when available; otherwise, the planner constructs an initial reference for the requested layout. Template reuse and numerical-reference reuse are separate mechanisms: the former preserves the problem structure, while the latter also supplies state, thrust, and progress values. Their evaluation protocols are specified in Sec. VI. No online pairwise collision constraint is added to the local planner. VI. E XPERIMENTAL EVALUATION

VII. D ISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

The shared network adapts spatial waypoints and intermedi-ate events so that independently solved rigid-body trajectories coordinate on a common physical grid. The final progress variable permits different completion metrics while retaining post-event motion. Thrust-difference regularization reduces the measured control jumps, and the forward backend pro-vides millisecond-scale execution with matching trajectory references. The evidence concerns state-based two-vehicle planning with gates represented by position tolerance balls. Terminal CPC uses a local approximation, and inter-vehicle collision avoidance is learned through the outer objective and assessed by sampled trajectory audits.
