A B S T R A C T 

Coordinating many miniature robots in a confined arena, with no leader, central planner, or global map, is a core challenge for swarm robotics. As the swarm grows denser, collision avoidance becomes the limiting factor, and the number of safely coexisting robots depends on the formulation of the inter-robot safety constraint. We present a distributed predictive flocking controller in which each robot solves its own nonlinear model predictive control (NMPC) problem, optimizing swarm cohesion and alignment costs, inter-robot and obstacle safety, and a task objective, while exchanging only predicted trajectories with local neighbors. Within this generalized controller architecture, solved in real time, we compare six barrier and control-barrier-function (CBF) safety formulations on a digital twin of an open-source vibration-driven bristlebot in a 0.64 m 2 arena. A look-ahead discrete-time CBF performs best overall, with the fewest collisions and the widest inter-robot margins across nearly every swarm size tested. We deploy it on phototaxis, phototactic orbiting, and cohesion without a stimulus tasks, using swarm sizes starting from ten robots and up to the geometric packing limit. The swarm holds well: collisions stay low in small swarms, rising steeply as the packing limit approaches, while coordination and cohesion degrade gently rather than collapsing. The swarm is found not to trade safety for order, keeping both across the confined arena, with local alignment high at every swarm size. 

## 1. Introduction 

Autonomous multi-robot systems are a promising approach for tasks in cluttered, inaccessible, or hazardous spaces, from confined-structure inspection to search in collapsed buildings and medical micro-robotics [1]. In these settings, many miniature robots must coordinate in proximity within a tight, bounded arena. As in natural swarms, such as flocking of starlings, schooling of fish, the crowded colonies of ants and honey bees, and even the collective migration of cells, robust group behavior emerges from purely local interactions among simple agents [2, 3, 4], with no central control [1]. Miniaturization, however, tightly bounds onboard compute, memory, and power [5], demanding control that is theoretically principled yet computationally light. Moreover, when the arena is fixed, a growing number of robots increases the density of the swarm and hinders smooth motion. When it is also cluttered with obstacles, coordination is even harder. In this scenario, collision avoidance becomes the central challenge. The standard way to coordinate such a swarm from purely local interactions is Reynolds’ boid model [6, 7], which reduces collective motion to three local rules (cohesion, alignment, and separation) that produce coherent group motion without global information [8]. Early control laws formalized these as artificial potential fields over a communication graph [9, 10], and reactive velocity-matching rules scale to tens of drones [11]. Such reactive methods, however, offer no predictive horizon, no formal safety guarantee under nonlinear dynamics, and no systematic handling of kinodynamic limits. These shortcomings are especially severe in confined, high-density settings, where many underactuated robots have little room to recover from unsafe motions.

Model predictive control (MPC) [12] addresses these limitations. For the nonlinear dynamics of mobile robots, this takes the form of nonlinear MPC (NMPC). It solves a finite-horizon optimal control problem (OCP) online, which provides closed-loop feedback, constraint satisfaction, and explicit multi-objective trade-offs. Distributed MPC (DMPC) extends this to a swarm by splitting the global problem into per-agent OCPs coupled through shared constraints [13, 14, 15, 16]. Solvers such as the Sequential Quadratic Programming real-time iteration (SQP-RTI) [17] solve each per-agent OCP in real time. Similarly, optimization-based swarm planners achieve strong real-world navigation [18, 19, 20, 21]. These methods are mainly developed for point-to-point navigation [14, 22], with collision avoidance typically encoded as geometric constraints such as separating hyperplanes or safe corridors. The closest works embed Reynolds behaviors in NMPC. They have been demonstrated on up to 16 quadrotors [23, 24], leader-follower teams [25, 26, 27], and fixed-wing unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) [28]. Most share one regime. They assume sparse flight in large 3D airspace, with the swarm migrating toward a shared goal. Even when several obstacles are present, they remain sparse relative to the available space, keeping the environment effectively low-density. Few address the opposite regime that we target, where many underactuated robots coordinate at high density in a fixed, confined, and cluttered arena under a task objective such as following a light gradient. Here, the inter-robot safety formulation, not free-space planning, governs how many robots can coexist. Each of these works, however, fixes a single safety formulation in advance, usually a distance constraint or a penalty. It remains open which formulation fits the most robots into a fixed arena with minimal collisions. Safety formulations differ in how collision avoidance is encoded as a constraint in the OCP. Such a constraint is classically formulated using a barrier function ℎ(𝑥 ) whose safe set  = { 𝑥 ∣ ℎ(𝑥 ) ≥ 0} is kept forward invariant to certify safety [29], typically imposed as a hard [30] or soft [31] distance constraint. A Control Barrier Function (CBF) [29] relaxes this strict boundary condition by allowing ℎ(𝑥 ) to decrease at a rate bounded by a class- function, and its discrete-time form (Dt-CBF) [32] imposes this at each MPC stage. Zeng et al. [33] enforces it as a hard constraint, which is often infeasible. Feasibility is restored by softening the decay with slack variables [34, 35, 36], adding terminal barrier terms [37, 38], or anchoring a soft CBF with one hard step [39]. Beyond feasibility, when the constraint has relative degree greater than one, as for a distance constraint under unicycle dynamics, a higher-order CBF (HOCBF) [40, 41] chains the barrier conditions. Lu et al. [39] compare a distance-constraint MPC, a hard discrete-time CBF, a slacked CBF, and their combination on a common footing, but for a single robot. In the multi-robot setting, safety is enforced by reactive schemes [42, 43], by post-hoc CBF-QP (quadratic program) filters [44], or by a CBF inside a decentralized MPC with neighbors as predicted trajectories [45, 46, 47, 48]. A formulation’s collision performance, however, likely changes with the swarm density. A comparison across densities is required to find the best one. In this work, we investigate the maximum number of miniature robots a distributed predictive controller can flock in a fixed arena, and design a controller to push that limit. Each robot solves its own NMPC combining Reynolds cohesion, alignment, and separation (gated by neighbor presence) with inter-robot/obstacle safety and the task objective. It selects the 𝐾 nearest neighbors within the communication radius and exchanges predicted trajectories synchronously as OCP parameters, giving a fully distributed, leader-free architecture with no central planner or global map. We evaluate it on a physics-informed digital twin [49] of the GRASPion [50], an open-source vibration-driven bristlebot studied as an active-matter model system. We characterize how densely the swarm packs a 0.64 m 2 arena while sustaining two behaviors. These are phototaxis (ascending toward a moving Gaussian light gradient) and a phototactic orbital mode (orbiting the source along the intensity level-set). We compare six inter-robot safety formulations on a common footing (Section 3.3). A slacked barrier paired with a discrete-time CBF at a forward handle [51] sustains the highest density with the fewest collisions. The main contributions of this paper are as follows: 1. A distributed predictive control system for dense swarms of miniature vibration-driven robots. Each robot solves its own NMPC fusing flocking, safety, and the task objective, coupled to neighbors only through exchanged predicted trajectories. 2. Demonstration that a single controller generalizes across qualitatively different collective behaviors. The same architecture produces gradient-ascending phototaxis, stable phototactic orbital formation, and generic flocking with no modification to the safety or coordination layers, showing that task objectives and coordination structure are separable and that new tasks can be added without re-deriving safety guarantees. 3. A careful comparison of six inter-robot safety formulations within one identical controller, isolating the safety constraint as the only varying factor. The Look-ahead Slack Dt-CBF gives the best overall trade-off for the phototaxis deployment target.

2.3. Control Architecture Overview 

Every robot runs an identical decentralized controller, outlined in Fig. 2. At each control step, the robot senses the local light field, and a behavior module maps this reading to a look-ahead reference that encodes where the robot should head next. A per-robot optimal control problem (OCP) tracks that reference. Flocking rules keep the group cohesive and heading-aligned, while barrier and control-barrier-function constraints enforce inter-robot and obstacle safety. Robots publish their predicted trajectories over the sensing graph and re-solve synchronously from their neighbors’ previous plans, coordinating the swarm without a central node. The rest of this section develops the remaining components in turn. These are the behavior module (Section 2.4), the flocking rules (Section 2.5), the six safety formulations (Sections 2.5.3–2.5.4), the reference-tracking and control-effort costs (Section 2.6), the assembled OCP (Section 2.7), and the decentralized execution loop (Section 2.8).

2.4.3. Extensibility to Arbitrary Behaviors 

Decoupling the reference generator from the OCP makes the architecture task-agnostic. Any behavior (directed migration, dispersal, transport, shape formation) can be integrated by mapping local observations to a look-ahead reference (𝒑 ref , 𝜃 ref , 𝑣 ref , 𝜔 ref ), while the DMPC preserves safety and flocking coherence. 

2.5. Reynolds Flocking Rules 

The behavior modes set each robot’s individual objective, while the collective motion arises from the local steering rules proposed by Reynolds [6, 7]. Cohesion binds robots into a group, alignment coordinates their heading, separation keeps them from colliding, and obstacle avoidance steers them clear of static hazards. The controller encodes cohesion and alignment as quadratic cost terms in the per-robot OCP, while separation and obstacle avoidance are enforced through barrier and discrete-time CBF constraints [29, 32]. We focus on the separation and obstacle avoidance by comparing six different formulations, ranging from hard barriers to slacked variants (Sections 2.5.3 and 2.5.4).

Each control step executes a synchronous parallel update [56] in which all robots compute their optimal control sequences simultaneously using only neighbor information from the previous timestep (Algorithm 1). 3.1. Simulation Setup 

We implemented our DMPC framework symbolically in Python using CasADi [59] and exported the resulting OCP to acados [17], which generates and compiles highly optimized C code via its code-generation pipeline. The solver uses the acados SQP-RTI scheme, ERK-4 integration, and the interior-point QP solver HPIPM [60], and a Gauss-Newton Hessian approximation of the OCP. Simulations were performed on the compute nodes of a throughput cluster, each with two 16-core 2.90 GHz Intel Xeon Gold 6326 (Ice Lake) processors, i.e. 32 physical cores, and 250 GB of RAM. Every run was pinned to a single core, so the per-robot solve times reported in Section 3.6 are single-core numbers. The physics environment was simulated using a custom 2D engine within a fixed 0.8 m × 0 .8 m workspace (horizon 

𝑁 = 20 , control step Δ𝑡 = 0 .1 s , up to 𝐾 = 5 neighbors). The NMPC and Reynolds penalty weights were tuned via Bayesian optimization with Optuna [61] to maximize swarm coherence while prioritizing safety and solver stability. The lower speed bound 𝑣 min = 0 .01 m/s (Table 3) keeps every robot moving instead of letting it stop. The environmental Gaussian light source rotated along a circular trajectory in the phototaxis experiment and remained stationary in the phototactic orbital experiment. All robots were initialized at uniform random positions in a central sub-region covering 90% of the arena  with a minimum initial separation of 𝐷 safe and ran for 9000 control steps ( 𝑇 sim = 900 s ), long enough for the swarm to reach and hold a steady state rather than being measured mid-transient.

3.2. Capacity and Operating Density 

We now characterize how densely the swarm can pack in the fixed arena. This section establishes the geometric capacity and selects the operating point used in the later analysis. 

Geometric capacity The swarm size is limited by the safety buffer, not the physical footprint. The elliptical footprint is small, only ≈ 11% of the arena at 𝑁 𝑏 = 50 , against ≈ 30% when the buffers are also considered. At 𝐷 safe = 7 cm , the buffers cap the swarm size at an upper limit of 𝑁 max = 137 non-overlapping safety disks of radius 𝐷 safe ∕2 (Table 1). This packing results in ≈ 82% area coverage, just below the ideal hexagonal 90 .7% because of boundary and finite-size effects. Feasibility Solver feasibility separates the hard and slacked formulations most sharply (Fig. 3a). The two hard formulations hold only about 90% to 92% feasibility at 𝑁 𝑏 = 25 . When a solver is infeasible (roughly 8% to 10% 

of the per-robot OCPs), the robot falls back to a reactive potential-field controller (goal attraction with inter-robot, obstacle, and wall repulsion). This matches the recursive-infeasibility limitation that Zeng et al. [33] identify for the hard discrete-time CBF. Softening the barrier with slack variables removes the problem. All four slacked formulations (Slack BF, Slack Dt-CBF, LA Slack Dt-CBF, and Slack Dt-HOCBF) hold at least 99 .4% feasibility across the entire density range, and 100% up to 𝑁 𝑏 = 25 ; the minimum over all densities and all four is 99 .47% , at the packing limit. The small remaining infeasibility comes from the hard arena-boundary constraint, not the safety constraint. At high density, robots pressed against the arena walls can briefly make it infeasible. Feasibility alone, however, does not separate these four. As the collision results show next, a formulation can be feasible everywhere yet still fail to keep robots apart. 

Collisions The formulations diverge in collision counts, not feasibility (Fig. 3b), and they separate by structure rather than by barrier order. At 𝑁 𝑏 = 100 the three formulations that pair a barrier anchor with a rate condition (d)–(f) average 

4.2 × 10 4 contacts, against 1.02 × 10 5 for the three without an anchor (a)–(c), a factor of 2.4. The two groups do not overlap at any density in the sweep. Carrying an anchor alongside the rate condition therefore matters more than which rate condition is used. Two further results follow within the anchored group. First, the look-ahead handle alone reduces contacts by a factor of 2.5 at low and moderate density, falling to 1.2 at the packing limit, where it takes them from 1.05 × 10 5 to 

8.6 × 10 4. Formulations (d) and (e) are identical apart from the handle, at the same decay rate 𝛼 = 0 .9, with disjoint confidence intervals at every density 𝑁 𝑏 ≥ 50 and no measurable solve-time cost. The reduction is therefore due to the handle, which gives the angular velocity direct authority over the constraint so a robot can steer around a neighbor rather than only brake. Second, the higher-order chain helps at moderate density and stops helping at the packing limit. The Slack Dt-HOCBF is second best in small swarms, but by 𝑁 𝑏 = 137 it falls behind the LA Slack Dt-CBF, 1.19×10 5

against 8.6×10 4. It does not fail by becoming infeasible, holding 100% feasibility across the whole range against 99 .5%

for (d) and (e). What it gives up is margin, and not just at the packing limit. The chain lets its robots run closer than the look-ahead variant at every density (Fig. 4a), 5.02 against 5.31 cm at the packing limit. Its two-step condition allows the barrier to shrink further before the robot has to act. That costs nothing when the arena has unoccupied space. As the available area shrinks, a smaller gap leads to contact. On this task, the deployed LA Slack Dt-CBF is best or tied-best at every density except 𝑁 𝑏 = 50 , where it is second behind the Slack Dt-HOCBF with disjoint confidence intervals. Elsewhere, the look-ahead advantage over the chain widens as the arena fills, from 1.11× at 𝑁 𝑏 = 100 to 1.39× at 

𝑁 𝑏 = 137 .

Safety margins The formulations also differ in the safety margin they sustain (Fig. 4). These runs use the deployed closed loop with the reactive layer active (Section 3.1), and are measured on post-steering positions, so they never show interpenetration. Each value is a mean over robots and steps of the nearest-neighbor distance, not a per-step minimum. The margins separate along the same structural line as the collision counts. On the inter-robot axis, the three anchored formulations (d)–(f) hold 10 .1–10 .6 cm at 𝑁 𝑏 = 10 and 5.0–5.3 cm at the packing limit. The three without an anchor (a)–(c) fall from 7.8–8.6 cm to 4.3–4.5 cm . Comparing group means, the anchor sustains roughly 

0.7 cm of extra clearance at maximum crowding. The deployed LA Slack Dt-CBF is widest of all at high density, with 

5.82 cm at 𝑁 𝑏 = 100 and 5.31 cm at 𝑁 max = 137 . At 𝑁 𝑏 = 25 , only the anchored formulations still hold a clear margin above 𝐷 safe , at 7.6–8.8 cm . The unanchored group sits at 6.8–7.1 cm , at or just either side of it. Beyond that density, every formulation operates inside the nominal buffer, already 6.5–6.8 against 5.8–5.9 cm at 𝑁 𝑏 = 50 . The task drives this, not the choice of formulation. Phototaxis gathers the swarm at the light peak, so the robots there sit far closer than the arena average of 30% buffer coverage at 𝑁 𝑏 = 50 (Table 1) would suggest, and neighboring buffers have to overlap. What the anchor leads to is a consistently larger share of the space that remains, and from 𝑁 𝑏 = 75 upward the formulation that holds the widest spacing also records the fewest contacts. On the robot-obstacle axis, the ordering is similar, but the effect concentrates at the low-density end. At 𝑁 𝑏 = 10 

the Slack Dt-CBF and the LA Slack Dt-CBF (d),(e) keep about 5.6 cm of clearance each, against 1.8–3.8 cm for the remaining four, the Slack Dt-HOCBF included. From 𝑁 𝑏 = 100 and up, every formulation converges to 1.6–1.7 cm .At that density, robots are pressed against obstacles by their neighbors rather than by their own control choices, so the safety formulation can no longer set the obstacle clearance. This is the margin-side counterpart of the collision result that obstacles stop mattering once inter-robot congestion dominates (Section 3.5). 

Computational cost The LA Slack Dt-CBF costs slightly more to solve because of its added look-ahead constraints. Its per-robot solve time is about 3.7 ms at 𝑁 𝑏 = 25 , roughly 1.0 ms above the cheapest formulations (Fig. 3c). Each robot optimizes against a fixed number of neighbors, so this per-robot cost barely changes with swarm size and stays within 3.66 –3.74 ms across the sweep. It is therefore well inside the 100 ms budget of a 10 Hz loop at every density. Section 3.6 reports the distribution and separates this per-robot cost from the per-step wall time of the single-process simulation, which does grow with 𝑁 𝑏 . The extra cost is small, so on phototaxis, the deployment target, the LA Slack Dt-CBF gives the best trade-off across feasibility, collisions, and safety margin. Therefore, we adopt it as the deployed formulation for the collective behavior simulations.

3.4.2. Phototactic Orbital Behavior 

In the phototactic orbital mode, each robot follows the tangent of the sensed intensity level set through the gradient-rotation reference (9), orbiting the source without global localization. The CBF constraints (26) prevent collisions as robots compete for the same orbit while the cohesion and alignment costs keep the swarm compact and co-rotating. From arbitrary initial positions, it converges to a stable orbital formation (Fig. 5c). Although the phototactic orbital mode is susceptible to crowding similar to the phototaxis mode, the decay of the local polar order is more muted in the former (Fig. 6b). This is a direct consequence of the co-location of local neighbors along concentric orbital paths, leading to better heading agreement, even for large swarms. Similarly, the NND also settles slightly below the safe buffer distance even for 𝑁 𝑏 = 137 , as the locally tangential motion along the orbital causes the robots to align along their major axes, allowing for sufficient separation among them (Fig. 6e). Both metrics are only mildly affected by the addition of obstacles to the system.

A shared gradient gives phototaxis and orbiting a common direction, so two robots that come close are already traveling the same way. Without a stimulus each robot follows its own random heading, and only the local coupling keeps neighbors together. In large swarms the same absence of a shared target protects it, since the robots never concentrate. At every larger swarm size orbiting stays safest and phototaxis worst, except in the cluttered arena where the stimulus-free mode moves ahead of orbiting at 𝑁 𝑏 = 50 and 𝑁 𝑏 = 75 . Obstacles raise contacts in every mode, but the orbital mode pays the largest penalty, 14 .5× at 𝑁 𝑏 = 100 against 1.6× for phototaxis. Obstacles sit directly on the orbit and force detours. The safety formulation decides how large a swarm can grow before contacts appear. The task decides how many contacts it has at any given size.

3.6. Computational Performance and Scalability 

The experiments run one task per core on shared compute nodes, so other jobs on the same node could slow down the solve time. We repeated the measurement on an idle node reserved for this run alone. Averaged over the density sweep, each formulation’s mean agrees with its shared-node value to within 0.07 ms , so sharing a node does not distort the per-robot times reported in Section 3.3, and the idle run also gives the full distribution. Each solve covers the full per-robot control step, including both consensus iterations. The mean and the 99 th percentile stay well inside both the 100 ms (10 Hz ) and 33 .3 ms (30 Hz ) budgets at every density. Across the six formulations 𝑝 99 peaks at 13 .1 ms , and at the operating densities 𝑁 𝑏 ≥ 100 it stays at or below 

3.9 ms . Worst-case single solves are larger and matter for a hard real-time guarantee. The maximum observed on the idle node is 98 .8 ms , so the 10 Hz budget is met at every density while the 30 Hz budget is not. These maxima are lone draws from the tail. They grow with 𝑁 𝑏 only because a larger swarm performs more solves per run, from 3000 

at 𝑁 𝑏 = 10 to 41100 at 𝑁 𝑏 = 137 , while the mean and 𝑝 99 do not grow. A 30 Hz deployment would therefore cap the solver iterations and fall back to the reactive controller on the rare overrun. The per-step wall time necessarily grows with 𝑁 𝑏 , since a single process advances all robots in sequence. It is at least 𝑁 𝑏 times the per-robot cost, so ≳ 37 , 93 , 184 and 374 ms at 𝑁 𝑏 = 10 , 25 , 50 , 100 . This is a simulation artifact. Each robot runs its own optimization onboard or in parallel when deployed, so the governing cost is the per-robot time. That cost is flat in 𝑁 𝑏 (Section 3.3), so computation does not limit the swarm size. The current single-process simulation bottleneck can be removed through parallel execution and is left as future implementation work. 

## 4. Conclusion 

We presented a distributed predictive flocking controller for dense miniature ellipsoid robot swarms in a confined arena. Each robot solves a local NMPC combining task tracking, Reynolds-style flocking, and inter-robot and obstacle safety constraints, using only exchanged predicted trajectories of active neighbors, without a leader, central planner, or global map. The results demonstrate that the same controller architecture generalizes across different modal objectives such as gradient-ascending phototaxis, stable phototactic orbital formation, and even flocking in the absence of any stimulus. The emergent collective behavior arises purely from local optimization of cohesion and alignment costs, coupled with safety constraints that maintain separation. This separability between the task and the coordination points towards a modeling paradigm where new tasks can plausibly be added without necessarily re-deriving the safety features or coordination structure. Each robot optimizes against a fixed neighbor set, so solve time is (1) in swarm size, measured at 3.7 ms on average and flat across the density sweep, making the scaling limit architectural (a serial solve with a single workstation caps closed-loop control at roughly 27 robots) rather than algorithmic. Comparing six safety formulations under identical conditions showed that performance is determined by structure rather than barrier order; pairing a slacked barrier anchor with a rate condition reduces contact counts by a factor of 

2.4 relative to formulations without an anchor, with no overlap across the density sweep. Considering the phototaxis mode as the deployment target, the LA Slack Dt-CBF is found to be most effective in preventing collisions, while also providing the highest inter-robot margins for most swarm sizes tested. The look-ahead handle drives this improvement, giving the turn rate direct authority over the constraint so a robot can steer around a neighbor rather than only brake. Removing this handle alone raises contacts by up to a factor of 2.5 at low density, and by 1.2 at the geometric packing limit. Moreover, a higher-order rate chain improves performance further at moderate density but loses its advantage at the packing limit, showing that a higher barrier order does not automatically guarantee better safety. The collective motion is analyzed through local polar order and NND analysis, which, apart from quantifying controller performance, also point toward a biomimetic reading of these results. The muted order decay and more efficient packing found for the orbital mode, versus the sharper degradation under phototaxis, mirror a structural distinction well documented in natural swarms: neighbors on a shared circulating path align through a shear-like velocity field, while neighbors converging on a common point align through a convergent one, with the latter being inherently more collision-prone and alignment-averse as group size grows. That our controller reproduces these qualitative results from local optimization alone, without it being an explicit design target, suggests the polar order and NND metrics already computed here could serve as a bridge to the natural flocking literature. Furthermore, the wall-following behavior observed in the absence of external stimuli concurs well with studies of natural fish schools and insect swarms in confined spaces, a systematic evaluation and comparison of which can open a path to tuning the flocking and safety weights toward biologically observed regimes rather than controller-internal ones.

The current work has two limitations. The vibration and friction induced locomotion of the real bristlebot is modeled only kinematically rather than physically, so variations involving surface, payload, and actuation asymmetry enter the controller only as unmodeled disturbance. The safety comparison is also internal by design: six formulations within one stack, which isolates the safety constraint but does not benchmark against external distributed-flocking baselines, most of which target fully actuated 3D quadrotors or underactuated 2D robots rather than vibration-driven platforms. Further extensions can be carried out in terms of optimization and learning; the slack weight, rate-condition horizon, and look-ahead distance form a compact design space suited to automated tuning against the contact-rate and feasibility metrics already collected here, while a learned residual dynamics model could absorb the friction disturbance currently left unmodeled, narrowing the gap between the kinematic model and the true vibration-driven actuation. Immediate next steps include physical validation on the GRASPion hardware, extension to dynamic obstacle avoidance, and robust or stochastic MPC formulations for stronger safety guarantees. Overall, these results establish a single, kinematic model-agnostic NMPC architecture that unifies task-driven behavior, emergent flocking, and safety against collisions, while the polar-order and NND signatures it reproduces open a concrete path toward grounding swarm-robotics control in the structure of natural collective motion.
