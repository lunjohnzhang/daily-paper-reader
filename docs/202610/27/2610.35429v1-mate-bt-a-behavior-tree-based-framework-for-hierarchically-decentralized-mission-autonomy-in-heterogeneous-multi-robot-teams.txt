# MATE-BT: A Behavior-Tree-Based Framework for Hierarchically Decentralized Mission Autonomy in Heterogeneous Multi-Robot Teams 

Sunbeen Lee 1, Hoseon Gi 1, Jik Hur 2, Inmo Jang 1∗

Abstract — Multi-robot teams executing predefined missions must adapt to runtime changes and sustain mission man-agement despite robot failures. This paper proposes MATE-BT (Mission, Allocation, and Task Execution with Behavior Trees), a hierarchically decentralized framework that separates mission execution, allocation, and task execution. An active onboard leader manages mission progression and team-level task assignment, with authority transferable to another leader-capable robot after failure. Interchangeable decentralized meth-ods resolve target assignments, while each robot adapts its local behavior through planning and acting with behavior trees. Webots simulations and real-robot experiments demonstrate mission continuity despite new targets and robot failures, including active-leader failure. In the physical experiments, all MATE-BT computation runs onboard. Hardware-in-the-loop resource measurements further support onboard execution on Jetson Orin Nano and Raspberry Pi 5. By updating only the affected layers without full-plan regeneration, MATE-BT maintained a response time of approximately 130 ms with 30 robots and 120 targets. Comparisons of four target-assignment plugins reveal scenario-dependent trade-offs between mission completion time and total travel distance. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Heterogeneous teams combine platforms with different capabilities and responsibilities for mission management and task execution [1]. In crewed–uncrewed operations, for ex-ample, onboard automation can support mission management on a crewed platform while uncrewed robots autonomously execute delegated tasks [2]. Such teams must sustain pre-defined missions despite environmental changes, platform failures, and disrupted communication with external mission management. This calls for decentralized mission autonomy based on onboard computation and inter-robot communica-tion [3]. Runtime disturbances can require responses at different functional levels [4], [5]. At the task-execution level, a capable UGV may suppress a newly blocking fire before con-tinuing toward its assigned victim, changing its local action sequence while retaining the assignment. At the allocation level, a growing fire may require additional robots, or a robot failure may require unfinished work to be reassigned. At the mission-execution level, the team must retain the ability to manage mission progression despite robot failures. These

Prior work has addressed different aspects of these func-tional requirements. For example, planning and acting with behavior trees (BTs), denoted PA-BT in this paper, sup-ports reactive robot-local behavior adaptation [6], while decentralized multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) methods coordinate assignments through local computation and com-munication [7]–[9]. Hierarchical multi-robot BTs provide a basis for organizing mission progression, allocation, and local execution [10]. We propose MATE-BT (Mission, Allocation, and Task Execution with Behavior Trees) , which connects these mechanisms in a layered onboard execution framework. MATE-BT adopts a hierarchical form of decentralization: an active onboard leader manages mission progression and team-level task assignment, while target assignment and task execution are decentralized across robots; if the active leader fails, another leader-capable robot assumes mission-management authority. 

A. Related Work 

This section reviews mission execution architectures for multi-robot autonomy, focusing on how mission manage-ment, task allocation, and robot-level execution are orga-nized. The architectures discussed below use BTs for ex-ecution, benefiting from reactive behavior through condition reevaluation and fallbacks, as well as modular composition and subtree reuse [11], [12]. The discussion here is organized by the responsibilities assigned to robots: (1) executing dispatched actions only; (2) managing local task execution under centralized coordination; and (3) participating in de-centralized coordination decisions. 

1) Action execution from symbolic plans: In monolithic architectures, mission management, task allocation, and task execution are encoded in a centrally generated symbolic plan, leaving each robot to execute only the actions dispatched to it. PlanSys2 [13], a ROS 2 successor inspired by ROS-Plan [14], compiles a Planning Domain Definition Language 

(PDDL) plan into a BT and dispatches its actions to robots. When a change invalidates the plan, execution requires a plan-level update and regeneration. The work in [15] ex-tends this structure by partitioning execution among team-specific BT executors and selecting local replanning, robot reallocation, or full replanning according to failure impact. Despite their different recovery scopes, both approaches rely on centrally generated symbolic plans that prescribe robot arXiv:2610.35429v1 [cs.RO] 26 Oct 2026 actions. 

2) Robot-local task execution: These architectures assign complete tasks, rather than individual actions, and delegate their execution to robot-local BTs. The HTN-based meth-ods in [16], [17] derive agent-specific task plans through centralized planning or plan decomposition. The framework in [18] centrally optimizes assignments and schedules, and that in [19] has a central unit select assignments from bids the robots estimate locally. Despite these differences, authority over mission progression and allocation remains central, and each robot adapts only within the task it has been assigned. In single robotic systems, [20], [21] manage mission phases or operating modes using finite-state machines (FSMs) above local BT execution, but do not address de-centralized coordination among independent robots. 

3) Distributed coordination among robots: These archi-tectures move at least part of the coordination authority to the robots, allowing them to participate in task allocation or delegation through local decision-making and inter-robot communication. An early BT architecture in [10] replicates global-task, task-assignment, and local-execution trees across robots, allowing each robot to compute mission progression and team-wide assignments. However, it does not specify how conflicting decisions are reconciled when local infor-mation differs, a broader challenge in decentralization [22], and evaluates fault tolerance analytically without simulated or physical robot experiments. [23] retains a central mission controller but allows robots to auction prerequisite actions and extend their local BTs on-line. The work in [24] distributes designated subtree execu-tion through utility-based delegation, while [25] extends this idea with decentralized auctions for capability implementa-tions and executing robots. These approaches distribute task allocation or delegation, but either retain centralized mission management or do not specify how mission management continues after the loss of its executing component. The studies reviewed in this subsection evaluate their proposed coordination mechanisms through analysis or simu-lation without reporting physical multi-robot experiments. To our knowledge, no existing physical demonstration integrates onboard mission management, decentralized allocation, and robot-local execution while evaluating mission continuity after the failure of either a task-executing robot or the active mission manager. 

B. Contributions 1) Layered execution with hierarchical decentralization: 

MATE-BT separates mission execution, task allocation, and task execution so that a disturbance can be handled at the layer whose decision it affects. The mission-management and team-level task-assignment functions are replicated across onboard leader-capable robots, allowing authority to transfer after active-leader failure while retaining the mission policy. Target assignments are resolved through decentralized MRTA using local computation and inter-robot communication, while each robot adapts its behavior locally through PA-BT. 

2) Interchangeable decentralized target assignment: Acommon plugin interface allows several established decen-tralized MRTA methods [7]–[9] to be interchanged without changing either the mission-management or task-execution structure. Controlled comparisons within each scenario show that plugin choice affects mission performance, with no method consistently preferred across the tested scenarios. 

3) System-level evaluation of responsiveness and mission continuity: A comparison with a monolithic baseline shows that, by updating only the affected assignments instead of regenerating the full mission plan, MATE-BT handles newly added targets and limits response-time growth as mission scale increases. Webots simulations and four-robot physical experiments demonstrate mission continuity after either a task-executing robot or the active leader fails. In the physical experiments, all MATE-BT computation runs onboard, while central infrastructure supplies only the communication layer and detected-target information. Resource measurements on Jetson Orin Nano and Raspberry Pi 5, spanning real-robot and hardware-in-the-loop tests, quantify the CPU and mem-ory overhead of onboard BT execution. II. P ROPOSED FRAMEWORK 

A. Problem Setting 

MATE-BT assumes a mission policy that is predefined yet abstract and structured as an ordered sequence of phases. Each phase specifies the tasks to be performed and the con-dition for phase completion. The policy may also designate a preferred robot type for a task. The mission policy is assumed to remain valid throughout execution. As an example of such a policy, we use a fire-suppression-and-rescue mission throughout this paper. First, in the Rescue phase , UAVs search for victims, and Rescue UGVs transport detected victims to the base. Fire UGVs escort the Rescue UGVs and clear fires obstructing the rescue routes. They can also perform rescue when necessary but have a lower carrying capacity than Rescue UGVs. This phase continues as new victims are detected and ends once all victims have been rescued. Then, in the Suppress phase , the Fire UGVs suppress the remaining fires until no active fires remain. Finally, in the Return phase , the surviving robots return to base, and the mission terminates once all have arrived. We distinguish between a task and a target to describe the allocation decisions involved in a multi-robot mission. A task denotes a type of work, such as search, rescue, escort, suppression, or return, whereas a target denotes the object or location to which that work applies, such as a victim, a fire, an escort position, or the base. For a target-directed task, execution begins once both the task and its corresponding target have been assigned. Each robot executes at most one task at a time, whereas a task request k may be assigned to multiple robots and may specify an upper bound nk on their number. During normal operation, we assume a connected, but not necessarily fully connected, inter-robot communication network. Messages can be delivered over multiple hops when needed, although delays may cause temporary differences in robots’ local information. Fig. 1. Overall architecture of MATE-BT. B. Overall Architecture 

MATE-BT separates mission execution, allocation, and task execution into three functional layers. Its implemen-tation comprises two BT structures, a team-level Swarm BT and a robot-level Agent BT , together with robot-specific action primitives (Fig. 1). The Swarm BT manages mission progression, while the Agent BT manages robot-local task execution through these primitives. Allocation spans the two BTs: the Swarm BT assigns tasks to robots, while an MRTA plugin in the Agent BT assigns targets for those tasks. Every robot runs an instance of the Agent BT onboard. Leader-capable robots also run identical instances of the Swarm BT, allowing any of them to take over mission man-agement; however, only the active leader ticks the mission-management subtree. We implement BT executors that run the Swarm BT and Agent BT asynchronously in separate ROS 2 nodes. Within each BT, nodes share information through a local blackboard. Our ROS-aware leaf-node interfaces connect the BTs to ROS: condition nodes evaluate robot and world states received through topics, whereas action nodes invoke action primitives through ROS actions, services, or topic publications. Task requests, execution feedback, and MRTA messages are exchanged among robots over ROS topics. Each robot periodically publishes an alive signal con-taining its ID, robot type, and leader priority. Swarm BT and Agent BT instances independently use these signals to maintain a local view of team membership for mission management and target assignment, respectively. A robot is excluded from this view if no signal is received for a configured timeout τ .

C. Mission Execution 

The Swarm BT uses a ReactiveSequence containing 

GatherMissionInfo , AmILeader , and a Parallel 

subtree comprising the Mission BT, AssignTask , and 

BroadcastTask (Fig. 1). GatherMissionInfo first updates the local blackboard with world information, team information, and task-execution feedback. 

1) Mission Policy Encoding and Progression: The Mis-sion BT encodes the mission policy as a Sequence of phases, each represented by a ReactiveFallback of its completion condition and a Parallel of leaf nodes that issue task requests. Each of these nodes records a required task and its parameters on the blackboard rather than invoking robot actions. Each leaf returns S UCCESS 

once all robots assigned to its task have reported S UCCESS ,and R UNNING otherwise. The phase-completion condition is reevaluated from world information whenever the phase is ticked. A phase returns SUCCESS if and only if its completion condition holds or the configured number of task-request nodes have returned S UCCESS . The Sequence then advances to the next phase. 

2) Mission-Management Authority and Handover: 

Leader-capable robots are ranked by configured priority, with ties broken by robot ID. AmILeader returns S UCCESS 

if and only if its robot is the highest-ranked candidate in its local membership view. Robots with identical views therefore select the same leader. When the active leader times out, the next-ranked candidate assumes mission-management authority. 

Remark 1 (Leader Convergence and Phase Recovery). Acommunication partition may split the team into groups with different leaders. After reconnection, the robots select a common leader once alive-signal exchange brings their membership views into agreement. When a robot becomes the leader or its membership view expands, it resets its Mission BT Sequence and reevaluates it from the first phase using current world information. All phases in the evaluated mission define completion using such observable information. 

D. Allocation 1) Task Assignment: Task assignment determines which current-phase task each robot performs, while leaving target selection to the subsequent target-assignment stage. MATE-BT resolves task assignment in two ways: ScopeOf ap-plies robot-type designations encoded in the mission policy, whereas AssignTask assigns the remaining robots to un-scoped tasks at runtime. A ScopeOf decorator addresses a task request exclu-sively to its designated robot type while that type remains available, and robots of that type are assigned only to the scoped task within the phase. If no robot of the designated type remains, the task request becomes unscoped and is handled by AssignTask .At each tick, after scoped requests are resolved,

BroadcastTask publishes the current task requests at every tick, and each robot’s ReceiveTask stores its assigned task and parameters in the local blackboard. 

2) Target Assignment: Target assignment selects a target, such as a victim, fire, or destination, for each robot’s assigned task. To support interchangeable MRTA methods for this step, we define a standardized plugin interface following the common two-phase structure of decentralized MRTA: local decision-making and inter-robot conflict resolution [22]. At every tick, AssignTarget in each Agent BT passes the available targets, the robot’s state, and received messages to the configured plugin through this interface, then stores the selected target in the local blackboard. The current imple-mentation provides plugins for CBAA , a greedy consensus-based auction, and its bundle extension CBBA [8], distributed Hungarian [7], which converges to an optimal one-to-one assignment through local communication, and GRAPE [9], which forms coalitions through an anonymous hedonic game. Collectively, these plugins cover the dominant MRTA problem classes involving instantaneous or time-extended assignment of single-robot-required tasks and instantaneous assignment of multi-robot-required tasks [26]. 

E. Task Execution Task BT serves as a placeholder subtree in the Agent BT. Using the task request and assigned target stored in the local blackboard, the Agent BT executor maps the current assignment to a desired postcondition and installs it as the child of Task BT whenever either changes. The 

Postcondition–Precondition–Action (PPA) library associates postconditions with the actions that achieve them and their required preconditions. If the desired postcondition is unsat-isfied, the executor applies PA-BT backchaining [6] over this library to recursively add the required actions for the post-condition and any unmet preconditions. Task BT executes the resulting subtree while its condition nodes reevaluate the latest blackboard state. Task completion is determined by the observed postcondition rather than by an action result or service response, and the resulting status is reported as task feedback. 

Remark 2 (Application-Specific Components). To instanti-ate MATE-BT, the application designer provides the com-ponents shown with dashed borders in Fig. 1: (i) a Mission BT encoding the mission policy, (ii) utility or cost models for task and target assignment and, if needed, a new target-assignment method implementing the standardized plugin interface, (iii) a PPA library for expanding the Task BT, and (iv) robot-specific action primitives and their ROS interfaces. This paper presented MATE-BT, a behavior-tree-based hi-erarchically decentralized framework for mission autonomy in heterogeneous multi-robot teams. It combines transferable onboard mission management, interchangeable decentralized MRTA plugins, and reactive robot-local execution while retaining a predefined mission policy. Webots simulations and real-robot experiments demonstrated mission continuity despite new targets and robot failures, including active-leader failure. All MATE-BT computation ran onboard in the real-robot experiments. Hardware-in-the-loop resource measurements further supported onboard execution on Jetson Orin Nano and Raspberry Pi 5. Compared with the mono-lithic baseline, which regenerates its full plan, MATE-BT maintained response times near the tick period with up to 30 robots and 120 targets. Applying MATE-BT to a new mission currently requires manually specifying the Mission BT and the PPA library. Future work could automate the generation and verification of these components using mission requirements and robot capabilities. The observed variation in plugin performance motivates automatic MRTA plugin selection or a single method that adapts to different mission conditions.
