# PushAround: Collaborative Path Clearing via Physics-Informed Hybrid Search 

Zili Tang 1, Zongyuan Li 2, Zishao Qiao 1, and Meng Guo 1

Abstract —Large vehicles in cluttered environments are often blocked by movable obstacles, motivating mobile robot teams to clear traversable corridors. Navigation Among Movable Ob-stacles (NAMO) approaches often abstract obstacle displace-ments while neglecting robot dimensions, obstacle mass, con-tact geometry and forces, yielding physically infeasible plans. 

PushAround is a physics-informed hybrid search that couples geometric connectivity reasoning with short-horizon simulation to construct executable clearing plans. Repeated W–clearance connectivity graph (WCCG) queries expose frontier gaps requir-ing widening and rank them by estimated downstream costs. The search generates direct or recursive pushing candidates, selecting obstacles, directions, contacts and forces, and admits physics-evaluated candidates. Numerical experiments validate WCCG against standard connectivity checks and demonstrate scaling to denser clutter and teams of up to four robots, with robustness to simulated execution-side physical-parameter mismatch. 

Index Terms —Multi-Robot Systems, Task and Motion Plan-ning, Manipulation Planning. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# IN warehouses, disaster sites and urban spaces, movable ob-stacles such as pallets, boxes and equipment can block large vehicles, motivating mobile robot teams to clear traversable corridors. Conventional path planners assume static obstacles and fail once routes are blocked [1]. Navigation Among Mov-able Obstacles (NAMO) creates routes by pushing, pulling or rotating obstacles [2], yet many methods abstract away robot dimensions, obstacle size and mass, contact geometry, forces and dynamics, producing geometrically valid but physically unrealizable plans . As shown in Fig. 1, a large robot may be unable to reach obstacle boundaries while a small robot lacks pushing force. Cooperative pushing by several small robots offers an alternative, but simultaneous pushes can trigger chained motions of several obstacles, introducing physical coupling that complicates planning.

III. P ROPOSED SOLUTION 

As shown in Figs. 2 and 3, PushAround first uses the W-Clearance Connectivity Graph in Sec. III-A and gap ranking in Sec. III-B to identify and prioritize blocking gaps. It then generates and evaluates physically feasible collaborative pushing modes in Sec. III-C. These components are integrated into the simulation-in-the-loop hybrid search of Sec. A. W–Clearance Connectivity Graph 

The feasibility of routing the vehicle reduces to whether a W -width corridor connects xSV and xGV. A W–clearance con-nectivity graph (WCCG) encodes obstacle adjacencies under clearance W for fast queries, as shown in Fig. 3. Grid- and sampling-based clearance checks typically depend on a chosen grid or sampling resolution and must update their queries when obstacles move [20]. Built from obstacle geometry, the WCCG needs no grid-resolution parameter in these repeated queries. 

1) Graph Construction: The WCCG is built by decompos-ing all clearance-relevant obstacle and workspace boundaries into convex components or segments. From each compo-nent C, a centroid node vc is created. When two compo-nents Cu and Cv have closest points pu P Cu and pv P Cv with distance less than W , bridge nodes are added at pu and pv .These bridge nodes are connected by a bridge–bridge edge, annotated with the gap width wuv fi }pu ´ pv }, and further linked back to their centroids with centroid–bridge edges. Narrow passages with wuv ă W are marked as potential bottlenecks. The resulting graph is given by: 

GW fi pV, Ec Y Ebq, (7) where V contains centroid and bridge nodes; Ec contains centroid–bridge edges; and Eb contains bridge–bridge edges annotated by gap width. 

2) Connectivity Criterion: Once GW has been constructed, connectivity queries can be performed without explicitly com-puting a geometric path. Let Face pGW q denote the set of connected planar faces induced by the embedded WCCG, i.e., the maximal regions separated by obstacle components and WCCG bottleneck edges. The consistency of WCCG connectivity with both continuous and rasterized configuration-space references is further evaluated numerically in Sec. IV-A3. Starting from xSV, it casts a ray toward xGV and follows the encountered frontier loop until either a valid exit is found or a blocking cycle is returned.

A query takes 1.07 ms, comprising 0.67 ms for querying and 

0.40 ms for construction, versus 1.36 ms for CC and 243 ms for FR. It also supplies the gap graph for PIHS. Per-push WCCG rebuilding is negligible next to physics rollouts.

This work presents PushAround, a multi-robot framework that jointly plans obstacle selection, contacts and forces through a hybrid search. Geometric gap reasoning identifies where the corridor must widen, while pushing evaluation assesses the corresponding rearrangements under physical constraints. Simulation studies examine obstacle density, team size, and execution-side physics mismatch; hardware experi-ments demonstrate path clearing with state-feedback replan-ning.
