Short title: Safe navigation in congested spaces 

Safe navigation in congested environments requires both collision avoidance and coordinated passage. We introduce neuromorphic decision dynamics (NDD), a decentralized, event-based controller, that provides navigation in congested spaces with guarantees on safety, liveness, robustness, and scalability. NDD makes safe-and-live deconfliction a controllable, locally tunable emergent property of lo-cal agent interactions, without requiring communication, preassigned priorities, models of neighbors’ objectives or future actions, or estimates of group size or global network properties. By design, NDD couples the dynamics of internal de-cision and attention states with changing physical states and locally observed relative motion. Each agent continuously modulates its speed along its prescribed path, responding as soon and as much as conflict resolution requires. Agents self-organize into disjoint, locally engaged groups that resolve conflicts concur-rently. When any group’s conflict becomes safety-critical, decision-action feedback destabilizes collective indecision, and coordinated conflict-resolution emerges that stabilizes a safe traversal order within the group. Order persists as conflicts clear, but groups may split or merge as agents autonomously adapt to new conflicts and those with resolved conflicts resume their nominal motion. We derive explicit conditions on sensing range, feedback gains, response time scales, and actuation limits that guarantee collision avoidance and traversal-order formation in finite time with no deadlock or livelock for finite, heterogeneous agent populations of arbitrary, changing size. Computational cost scales with the number of local inter-actions sensed by each agent. Large-scale simulations, comparisons with existing approaches, and hardware experiments demonstrate NDD in complex congested scenarios and validate its scalability. One-sentence summary: Neuromorphic decision dynamics enable robust safe-and-live multi-robot navigation with tunable emergent collective behavior.

Autonomous systems are increasingly operating in environments populated by other intelligent agents where, as a single agent decides and acts, it simultaneously shapes the choices available to others. For moving agents navigating shared physical spaces, any one agent’s movement decisions may influence and be influenced by the movement decisions of other nearby agents. Moreover, bottlenecks will create local competition over limited available space, such as when movers (pedes-trians, vehicles, robots) heading in different directions, and without a common protocol, seek to safely pass by one another through narrow passages, intersections, and busy, congested spaces. Resolving the competition is challenging because it requires coordination, not only to ensure safe (collision-free) passage, but also to avoid deadlocks and livelocks. Deadlocks can manifest as mutual yielding ( 1) or motion freezing when an agent deems every forward motion unsafe ( 2). Livelock occurs when there is repeated motion adjustment without progress ( 3). When agents fail to form a traversal order through the contested region, they can land in a deadlock or livelock. Even immediate locally feasible and safe actions can eventually lead to deadlock or livelock ( 4). 

Safe-and-live deconfliction refers to collective behavior that preserves both safety and liveness in contested environments. To the best of our knowledge, a general decentralized design of safe-and-live deconfliction that jointly guarantees safety and liveness without preassigned traversal priorities remains challenging, even when agents can communicate. In decentralized settings, coordination requires each agent to account for interaction structure including: (i) the set of others with which the agent is in conflict, (ii) how much each conflict matters, and (iii) which agent involved in the conflict should pass through the conflict region first. In this paper, we address decentralized safe-and-live deconfliction with guarantees by designing neuromorphic decision dynamics that leverage interaction structure on-the-fly. Our solution addresses the harder problem in which agents have no communication, only partial observations of their neighbors’ actions, limited actuation, and the number of agents is finite but otherwise arbitrary and time-varying. Existing coordination methods ( 2, 5–8 ) often seek to simplify the problem by separating the assessment of interactions from movement decisions: an agent first predicts the intended behavior of its neighbors and then selects an action conditioned on those predictions. This reduces the interactive multi-agent problem to a single-agent optimal control problem. However, the approach rests on the strong assumption that the interaction structure needed for coordination can be determined 

before an action is selected and remains valid while the agents react to one another. Congested environments violate this assumption because the same agent can be involved in multiple conflicts simultaneously, which makes the interaction structure an outcome of the movement decisions it is meant to inform. For example, an agent cannot predict a neighbor’s response from their shared interaction alone because that response may depend on the agent’s own action, which has yet to be selected. As a result, the traversal orders agents arrive at need not agree, leading to mutual yielding or repeated readjustment. Moreover, if an agent predicts conservatively and plans against the worst admissible behavior of every neighbor, it may find no forward motion safe and freeze. Near-symmetric encounters introduce additional ambiguity because several choices remain equally likely based on the available observations. Anticipating the resulting configurations and planning for an appropriate response become combinatorially intractable ( 9, 10 ). The behavior of human crowds shows that coordination can emerge from fast, differentiated responses to local motion cues (11, 12 ). In busy spaces, people usually keep moving along their preferred routes while making small speed adjustments: they slow slightly to let another person 2pass, accelerate into an opening, or time their motion through a merge or unsignalized intersection. They rarely need to stop completely or make a discrete change of direction. Instead, continuous speed modulations create gaps, resolve local conflicts, and allow traversal order to emerge ( 11, 12 ). Inspired by these studies, we define coordination cues to be local motion cues, such as speeding up, slowing down, and small differences in projected arrival times to contested regions. We are thus motivated by the following scientific question: Can a fully decentralized feedback controller utilize transient, endogenous coordination cues to generate provably-robust safe-and-live deconfliction in interacting groups of arbitrary and changeable size? Our thesis is that to successfully exploit coordination cues that emerge from interaction, safe-and-live deconfliction must be designed to emerge from the closed loop between the decisions and actions of agents and the transient cues they generate. Deadlocks and livelocks should not be treated as configurations that agents must detect and repair. Instead, the controller should, by construction, make deadlocks and livelocks unstable states of evolving interactions, so they are autonomously destabilized by agents’ movements. Recent work on collision-avoidance control policies based on control barrier functions (CBF) also identified unstable equilibria as the key mechanism behind deadlock-free behavior in multi-agent navigation ( 13, 14 ). However, the analysis is descriptive rather than constructive, limited to small groups, and certified only with centralized information or by co-optimizing agents’ actions. Under purely local, model-free control policies, the deadlock equilibria remain stable, so the mechanism does not carry over to the decentralized setting considered here. According to ( 14 ) it is an open problem “how to design a method that creates unstable equilibria and controls how agents depart from unresolved configurations.” We address the open problem by introducing Neuromorphic Decision Dynamics (NDD), a dynamical system in continuous time for decision formation that synthesizes controlled instabil-ity by coupling decisions and actions across the interacting group. NDD constructs and certifies 

safe-and-live deconfliction as a controllable emergent property of local interactions. Its activation– persistence–recovery cycle mirrors the response of an excitable neural dynamical system, mo-tivating the term neuromorphic decision dynamics . Each agent makes decisions locally using anonymous, egocentric measurements of nearby agents’ relative positions and velocities. Whereas communication- and schedule-based approaches coordinate through shared information ( 15–18 ), and prediction- and game-theoretic approaches model neighbors’ behavior ( 5,19–21 ), NDD couples agents through their observed motion. It requires no communication, common clock, synchronized updates, global information, or models of neighboring agents’ control objectives or future actions. NDD can interface with different robotic platforms and low-level tracking controllers that satisfy the stated actuation and tracking conditions. NDD preserves each agent’s preferred path, continuously modulating only its traversal speed as conflict evidence arises and decays. For an interacting group of arbitrary and time-varying size, we show analytically that NDD induces a traversal order and sufficient temporal spacing by means of a controlled group-level instability. Locally tunable control parameters enable the emergence of group-level positive feed-back through which agents’ speed changes alter the conflict evidence driving their decisions. As conflicts become safety-critical, this positive feedback overcomes decision damping, destabilizing the collective-indecision equilibrium in which the group has not selected a traversal order. Infinites-imally small endogenous asymmetries in local motion cues can then select a stable differentiated 

motion pattern that establishes this order (Fig. 1, A to F). Decision values rapidly depart from a resting state near zero and remain differentiated as long as the conflict persists. The resulting motion 3pattern creates temporal separation by accelerating earlier-arriving agents, slowing later-arriving agents, or coordinating both responses. As this separation grows, the evidence of conflict weakens, and the decision values return to rest, restoring nominal motion. This allows agents to adapt con-tinuously as new local evidence arises in rapidly changing environments. The response requires no discrete proceed-or-yield decisions or rule-based switching logic. We derive explicit conditions on sensing range, decision and control gains, and actuation limits that ensure the controller is tuned to exploit emerging coordination cues before they decay and is robust under bounded sensing noise, uncertainty in predicted relative motion, and actuation error. We validate deployability of our framework in hardware experiments and demonstrate scalability in large-scale simulations of complex multi-agent scenarios.

Collective recurrent-network interpretation The closed loop can therefore be viewed as an embodied continuous-time recurrent network. Each agent is a dynamical node with state (𝑧 𝑖 , 𝑢 𝑖 , 𝑣 𝑖 ),and sensed interactions form directed edges whose strengths and connectivity evolve with agent motion. The leaky decision and attention dynamics and stable speed tracking retain and gradually attenuate the effects of past evidence and commands, giving the network a fading memory. The same instantaneous observation can therefore produce different responses depending on the internal state inherited from the interaction history.

## Finite-time guarantees for decentralized safe-and-live deconfliction 

The following theorem states the safety and finite-progress guarantees for populations in which every agent implements NDD. 

Admissible conflict episodes and scope We consider a cooperative population in which each agent follows its prescribed path and local control law, without inter-agent communication or access to neighbors’ internal states. A conflict episode—a maximal time interval with at least one active physical conflict — is admissible if its conflicts are physically resolvable by speed modulation and each agent senses a uniformly bounded number of neighbors at any time. Admissibility also requires: 1) crossing angles bounded away from zero, to exclude head-on encounters; 2) bounded tracking errors, actuation delays, and stopping distances; 3) a positive minimum traversal speed attainable by every leading mover; and 4) finitely many conflict events without Zeno switching (Assumption 1). Finally, traversal-order negotiation also requires a small symmetry-breaking cue—arising naturally from asynchronous updates, heterogeneous actuation or tracking, measurement noise, or geometric asymmetries—to generate the entry-time offsets that select a differentiated equilibrium branch.

Concurrency further enhances throughput: leading movers that share no physical conflict can clear independent conflicts simultaneously. 

## Simulation benchmarks 

We evaluated NDD against optimal reciprocal collision avoidance (ORCA) ( 28,29 ), LivePoint ( 30 ), and centralized and decentralized control barrier function quadratic programs (CBF-QP) ( 31, 32 )using homogeneous Dubins-car agents. All methods used the same 50 randomized start–goal scenarios for each 𝑁 ∈ { 4, 8, 12 , 24 , 32 }. Across all 250 NDD rollouts, every NDD agent reached 14 its goal without a safety violation, pairwise separation stayed above the safety threshold, and agents retained their prescribed paths (Fig. 6, A to C). LivePoint achieved the highest baseline safe-agent success, although not even close to NDD’s 100% success and with performance that declined with group size. ORCA and both CBF-QP variants had lower success and mean minimum separation below the safety threshold. Two highly congested 15-agent scenarios—a near-antipodal exchange creating a central bottleneck and intersecting groups of nearly parallel paths—highlight this contrast: NDD maintained collision-free passage along the prescribed paths, whereas all three displayed baselines incurred collisions in the contested region (Fig. 7, A to H). 

## Hardware Experiments 

We evaluated NDD using six TurtleBot3 differential-drive robots on prescribed straight routes in an indoor workspace (Fig. 8). A Vicon motion-capture system supplied positions and orientations through ROS; planar velocities were estimated from successive position measurements and low-pass filtered. Each robot’s controller ran in a separate ROS node with asynchronous pose updates and no inter-robot communication, modulating forward speed around the nominal 0 .2 m /s. Two antipodal repetitions with starting positions differing by only 0 .8–2 .5 cm per robot produced different traversal orders: robot 2 (orange) passed first in one trial and last in the other (Fig. 8, A to F). These contrasting outcomes illustrate that traversal order emerges from coupled motion without preassigned priorities. In NDD, small asymmetries in local motion cues bias decisions; the resulting speed changes alter neighbors’ cues and amplify differentiation across the group. Orthogonal and mixed-lane encounters further show adaptive yielding: robots adjust their speeds throughout passage, and their decisions relax toward zero as they complete their crossings (Fig. 8, G to L). 

# Discussion 

NDD establishes a constructive design principle for controlling desired emergent behavior in em-bodied multi-agent systems. No communication, centralized scheduling, or combinatorial traversal-order assignment is required. Each agent extracts task-relevant relational information from local observations, constructs continuous-time interaction evidence, acts on that evidence, and uses the resulting motion cues to update its decision. Weak evidence keeps the group near collective indeci-sion. For safe-and-live deconfliction, safety-critical conflict drives the group through a bifurcation that amplifies even infinitesimally small asymmetries in motion into rapid differentiation in deci-sions. The tightly coupled decision–action–perception dynamics then create the temporal separation required for collision avoidance and the strict traversal order required for progress, allowing safety and liveness to emerge from a single closed loop. Agents recover nominal motion as conflicts clear. We derive explicit conditions on sensing, actuation, and feedback design parameters that set the commitment threshold and guarantee finite-time, robust, safe, deadlock-free, livelock-free de-confliction under heterogeneous actuation, sensing noise, and time-varying interaction topologies. The Supplementary Text provides complete certification details. Applying the NDD design with a certified guarantee requires only one choice of controller design parameters that satisfy the five compatibility conditions listed in Supplementary Section S5.4. This choice is independent of the total population and realized sizes of engaged groups. 15 Related work 

Safe-and-live deconfliction methods broadly comprise reactive avoidance and safety filters, explicit liveness mechanisms, and game-, optimization-, or learning-based coordination. Separating safety from liveness risks rejecting progress-enabling motion or disrupting the cues that establish traversal order. Methods that address safety and liveness within a single formulation account for coupled interactions, but shift the burden to representing those interactions in the decision problem. They must model or learn how each action propagates across connected conflicts before selecting a response. As the conflict group grows or changes, real-time computation can become infeasible or intractable. The unresolved challenge is therefore to allow a mutually consistent traversal order to emerge and adapt as interactions unfold. 

Reactive Collision Avoidance and Deadlock Recovery Reactive collision-avoidance methods compute motion commands from geometry or relative velocity using velocity obstacles ( 33 ), reciprocal variants ( 3, 28, 29 ), and potential fields or navigation functions ( 34–36 ). Safety filters provide stronger guarantees by modifying nominal inputs that would violate safety constraints. Control barrier functions establish forward invariance of a safe set ( 31, 32, 37 ), while data-driven variants learn agent dynamics, barrier certificates, or distributed safety controllers from data ( 38– 41 ). These mechanisms do not generally determine which agent should pass first through shared space: agents may yield simultaneously or repeatedly revise their motion. Geometric symmetry can force reactive controllers into deadlock even with heterogeneous control gains ( 1). Deadlock-avoidance methods add recovery or preventive mechanisms to preserve liveness when reactive avoidance is insufficient. These include formation-level escape motions certified for two-and three-robot formations ( 42, 43 ), trajectory exchange and replanning ( 15 ), leader-guided recov-ery ( 44 ), deadlock-free liveness sets ( 45 ), point-cloud barrier functions and speed regulation ( 30 ), or model predictive control (MPC)-based recovery and preventive risk conditions ( 46, 47 ). These methods enforce liveness through explicit recovery maneuvers, planning rules, or constraints de-signed to exclude deadlock, rather than through the continuous formation of a traversal order during local interactions. Recovery can require communication ( 15,44 ) or departures from nominal paths ( 43 ), limiting applicability when communication is unavailable or paths must remain fixed. 

Prediction- and Planning-Based Coordination Planning methods encode passage alternatives through mixed-integer or rule-based trajectory branches ( 48,49 ), topological predictions ( 7), learned winding strategies ( 8), or opinion-guided planning and game reduction ( 50 ). Multirobot planners establish precedence through optimized planning sequences ( 51 ), priority graphs or search ( 16, 52, 53 ). When multiple conflicts become coupled in real time, local priorities selected independently from partial observations may not define a consistent group-wide traversal order. Nonlinear opinion dynamics enable rapid commitment from social cues ( 54 ) or dynamic-game values ( 55 ); spiking extensions permit fast switching as environmental information changes ( 56 ). Social-cue approaches increase attention as a human approaches without requiring a predictive model of human behavior, while game-derived opinions guide trajectory optimization. Hu et al. ( 55 )establish stability conditions for the game-induced opinion equilibria of two agents with two options each. These methods resolve two-option intent ambiguity, but do not establish group-wide safe traversal orders for arbitrary time-varying conflict networks. Interaction-aware prediction and games model reciprocal responses through cooperative crowd forecasts ( 2, 19 ), influence-aware 16 planning ( 5), coupled strategies ( 20, 21 ), social preferences ( 6), bounded rationality ( 57 ), or mixed strategies ( 58 ). They require models or data for agents’ dynamics, objectives, and responses, and their coupled decision problem grows with the interacting group. Trajectory optimization and MPC incorporate dynamics, collision constraints, and progress objectives ( 59–63 ), including centralized nonlinear MPC with collision- and deadlock-free motion guarantees for nonholonomic robots ( 64 ). Growing conflict coupling challenges tractability and feasibility. Pairwise collision constraints characterize unsafe states, whereas deadlock and livelock depend on sequences of closed-loop responses and require an explicit liveness construction. Learning-based methods encode interaction reasoning in contextual crowd models ( 65 ) or poli-cies mapping observations to actions ( 66–69 ). They capture patterns difficult to specify analytically, but depend on training coverage. Changing group composition and conflict geometry complicate safety and liveness certification. 

Urban Transportation and Connected Vehicle Systems Urban transportation research coordi-nates connected and automated vehicles at intersections and merges by solving for access order and timing. Approaches include space–time reservations ( 17,70 ), auctions ( 71 ), conflict-point optimiza-tion ( 72 ), and polling ( 73 ). Networked-control approaches compute vehicle trajectories after the crossing order or safety constraints are established. Decentralized optimal-control and distributed schemes coordinate crossing times and vehicle controls while enforcing rear-end and lateral safety constraints, including in experimental implementations ( 18, 74, 75 ). Recent mixed-traffic formula-tions jointly optimize traffic signals and connected automated vehicle trajectories using distributed optimization ( 76 ), or learn human-driver behavior online to generate robust, time-optimal merging trajectories under prediction uncertainty ( 77 ). These methods exploit structured lanes and shared conflict zones. Reservations and auctions rely on intersection managers and vehicle-to-infrastructure communication; distributed controllers still exchange states, schedules, or crossing orders ( 18 ). Dense, unstructured robot navigation may offer only local observations, without communication or a fixed coordinator. Traversal order must therefore form and adapt through local interaction. 

## Importance and future directions 

Our multitime-scale autonomy architecture embeds NDD’s fast, continuous-time decision dynamics as a lightweight interaction layer within a hierarchical autonomy stack (Fig. 2). NDD resolves crossings and merges through local speed modulation while preserving nominal steering along the planned path, including turns. The planning module replans paths when speed modulation alone is insufficient, as with static obstacles or direct head-on encounters. Future work could extend NDD to modulate nominal steering and handle static obstacles within the local interaction layer, potentially improving efficiency. Alternative interaction-evidence and decision-to-control maps could extend this architecture to other tasks. For example the decision state could regulate aerial-vehicle altitude or manipulator speed and task timing, or incorporate traffic conventions. An inference layer could add intent, semantic context, communication, and cooperation estimates. Although fixed local NDD parameters remain valid as groups form, split, or merge, adaptation to congestion could improve performance. Future work should establish safety and liveness conditions for adapting controller gains, attention parameters, and evidence weights through local observations or online learning. 17 NDD provides a general, resource-aware foundation for shaping and certifying collective behavior through local physical interactions. 

## Limitations 

Our guarantees assume that all agents cooperate and use NDD. Extending them to mixed crowds requires analysis of other decision policies; potentially noncooperative agents also require real-time cooperation inference. Nonlinear opinion dynamics ( 23 ) can support this inference and inform NDD responses. Developing the NOD–NDD coupling and establishing mixed-population guarantees remain future work.

## S1 Practical guide to implementing NDD for a given mobile robot 

NDD is an event-triggered dynamic controller designed to sit between a robot’s existing global path planner and its nominal motion controller. During nominal execution, the robot follows a planned straight or smoothly curved path in continuous time. When NDD predicts a conflict—evaluated in closed form using only local relative-motion observations—it autonomously modulates the robot’s traversal speed to resolve the encounter. If speed adaptation alone is insufficient (e.g., encountering a static obstacle blocking the path or a head-on conflict in a narrow corridor), NDD signals the architecture to trigger path replanning (Fig. 2). Below, we outline a parameter-tuning workflow to implement NDD on any mobile robot platform, enabling real-time safe-and-live deconfliction under strict compute budgets.

The following sections build up to prove finite-time safe-and-live deconfliction in Theorem 1 of the main text. Under the conditions stated in each result, local NDD feedback establishes a traversal order, produces the temporal separation needed to realize that order safely, and adjusts decisions and speeds continuously as new physical conflicts arise. We start with the following assumptions: 

Assumption 1 (Finite conflict activations and no Zeno behavior) A graph event is a conflict ac-tivation or physical clearance, as defined in Material and Methods Section. Every bounded time interval contains only finitely many graph events (no Zeno behavior). Each conflict episode con-tains finitely many conflict activations, including those involving agents entering the population and those that merge groups. 

S4 Assumption 1 holds naturally for a finite population of agents traversing straight paths with nondegenerate crossings. Each pair has at most one crossing encounter, and retaining an activated conflict until physical clearance prevents repeated switching during that encounter. Consequently, the number of graph events is finite. The same reasoning extends to piecewise-straight paths with finitely many segments and to curved paths with finitely many crossing encounters. Episodes involving agent arrivals are also covered when finitely many agents participate in each episode. The term “final” is relative to the current episode; an agent’s final conflict is the last of its physical conflicts to clear in that episode. 

Assumption 2 (Reciprocal sensing) For each conflicting pair considered in the analysis, both agents can sense one another.

Assumption 3 (Stable speed tracking) For each admissible constant speed command, the realized-speed dynamics have a locally unique, exponentially stable equilibrium at the commanded speed, with a known bounded transient response within the agent’s actuation limits.

## S4 Local control design ensures the emergence of a traversal order across an engaged group 

We analyze the steady states of the fast decision–attention–speed subsystem to show how a collective traversal order emerges from local NDD controllers and how local parameter choices tune this emergent response. The time-scale separation established in Section S3 allows us to hold the variables evolving on slower time-scales (remaining traversal distances and sensed interaction network) fixed, during the negotiation, while retaining the feedback between decisions, attention, realized speeds, and speed-dependent conflict evidence. The entries of the interaction-sensitivity matrix 𝑨 in Equation (6) quantify how decision-induced speed changes affect each agent’s conflict evidence. They depend on the locally tunable pressure and weighting parameters and the decision-to-speed map. As agents sense and respond to one another’s motion, their local NDD feedback autonomously shapes the entries of 𝑨 . Its dominant eigenvalue 

𝜆 max ( 𝑨 ) captures how these local interactions collectively govern the stability of indecision. We identify a threshold 𝜆 ∗ such that collective indecision loses stability when 𝜆 max ( 𝑨 ) crosses it from below. We derive local controller conditions that ensure pressure-dependent feedback drives this eigenvalue above 𝜆 ∗ when any interaction becomes safety-critical and returns it below 𝜆 ∗ when all interactions are weak. Under the local bifurcation conditions in Section S7, this transition gives rise to attracting equilibria with differentiated decisions and speeds. We show how the resulting speed differences between conflicting agents, measured relative to their nominal speeds, define a consistent traversal order. We then establish conditions under which the decision–attention–speed state tracks the changing equilibrium closely enough to preserve this order as the geometry evolves slowly. As conflicts clear, declining pressure brings the system back below threshold, allowing decisions and attention to relax and agents to resume nominal motion. ## S6 Safe-and-live deconfliction across a changing conflict graph
