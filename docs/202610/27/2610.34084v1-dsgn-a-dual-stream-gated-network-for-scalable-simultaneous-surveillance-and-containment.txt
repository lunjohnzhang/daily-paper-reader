# DSGN: A Dual-Stream Gated Network for Scalable Simultaneous Surveillance and Containment 

Heng Zhang 1,†, Ziyang Zuo 1,†, Guoxiang Zhao 2,∗, and Xiaoqiang Ren 1,2,3

Abstract —The simultaneous surveillance and containment (SSC) problem arises in multi-robot applications such as perime-ter security and environmental monitoring, where robots must survey a region of interest and coordinate to enclose targets once they are detected. Surveillance favors spatial dispersion for coverage, whereas containment requires target-centric cohesion for enclosure. This conflict makes heuristic switching unstable, and the instability becomes more pronounced as larger robotic networks introduce more inter-agent and agent-target relations. End-to-end DRL reduces reliance on hand-crafted switching, but conventional single-stream policies often mix surveillance and containment cues in one shared feature space that could entangle dispersion-oriented and target-centric signals and weaken task transitions. In this paper, we propose the Dual-Stream Gated Network (DSGN), an architecture designed to address scale-amplified task-transition difficulties by separating surveillance and containment into specialized representation streams and coordinating them through a context-aware gating mechanism. A probabilistic prior further regularizes the gating process, re-ducing ambiguous intermediate decisions and supporting clearer behavioral transitions under large-scale interaction complexity. Evaluations show that DSGN maintains strong task efficacy and scalability under constant-density cross-scale settings. Additional ablation and phase-specific evaluations show that the proposed dynamic gating mechanism and probabilistic prior support effective task arbitration and reliable mode switching in both pure surveillance and task-switching scenarios. The source code is available at https://github.com/zuozysama/DSGN. 

Index Terms —Multi-Robot Systems, Reinforcement Learning, Surveillance Robotic Systems.

Although searching and containment have each been extensively studied, they are generally formulated separately. The simultaneous surveillance and containment (SSC) problem considered here couples them by requiring a multi-robot system to balance continuous area coverage with timely containment of targets. Centralized coordination [7] can exploit global information, but its dependence on the global system state makes it prone to the curse of dimensionality and limits its applicability to large-scale robotic networks. Distributed methods [8], [9] mitigate this limitation by allowing robots to coordinate through local observations and inter-robot interactions. Existing distributed methods relevant to the containment component of SSC have primarily focused on target interception and containment. Differential game methods [9] model interactions between pursuers and evaders as coupled optimization problems, but they can be difficult to apply when environment dynamics are hard to model. Bio-inspired heuristic methods [10] use manually designed interaction rules, which are intuitive to implement but can be hard to scale across varying team sizes, environments, and target behaviors. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) methods [11] have therefore been introduced to learn containment policies directly from interactions, reduc-ing dependence on explicit dynamics modeling and manually specified interaction rules. These studies cover both single-target containment [12] and multi-target settings [13], and re-cent work [6] has scaled up multi-target coordination to dozens of robots by leveraging attention-based architectures. The minimal capture time in the graph-based pursuit-evasion for-mulation has been investigated in [14]. However, in practical multi-robot applications such as environmental monitoring [5] and autonomous fishing [3], targets may not be immediately known in the region of interest. This means that containment alone is insufficient and that persistent surveillance is also required to locate targets before coordinated interception or enclosure [4], [5]. Multi-robot surveillance has been widely studied for persis-tent coverage and target localization. Classical visibility-based pursuit-evasion studies established complete search strategies and searchability conditions in polygonal and curved envi-ronments [15], [16], [17]. Multi-robot target search has also addressed scalable planning for moving targets [18], lim-ited sensing and environment geometry [19], and DRL-based mobile-target search [20]. These studies primarily address target search and detection, whereas SSC couples persistent surveillance with post-detection geometric containment. However, specifying analytical switching conditions for coupled target search and interception in SSC is difficult because the desired behavior depends jointly on coverage status, target observations, robot distribution, and containment geometry. An end-to-end DRL paradigm offers an alternative to discrete logic for the SSC problem by learning decentral-ized policies directly from robot local observations [11], [23]. However, conventional unified architectures often encode surveillance and containment cues in a shared feature space, where dispersion-oriented coverage signals and target-centric enclosure signals can introduce conflicting optimization de-mands [24]. Within this end-to-end paradigm, a commonly used design is to organize a single policy with specialized representation streams and coordinate them through a gat-ing mechanism, as explored in mixture-of-experts [25] and modular reinforcement learning [26]. Fixed or hand-crafted gating mechanisms can be difficult to adapt as observations and task demands change, while naive adaptive gating may incur chattering that could cause task failures. To address this challenge, we propose the Dual-Stream Gated Network (DSGN). DSGN separates state processing into two specialized streams. A spatial-explorative stream 

reasons about coverage gaps for surveillance, and a tactical-exploitative stream computes geometric formations for con-tainment. A context-aware gating mechanism coordinates the two streams to produce a unified policy for surveillance and containment. We further regularize this gating process with a Beta-distribution prior to stabilize switching decisions. Numerical experiments show that DSGN improves the balance between surveillance and containment and maintains perfor-mance as the number of agents increases. The key contributions of this work are as follows: 1) Task-Disentangled Representation for SSC: Built on transformer-based relational encoding, DSGN separates surveillance and containment into two task-specific attention streams, which reduce interference between dispersion-oriented coverage and target-centered containment within a unified end-to-end pol-icy. 2) Robust Distributed Task Arbitration: The context-aware gating mechanism and Beta-distribution regularization enable robust switching between surveillance and contain-ment. Together, these components support stable and deci-sive task transitions when target observations are intermittent or task demands change. 3) Scalable Multi-Robot Coor-dination Performance: Across constant-density cross-scale evaluations, DSGN maintains high task efficacy, execution optimality, safe coordination, and stable formation quality as team and environment scales increase. To address the structural incompatibility between surveil-lance and containment outlined in Section II, we propose the Dual-Stream Gated Network. DSGN decouples the state representation process into spatial-explorative reasoning for area surveillance and tactical-exploitative coordination for target containment. This architectural inductive bias allows each pursuer to preserve task-specific representations while a context-aware gating mechanism dynamically arbitrates the two streams to produce a unified decentralized policy.

B. Network Architecture 

The proposed DSGN, illustrated in Fig. 2, is an end-to-end pipeline that maps the structured input sequence Xtn to a discrete kinematic command atn ∈ A . Instead of prematurely compressing surveillance and containment cues into a single shared representation, DSGN processes heterogeneous obser-vations through four stages: heterogeneous feature projection ,

relational context encoding , task-disentangled gated fusion ,and distributional action inference . This design first aligns different entity types into a common latent space, then reasons about their spatial relations, and finally arbitrates two task-specific contexts through a learnable gate. 

1) Heterogeneous Feature Projection: The input sequence 

Xtn contains heterogeneous entities, including the ego token, teammates, observed evaders, visible obstacles, and surveil-lance nodes. These entities carry different semantics and feature dimensions: for example, a surveillance node mainly reflects coverage urgency through idleness, whereas an ob-served evader provides target-centric geometric cues. To avoid mixing such semantics at the raw-feature level, we use type-specific multi-layer perceptrons to project each token into a common latent space Rd, where d denotes the latent feature dimension. A learnable type embedding Etype is added to preserve categorical identity after projection, and the validity mask M n,t

A. Training Setup 

We extend the open-source multi-robot navigation simulator described in [30] by retaining its kinematic, sensing, and col-lision models and adding surveillance nodes, reactive evaders, and multi-target containment. The workspace is discretized into a uniform rectangular grid, and one surveillance node ck is placed at the center of each grid cell for persistent surveillance and idleness computation. For an environment of size W × H,the maximum grid-cell side length is Lmax = ηR sense 

√2,ensuring that half the diagonal of each cell is no larger than ηR sense . With Rsense = 20 m and η = 0 .95 , this gives 

Lmax ≈ 26 .9 m. The numbers of grid cells along the two axes are Nx = ⌈W/L max ⌉ and Ny = ⌈H/L max ⌉, with cell dimensions ∆x = W/N x and ∆y = H/N y .During training, each agent’s input includes at most the nearest 5 obstacles, 5 teammates, 8 evaders, and 16 surveil-lance nodes; unused entries are zero-padded. Successful con-tainment follows Eq. (1) with λuni = 3 . A training episode terminates when all evaders are contained, fewer than three pursuers remain active, or the episode exceeds 3000 time steps. To ease learning in the early stages, training follows a progressive curriculum that gradually increases the number of entities, as shown in Table I. 

B. Evaluation Setup 

Without retraining, each policy is tested across nine scale– density configurations (Table II), ranging from 12v3 (12 pursuers versus 3 evaders) to 77v19. The largest evaluation setting, 77v19, exceeds the largest curriculum training setting, 15v4. Within each density level, the environment size varies with team size to maintain approximately constant mobile-entity density across map scales. This design helps separate the effects of increasing team and map sizes from changes in overall crowding. All experiments use a consistent rectangular outer boundary. Except for the separately reported action-granularity test in Section IV-E4, each method and arbitration variant is evaluated over 40 trials per random seed, and across 5 independent random seeds in every task scenario. Initial poses of pursuers, evaders, and obstacles are randomized in each trial. For these comparisons, results are reported as seed-level means across seeds with 95% confidence intervals. m/s and use a 3 × 3 action space ( aacc ∈ { 0, ±0.4} m/s 2,

ω ∈ { 0, ±π/ 6} rad/s). These actions form a platform-agnostic high-level command interface; related learned pursuit policies have been deployed in real-world settings [11]. Evaders are guided by a reactive artificial potential field (APF) [31] and use vmax = 3 .5 m/s with a finer rotational spectrum (ω ∈ { 0, ±π/ 12 , ±π/ 6} rad/s) under the same acceleration limits. This kinematic difference precludes naive individual pursuit. We evaluate cross-scale performance using five metrics. 

Strict Success Rate (SR) is the fraction of trials in which all evaders are contained without collisions. Containment Time 

(Tcap ) measures the time from episode start to containment completion, conditioned on episodes that complete contain-ment. Collision Ratio (CR) is computed per episode as the number of pursuers that experience at least one collision with an obstacle or teammate, divided by the total number of pursuers; repeated collisions by the same pursuer are counted once. Containment Angle Variance (Var) is computed when an individual evader is contained and measures the angular uni-formity of the containment formation. Patrol Completion Steps 

(P-Steps) measures the steps from containment completion to final surveillance coverage, conditioned on completion of both stages.

We compare DSGN with TransIQN [6], IQN [28], Deep Q-Network (DQN) [23], and a reactive Heuristic baseline based on APF [31] under identical settings. For the main comparisons, all learned methods use the same shaped re-ward (Section III-C), progressive curriculum (Table I), action space, and 7M steps training budget per seed. The TransIQN baseline uses the same transformer backbone as DSGN but represents surveillance and containment in a single ego-token feature, providing a direct comparison between shared and task-specific relational representations. D. Results and Discussion 

Fig. 4 compares DSGN, its three gate and prior ablations, and the fixed equal-weight Average variant across the nine configurations in Table II; Table A.I summarizes the results by scale. 

Task Completion : DSGN has the highest or tied-highest scale-averaged Strict SR at all three scales in Fig. 4(a), despite not leading in every configuration. This supports more reliable task completion through separate surveillance and containment streams. 

Containment Efficiency : In Fig. 4(b), DSGN’s scale-averaged Tcap exceeds the fastest variant by only 0.1–2.1 s. Its short times suggest that relational attention aids spatial assignment around evaders, while dual-stream processing adds little execution delay. 

Safety : DSGN has the lowest scale-averaged CR at all three scales in Fig. 4(c), although the best variant varies by configuration. This suggests that disentangled task processing reduces interference between surveillance and containment behaviors. 

Post-Containment Surveillance : DSGN has the lowest scale-averaged P-Steps at all three scales in Fig. 4(d), despite some configuration-level exceptions. This suggests that its surveillance stream supports prompt coverage completion after containment. Together, Fig. 4 and Table III suggest that task-specific streams complement relational reasoning: unlike a shared stream that may mix surveillance and containment cues, DSGN addresses this issue by separating the two objec-tives into specialized streams and coordinating them through context-aware gating. This design supports safer and more consistent multi-robot coordination as the number of interact-ing agents increases. 

E. Ablation Study 

To analyze task arbitration and mode switching, we conduct structural ablations and phase-specific evaluations (Tables III– V and Table A.I); we also assess sensitivity to search-region geometry and action granularity (Tables A.II and A.III). Ran-dom replaces the learned gate with a random gate; Average assigns equal weights to the two streams; Rule-based uses a hand-crafted spatial rule for arbitration; DSGN-WO-BETA removes the Beta-prior regularization; DSGN-SCALAR re-places vector-wise gating with a single scalar gate; and DSGN-SCALAR-WO-BETA combines scalar gating without the Beta prior.

Removing either the Beta prior or vector-wise gating worsens scale-averaged SR, CR, Var, and P-Steps at all three scales (Table A.I). This supports both components’ roles in reliable task arbitration, while Average retains a modest 

Tcap advantage.
