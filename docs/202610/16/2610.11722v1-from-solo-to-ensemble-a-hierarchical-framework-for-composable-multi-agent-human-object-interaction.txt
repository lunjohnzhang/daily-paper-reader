# From Solo to Ensemble: A Hierarchical Framework for Composable Multi-Agent Human-Object Interaction

Abstract: Physics-based human-object interaction has achieved robust single-agent manipulation skills, yet extending them to multi-agent cooperative tasks remains challenging. Existing approaches typically adapt interaction policies through task-specific fine-tuning, which entangles low-level contact-rich execu-tion with high-level coordination and limits reuse across object geometries, inter-action types, and team sizes. We propose a hierarchical framework that converts a single-agent HOI policy into a reusable Object-oriented Motion Skill . Specifi-cally, we reinterpret teacher rollouts as object-oriented action supervision by ex-tracting short-horizon object-proxy motions from executed trajectories, and distill task-specific teachers into a low-level skill operating in an Object-oriented Action Space . For downstream tasks, the distilled skill is frozen as a reusable executor, while a high-level policy coordinates multiple agents by generating region-wise object-oriented actions conditioned on the shared object, task goal, agent states, and local manipulation regions. This formulation shifts multi-agent HOI learning from direct contact-rich full-body control to compact object-level proxy-motion coordination. Experiments on diverse HOI tasks show that the distilled Object-oriented Motion Skill supports robust proxy-motion execution and enables com-posable policy learning across different interaction types, object geometries, and team sizes. 

Keywords: Multi-Agent Human-Object Interaction, Skill Distillation 

## 1 Introduction 

Physics-based character control has made substantial progress in learning robust full-body skills from motion imitation, adversarial motion priors, latent skill learning, and unified humanoid con-trollers [1–5]. Recent human-object interaction (HOI) methods further extend these advances to contact-rich object manipulation and human-scene interaction [6–10]. However, multi-human HOI introduces a distinct coordination challenge: multiple humanoids must coordinate contact locations, motion directions, and force application around the same manipulated object. Existing methods have explored transferring interaction skills to multi-human tasks. For example, CooHOI [11] adapts learned interaction policies to cooperative manipulation through object-level coupling. Such transfer is typically realized through policy initialization or task-specific fine-tuning. Although the adapted policy can solve a specific cooperative task, it still entangles low-level motor skill and high-level coordination within a single task policy. As a result, the learned behavior is difficult to reuse, compose, or transfer to new object geometries and cooperative configurations. Our key insight is that a single-human HOI policy should not be directly fine-tuned into a task-specific multi-human policy. Instead, it should be preserved and converted into a reusable Object-

This leads to a hierarchical and decoupled control framework, where the low-level Object-oriented Motion Skill handles contact-rich execution and the high-level policy performs compositional object-level coordination through proxy-motion planning. To make the skill reusable, the control interface should neither be tied to a specific task goal nor over-constrain the humanoid motion. We therefore define the Object-oriented Action Space as short-horizon motions of an object proxy, allowing the low-level policy to learn how to realize desired proxy motion through contact selection, balance control, and full-body coordination. Specifically, we reinterpret rollouts from single-human HOI teacher policies as object-oriented ac-tion supervision by extracting future object-proxy motions from their executed trajectories. This converts task-specific teachers into an Object-oriented Motion Skill that can be frozen and reused as a low-level executor. In downstream tasks, a high-level policy observes the shared object, task goal, agent states, and local manipulation regions, and generates region-wise object-oriented actions. In this way, downstream RL no longer optimizes contact-rich full-body control directly, but instead learns cooperative decision making within the compact Object-oriented Action Space. Our contributions are threefold: • We establish a transferable formulation for physics-based multi-human HOI, shifting the focus from task-specific cooperative policy learning to transferable object-oriented motion coordination. • We develop a distillation-based hierarchical learning framework that converts single-human HOI knowledge into a reusable Object-oriented Motion Skill, enabling high-level policies to coordinate multiple humans through short-horizon object-proxy motions. • We validate the framework on cooperative HOI tasks, showing that an Object-oriented Motion Skill distilled from a single-human skill supports robust proxy-motion execution and enables high-level policy learning across different interaction types, object geometries, and team sizes. 

## 2 Related Work 

Human-Object Interaction Motion Synthesis and Physics-Based Control. Character anima-tion and motion synthesis have extensively studied human motion in 3D scenes and human-object interaction, including kinematic human-scene and dynamic-object interaction methods [12–24]. Re-cent diffusion-based approaches further improve motion diversity and controllability by condition-2ing on scene constraints, text, action labels, or object trajectories [25–36]. However, such kinematic generation methods often lack physical validity, especially for long-horizon contact-rich interactions that require sustained contacts, force exchange, and object dynamics. Physics-based character control addresses this issue by learning closed-loop policies for physically plausible full-body behaviors from motion imitation, adversarial motion priors, latent skills, and large-scale motion datasets [1–5, 37, 38]. These techniques have been extended to physics-based HOI and human-scene interaction, enabling contact-rich behaviors such as object catching and ma-nipulation, scene-level interactions, dynamic HOI imitation, reusable sports skills, task-tokenized scene interaction, and whole-body HOI control across diverse objects [6, 7, 9, 10, 39–46]. While these methods substantially improve physical realism and task coverage, their control abstractions are usually tied to reference motions, task labels, or single-human interaction settings. 

Multi-Character Interaction and Cooperative Object Manipulation. Compared with single-human HOI, cooperative interaction introduces a stronger coordination challenge: a shared object-level task must be decomposed into per-human contacts, motions, and force application. Existing multi-person datasets and kinematic animation methods have enabled visually plausible human-human interactions and choreographic motions [47–66]. However, these methods mainly target motion synthesis and do not guarantee physically valid contacts, forces, or shared object dynamics. Recent physics-based multi-character methods improve physical plausibility by combining gener-ated motions with controllers or converting multi-human motions into executable policies [8, 67–73], but they mostly focus on human-human interaction, sports, reaction, or collision avoidance rather than contact-rich cooperative object manipulation [74–76]. Cooperative object manipulation has also been explored in legged robotics, including multi-robot payload transport, pushing, and collaborative carrying [77–82]. These systems demonstrate object-level coordination, but they typically rely on quadrupedal morphology, engineered manipulation interfaces, or task-specific control designs, and do not address full-body humanoid HOI with motion priors. Most closely related to our setting, CooHOI [11] learns cooperative human-object interaction through shared object dynamics, but its adaptation still couples low-level motor execution with task-specific multi-human coordination. In contrast, our method preserves single-human HOI skills as a reusable low-level skill operating over object-oriented proxy motions, enabling high-level policies to compose local object motions across object geometries, interaction types, and team sizes. 

## 3 Method 

Our method builds a composable HOI control hierarchy around object-oriented proxy motions. Sec. 3.1 first introduces the Object-oriented Motion Skill , a reusable low-level policy that realizes short-horizon motions of object proxies through physically feasible humanoid control. Its Object-oriented Action Space provides a compact interface between high-level task reasoning and contact-rich execution. Sec. 3.2 then trains task-specific high-level policies on top of the frozen skill, allow-ing single- and multi-human HOI tasks to be solved by generating region-wise proxy motions rather than full-body motions or torque-level actions. Fig. 2 provides an overview of the full pipeline.

3.2 High-Level Policy: Composable Cooperative Planning for Shared Object Manipulation 

The learned Object-oriented Motion Skill enables downstream HOI tasks to be formulated as proxy-motion planning over task-relevant manipulation regions. Instead of generating full-body humanoid 5motions or low-level control actions, the high-level policy predicts object-oriented actions that spec-ify how local object proxies should move in the near future. The frozen Object-oriented Motion Skill then realizes these proxy-level motions through physical humanoid control. This forms a com-posable hierarchy: downstream tasks only train task-specific high-level policies, while sharing the same low-level Object-oriented Motion Skill. For a task with N humanoids, we assign each humanoid a manipulation region ρi according to the object geometry and interaction role. This region specifies where the humanoid interacts with the object, but does not prescribe its full-body motion, contact pattern, or the complete object transport strategy. ## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Evaluation on Compositional HOI Tasks Evaluation Protocol. We evaluate whether the Object-oriented Motion Skill learned from single-human interaction can serve as a reusable low-level interface for downstream object manipulation. In all tasks, πlow is frozen, and only a task-specific πhigh is trained to predict object-oriented actions for local proxies, which are then executed by the frozen skill. We test multi-human carrying and pushing, including two-human sofa carrying, four-human H-box/cross-box carrying, and two-/four-human sofa pushing. We also include single-human armchair/table carrying and large-box pushing to evaluate transfer across object geometries and interaction modes. Each object or manipulation region is represented by the same cuboid proxy format as in skill learning, allowing all settings to share the Object-oriented Action Space without modifying πlow .All experiments are conducted in Isaac Gym [83] with 4096 parallel environments. We use the same humanoid model as prior physics-based HOI controllers [11, 42], with 15 rigid bodies and 28 PD-controlled joints. Objects are initialized with random yaw in [0 , 2π] and root-to-target distance of 4–10 m . We report success rate(%) and precision(cm) over 4096 environments, with success defined as the object root reaching within 0.2 m of the target.

Proxy-motion Tracking. Table 2 shows that our method achieves the best tracking performance on teacher-rollout targets. This indicates that relabeling successful teacher rollouts into future proxy motions preserves physically feasible interaction behavior without exposing the student to full-body reference motion. The advantage becomes more pronounced on random proxy-motion targets. Al-though the adapted baselines receive the same object-oriented action, their performance drops when the target motions move away from the teacher-rollout distribution. In contrast, our Object-oriented Motion Skill maintains higher target-reaching and trajectory success rates, showing that teacher-guided relabeling and random-motion RL post-training expand the executable region of the Object-oriented Action Space. These results support object-oriented motion as a compact low-level in-terface: it provides sufficient temporal guidance for object transport while preserving flexibility in contact selection, balance, and locomotion. 

Ablation. Table 2 further validates the contribution of the two training stages. Removing on-line distillation causes a clear performance drop, especially on random proxy-motion targets. This suggests that object-proxy tracking rewards alone are insufficient to bootstrap contact-rich carry-ing, since stable grasping, balance, and locomotion must first emerge before trajectory rewards be-come informative. Teacher-guided rollout and online behavior cloning therefore provide a feasible-interaction prior that stabilizes early skill acquisition. Removing random-motion RL post-training preserves strong performance on teacher-rollout targets but substantially weakens generalization to random proxy-motion targets. This indicates that rela-beled teacher rollouts provide high-quality supervision near the source task manifold, but cover only a limited range of executable object motions. Random-motion post-training expands this range and turns the distilled policy from a teacher-rollout follower into a reusable Object-oriented Motion Skill for downstream high-level policy learning. 

## 5 Limitations 

Our framework studies compositional HOI within a reusable Object-oriented Motion Skill, where high-level policies coordinate local proxy motions through a shared low-level executor. However, it does not explicitly address composition across heterogeneous skill types or long-horizon temporal task structures, such as multi-stage task decomposition and dynamic role reassignment. Incorporat-ing skill-level composition with higher-level planning is a promising direction for future work. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented a hierarchical framework for composable physics-based HOI that converts task-specific single-human interaction policies into a reusable Object-oriented Motion Skill . By rela-beling teacher rollouts with short-horizon object-proxy motions, our method learns a low-level skill under the Object-oriented Action Space , instead of directly fine-tuning single-human policies into task-specific cooperative controllers. The frozen skill realizes region-wise object-oriented actions 8through contact-rich full-body control, allowing downstream policies to transfer across different object geometries, interaction types, and team sizes.

A.1 Training and Implementation Details Character Model and Policy Inputs. We use a customized simulated humanoid adapted from the AMP character model [2]. The humanoid consists of 15 rigid bodies, 12 actuated joints, and 28 

degrees of freedom (DoFs). The 28 DoFs correspond to the actuated joint space controlled by low-level PD servos, where the policy predicts target joint configurations rather than directly applying torques. At each time step t, the proprioceptive state st describes the physical state of the character. Following AMP [2], it includes the positions, rotations, linear velocities, and angular velocities of all rigid bodies. All quantities are expressed in the character-centric local frame, except for the root orientation and root height, which are kept in the world frame to preserve global elevation information. We represent body rotations using the 6D rotation representation and exclude the global root position from the proprioceptive input. This yields a 223 D state vector st ∈ R223 . At each time step, the policy outputs an action at that specifies target joint rotations for PD control. For 3D spherical joints, the target rotations are parameterized as exponential maps. No external perturbation forces are applied to the humanoid during simulation. For low-level skill learning, we formulate proxy tracking as a short-horizon object-motion execution task. Optimization and Hyperparameters. All policies are trained in Isaac Gym with 4096 paral-lel environments. We use the same simulated humanoid model and PD-control interface for both low-level and high-level policies. The control frequency is 30 Hz, with two physics substeps per control step. Both πlow and πhigh are parameterized by MLP actor-critic networks with hidden sizes [1024 , 1024 , 512] and ReLU activations. Table A.1 summarizes the main implementation details and hyperparameters. The low-level Object-oriented Motion Skill is trained in two stages. In the distillation stage, the student is trained for 15000 epochs using teacher rollouts and the auxiliary human tracking reward. It is then post-trained for another 5000 epochs using randomly generated proxy-motion targets, where only the object-proxy tracking objective is used. For downstream tasks, πlow is frozen and only the high-level policy πhigh is optimized. We train the high-level policy for 10000 , 20000 , and 

40000 epochs for single-agent, two-agent, and four-agent tasks, respectively. To stabilize high-level training, we clip the proxy-motion increments predicted by πhigh . Since the control frequency is 30 Hz, a maximum proxy speed of 2.5 m /s corresponds to a per-step displace-ment limit 

∆pmax = 2.530 ≈ 0.083 m . (A.17) We therefore clip each consecutive-frame translational proxy-motion increment to this range. This prevents the high-level policy from producing physically infeasible proxy trajectories that cannot be executed by the frozen low-level skill. 

Failure mode compared with CooHOI. Fig. A.1 shows a typical failure case of CooHOI [11]. After one or two failed attempts to lift the object, the policy can get stuck: the agents stay around the object but stop making further effective adjustments. In other words, once the interaction falls into a bad local state, CooHOI may fail to recover and the whole cooperative behavior enters a deadlock. A likely reason is that CooHOI relies on implicit coordination through object-conditioned observations, which can be sensitive to the local object state, especially object velocity. When the agents are already close to the object while the object remains static, the policy may no longer receive a strong enough signal to continue corrective behavior. Our method is less prone to this issue. The high-level policy explicitly predicts short-horizon local future trajectories, and the low-level skill is further post-trained with RL to respond robustly to diverse trajectory commands. As a result, even from imperfect contact states or near-static configurations, our policy can still keep producing meaningful corrective actions instead of becoming inactive. 20
