# Auditable and Deployable Multi-Agent Motion Planning via Posetal Games from Legal Guidelines 

Florian Lercher 1,2*, Karan Mahesh 3,4*, Yujun Huang 1, Meshal Alharbi 1, Runyu Zhang 1, Hanna Krasowski 5,Michael R. Benjamin 3, Matthias Althoff 2, Gioele Zardini 1

Abstract —When robots are deployed in the real-world, they must interact with other agents and accomplish their own goals while adhering to legal constraints. These potentially conflicting objectives are often only partially prioritized, making scalarized objectives or fully specified hierarchies unsuitable. We present a framework for multi-agent motion planning under legal re-quirements based on games with partially ordered preferences (posetal games). Scenario-independent legal guidelines order abstract legal concerns, from which we automatically derive agent preferences and a legal game for any concrete scenario. Agents may refine their legal preferences with private objectives, and regulators may refine the guideline as laws evolve. We prove that such customizations can only remove equilibria and thus preserve legality. To coordinate agents without explicitly communicating private preferences, we introduce belief play, which provably recovers the true equilibria from conservative local games, and a distributed iterative best-response protocol requiring less information. We apply the framework to maritime navigation, systematically lifting pairwise traffic rules to multi-vessel encounters. On seven encounters with up to four vessels, every rule violation of the committed equilibria is certified as forced by the legal hierarchy or unsatisfiable, and defectors are identified from public information in all tested cases. These results indicate that guideline-based posetal games offer an auditable route from partially specified laws to multi-agent motion planning. 

Index Terms —Motion and path planning, multi-robot systems, game theory, formal methods, marine robotics, legal compliance

Since not all objectives can be sat-isfied simultaneously in general, minimum-violation planning prioritizes them and systematically violates lower-priority ob-jectives when necessary to satisfy higher-priority ones [1], [2]. Legally compliant multi-agent motion planning is nevertheless difficult for at least three reasons. First, some objectives are incomparable, in the sense that one cannot assert that either objective is more important than the other. This is particularly relevant for legal regulations, where authorities typically spec-ify only a partial priority order [3]. Second, many traffic laws are stated as rules between pairs of agents, so the obligations of an agent depend on which other agents are present, and obligations toward different agents might conflict (Fig. 1). Third, in multi-agent settings, agents respond strategically to the actions of other agents [4], whose private objectives are typically unknown. Taken together, these obstacles call for a framework that derives the preferences of the agents from the law for every scenario, preserves the partial priorities of the law, and coordinates the agents without requiring them to disclose their private objectives. A particularly important instance of this problem is motion planning for autonomous vessels in the maritime domain. The International Code of Safety for Maritime Autonomous Ships [6], adopted in 2026, requires autonomous vessels to comply with the maritime traffic rules laid out in the Conven-tion on the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGS) [5]. However, the COLREGS are only defined for pairs of vessels which may lead to conflicting requirements in encounters involving more than two vessels, as shown in Fig. 1. Auditability requires agents to exhibit predictable behavior and provide explanations for their actions, particularly when not all conflicting objectives can be satisfied. Customizability 

enables regulators to revise legal priorities and manufacturers to refine priorities that laws leave unspecified. Moreover, any 

deployable algorithm has to allow agents to coordinate despite their limited knowledge of the private objectives of other agents. To meet these requirements, we present, to the best of our knowledge, the first systematic method for automatically constructing legally compliant motion-planning games from scenario-independent legal guidelines. Our framework repre-sents objective hierarchies as partially ordered preferences, resulting in posetal games [4], for which we provide solution protocols that operate under limited information. The key idea is to separate what the law prescribes from what a scenario instantiates. Regulators specify a guideline , i.e., a partial order over abstract legal concerns such as collision avoidance, and every concrete scenario inherits the preferences of its agents by matching their legal metrics to these concerns. Since every customization of the resulting preferences is a refinement, and refining preferences can only remove equilibria, legality is preserved by construction. We demonstrate the practical appli-cability of the framework through a case study of COLREGS-compliant motion planning for multi-vessel encounters. 

A. Related Work 

Our work lies at the intersection of formal specifications for robotics, game-theoretic motion planning with ordered ob-jectives, and COLREGS-compliant maritime navigation. Since formal specifications provide an interpretable representation of complex objectives and serve as the building blocks of posetal games, we first review their use in robotics, followed by specification-based approaches to multi-agent motion planning and related game-theoretic formulations with ordered prefer-ences. Finally, we discuss existing approaches for COLREGS-compliant multi-vessel motion planning to provide context for our case study. 

1) Specifications for Robotic Interactions: Formal speci-fications, initially used primarily for offline monitoring and runtime verification, have become core components of learn-ing, synthesis, and control methods [7]–[14]. They provide interpretable representations of objectives and constraints and, in multi-agent settings, requirements governing interactions among agents. For example, autonomous-driving systems must satisfy specifications ranging from lane-keeping requirements to appropriate responses to emergency vehicles [15], [16]. In unstructured environments, interaction requirements are commonly expressed through pairwise rules or fixed priority structures, which favor concise representations at the expense of completeness. For instance, the airborne collision-avoidance system ACAS-X determines priorities for pairwise aircraft encounters and relies on heuristics to combine the resulting evaluations [17], [18]. Even in this pairwise setting, the pre-computed lookup tables are sufficiently large to motivate ded-icated compression methods [19]. This scalability challenge is further amplified in domains such as warehouse navigation and maritime robotics, where agents and interactions are more diverse [20]–[23]. Rulebooks [3], [24] reduce the need for exhaustive specification by prioritizing objectives and permit-ting lower-priority objectives to be violated when necessary to satisfy higher-priority ones. Our framework builds on this principle and extends it to multiple strategic agents whose obligations depend on the scenario. 

2) Motion Planning with Formal Objectives: Motion-planning methods based on formal objectives generally adopt one of two perspectives: single-agent planning, in which other agents are treated as uncooperative, or multi-agent planning, in which varying degrees of cooperation are assumed. In single-agent settings, the behavior of surrounding agents is typically modeled using worst-case assumptions, reflecting minimal or no cooperation [22], [25]. Moreover, formal safety specifications are assigned higher priority than performance objectives to guarantee specification compliance by the ego agent [22], [26], [27]. In multi-agent settings, game-theoretic motion planning naturally models strategic interactions between agents. Most existing approaches require the objectives of each agent to be expressed as a scalar cost function, e.g., [28]–[31]. How-ever, scalarization can obscure the relative priorities among objectives so that the resulting behavior becomes difficult to interpret and predict. 

3) Games with Ordered Objectives: To increase inter-pretability when rich preference structures are present, game-theoretic formulations that allow for enforcing orders of pref-erences have been proposed. The simplest structure is a two-level hierarchy, common when constraints, such as safety re-quirements, have strict priority over the performance objective. For example, in urban driving games [32], a shared collision-avoidance objective takes strict priority over individual costs. These personal costs may only depend on the personal action of the agent, a limitation that is lifted in [33] by incorporating zero-sum components. Games of ordered preferences [34] further generalize the objective hierarchy by allowing an arbitrary number of levels, corresponding to a total order over objectives. An iterative best-response solver for these games is presented in [35]. However, requiring a total order over legal requirements places a significant burden on regulators. Posetal games [4] relax this assumption by enabling objectives to remain incomparable, i.e., they admit a partial order over the objectives. Thus, they support the most general preference structure among the methods considered here. Although the methods in [4], [32]–[35] compute Nash equilibria, their practical deployment remains challenging. In particular, they assume complete knowledge of the preferences of all involved agents. Moreover, they do not provide amechanism for translating general legal requirements into the preferences required to model a specific scenario. 

4) COLREGS-Compliant Multi-Vessel Motion Planning: 

The COLREGS have been translated into formal specifica-tions to provide a precise, machine-interpretable representa-3

tion [36]–[38]. These formalizations support the monitoring, testing, and synthesis of behavior in pairwise vessel en-counters. However, because the COLREGS primarily specify pairwise obligations, they do not explicitly prescribe how to prioritize conflicting obligations arising from simultaneous encounters with multiple vessels, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Specification-based approaches to maritime navigation have predominantly adopted an ego-centric perspective in which the behavior of surrounding vessels is monitored or modeled rather than strategically coordinated. Examples include real-world monitoring of COLREGS encounter situations for collision avoidance [39] and reactive vessel simulations in which the first detected conflict determines the executed maneuver [40]. Such methods can enforce or evaluate the obligations of an individual vessel, but do not explicitly model the potentially heterogeneous preferences of multiple interacting vessels. Existing multi-agent approaches commonly employ central-ized game-theoretic methods or homogeneous reinforcement-learning controllers with scalarized objectives [41]–[45]. How-ever, scalarization obscures the priority structure among objec-tives, and centralized or homogeneous formulations generally cannot accommodate private, agent-specific preferences. To the best of our knowledge, our framework is the first to systematically lift the pairwise COLREGS rules to encounters involving an arbitrary number of vessels while preserving an interpretable hierarchy of legal objectives. It further supports agents with private preferences and solution protocols that operate without requiring those preferences to be disclosed. 

B. Contributions 

We present a framework for multi-agent motion planning under legal requirements with partially ordered objectives, which automates the translation of practical scenarios into the theory of posetal games [4]. Our contributions are fourfold.

Systematic construction of legal posetal games. We introduce guidelines to represent priorities among legal concerns independently of the concrete scenario. In this section, we address part (i) of Problem 1 and present a process that transforms laws and a given scenario into a posetal game whose solutions meet legal requirements. In Sec. III-A, we introduce legal metrics to quantify the legal concerns and explain how to derive these metrics from generic rules. As shown in Fig. 4, the relevant legal metrics depend on the agents and interactions present in a particular scenario so that manually specifying a preference for every possible scenario is infeasible. Sec. III-B therefore introduces guide-lines to allow regulators to specify a scenario-independent partial order over legal concerns, such as collision avoidance. A. Legally Required Metrics 

Legal requirements typically include pairwise rules that the ego agent must follow with respect to every other agent. This is particularly common in traffic applications, including road [46], sea [5], and air [47] traffic. Many existing for-malizations express each pairwise rule as a generic temporal logic specification φ parameterized by the two involved agents, e.g., [48]–[51] for road traffic and [22], [36]–[38] for sea traffic. Temporal logics are well suited, as they can constrain the temporal evolution of a trajectory, e.g., the temporal operator G demands that a property always holds. While the standard semantics of temporal logic are Boolean, indicating only whether a trajectory satisfies the specification, robustness measures [52] provide quantitative semantics. The sign of the real-valued robustness value indicates satisfaction or violation and its magnitude reflects the corresponding margin. The literature provides many robustness measures (see, e.g., [53, Sec. 2.2] for a survey); the most suitable choice depends on the particular rule being formalized [54]. Robustness measures enable us to turn a temporal logic rule set into the numerical metrics required by our framework. Let

B. Guidelines 

To represent abstract legal concerns, we introduce a set of labels L, each capturing a single aspect of the law. For our example in Fig. 3, the labels could include collision 

for collision avoidance and high -vul and low -vul for the vulnerability of a robot. A concern C ∈ 2L combines several labels to model a more specific aspect of the law. To associate concrete metrics with concerns, we label each metric m with a concern Cm, and say that it addresses every less specific concern C ⊆ C m. In our example, a collision metric may carry the label collision together with a label describing the vulnerability of the other robot, e.g., high -vul . This metric addresses both the general collision concern {collision } and the more specific concern {collision , high -vul } that only captures collisions with highly vulnerable robots. To model the partial order over abstract legal concerns, we introduce guidelines.

B. Distributed Iterative Best-Response 

For our second protocol, we drop the assumption that every agent knows the action space of the others. Thus, the agent 

i ∈ A only knows its own action space Γi and true preference 

Pi. The protocol performs distributed best-response iteration, which yields a NE of the true game once it reaches a fixed point. The agents first assemble an initial action profile, to which each agent contributes its own action, and then forward the current profile from agent to agent. Upon receipt, an agent replaces its own action with a best response under its true preference and forwards the updated profile. Once no agent changes its action, every agent plays a best response, and the profile is a NE of Gtrue . Existing algorithms for distributed IBR, e.g., [60], which are defined for classical games, can be applied to posetal games as the notion of a best response transfers (see Sec. II-A3). If time permits, we can restart the iteration from different action profiles to obtain multiple NEs and select the one that maximizes the social metric s. Note that the protocol can only guarantee optimality of the solution after exhaustive exploration of the action space.

D. Regulatory Change 

We outline how the guideline refinement operations from Sec. III-D could be used to accommodate potential future changes to the COLREGS. Although these modifications are hypothetical, they reflect changes that may reasonably be considered by regulators. We evaluate the framework in one-shot COLREGS motion-planning games, in which each agent commits to a complete dynamically feasible trajectory before the encounter begins. Our goals are threefold. First, we verify that every rule vio-lation at the committed equilibria can be audited and that de-fectors can be identified from public information. Second, we confirm that private and regulatory customizations shrink the set of equilibria as predicted by Theorem 2 and Proposition 4, and we quantify the efficiency cost of private preferences. Finally, we compare the communication cost and solution quality of the two distributed protocols. We first describe the common experimental setup, and then address the three goals.

We presented a framework for multi-agent motion planning under legal requirements with partially ordered objectives, building on posetal games [4]. Guidelines capture priorities among legal concerns independently of the concrete scenario, from which we systematically derive partially ordered agent preferences and the corresponding legal game. To customize the framework while guaranteeing legality, agents augment these preferences with private objectives and regulators re-fine the guideline. Since every such customization is a re-finement and the set of equilibria depends antitonically on the preferences, customizations can only remove equilibria, so that legality is preserved by construction. Even under private preferences, we can deploy our framework using a distributed IBR protocol or belief play, which coordinates agents through conservative local beliefs and provably recovers the equilibria of the true game. We instantiated the framework for COLREGS-compliant motion planning, where it naturally lifts the pairwise rules to multi-vessel encounters. Using the case study, we demonstrated customizability and auditability, including the ability to explain required rule violations and to identify defectors. Our evaluation showed that distributed IBR exchanges solution quality for communication efficiency and weaker information assumptions compared to belief play. Several directions remain open. First, we would like to ex-tend our framework into a receding-horizon scheme, in which agents repeatedly re-solve the game as the encounter evolves and refine their beliefs about the preferences of the others based on observed behavior. The main difficulty is to update the beliefs from observations while keeping them conservative, since the technical result only holds for conservative beliefs. Second, deploying such a scheme on real vessels requires real-time capable solvers. While exhaustive enumeration of the joint action space provides exact references, its cost grows ex-ponentially with the number of agents. Specialized planners for COLREGS encounters that exploit the structure of the game, such as the pairwise symmetric collision metrics, could sub-stantially reduce this cost. Establishing conditions under which the COLREGS game is a potential game would, in addition, guarantee the convergence of distributed IBR, which we have so far observed empirically. Third, the auditability results are relative to the finite action lattice, so that a violation classified as unsatisfiable may become satisfiable under a richer action space. Characterizing how the audit outcome depens on the discretization would strengthen the legal interpretation of such certificates. Fourth, a defector whose action is still a legal best response can steer the equilibrium selection without violating the law, which calls for a mechanism-design perspective on the social metric. Finally, applying our framework to other domains in which regulators specify only partial priorities, like autonomous driving or airborne collision avoidance, would further test its generality. In both domains, interaction rules are also stated pairwise, so the lifting of pairwise rules to many agents would apply directly. APPENDIX
