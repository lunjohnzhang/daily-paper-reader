## 1 Purpose, scope and spatial representations 

Robotic exploration, coverage and deployment algorithms require reproducible environments whose geometry and visibility can be varied independently of the algorithm. This work provides a config-urable generator for two- and three-dimensional worlds, with continuous free-space geometry, discrete occupancy grids and geometric line-of-sight queries. It supplies benchmark worlds and observations, rather than exploration policies. The generator supports six geometry classes (orthogonal, polygonal/polyhedral and curvilinear in 2D and 3D) and six construction families (scattered rooms, clusters, winding passages, caverns, branches and loops). Room and passage dimensions, obstacle configurations and placement can be controlled through fixed values, uniform ranges or probability distributions. Configurations, seeds, geometries and visibility results are saved for reproducible comparisons. The implementation is available at https://github.com/EdwinMeriaux/Dungeon_and_Visibility_ World_Generator .

## 3 World classes and construction families 

Two choices define a generated world: its geometry class determines the form of its walls, while its 

construction family determines how rooms and passages are arranged. A family can be realized in any of the six geometry classes below. 

3.1 Orthogonal, polygonal and curvilinear geometry 

Orthogonal worlds have axis-aligned boundaries. General polygonal worlds have straight edges in 2D; their 3D counterparts are polyhedral, with flat faces. Curvilinear worlds have curved boundaries, represented by a saved tessellation in 2D and ellipsoids and spherical passage sweeps in 3D. 

Orthogonal Polygonal or polyhedral Curvilinear 2D Horizontal and vertical walls Slanted straight walls Approximated curved walls 3D Axis-aligned boxes Flat-sided chambers and passages Ellipsoidal chambers and rounded passages 

3.2 Six construction families 

The shared layout generator records room templates and passage paths before class-specific walls are realized. The room-first families place chambers and connect them; the passage-first families build a backbone or loops and add chambers along them. 

Family Construction before geometric realization Scattered rooms Uneven polar placement of unequal chambers, followed by graph-connected bent paths. Room clusters Random anchors and Gaussian groups of room centers, joined through candidate connections. Caverns A small population of large central chambers connected through graph edges. Winding passages A smoothed main passage with selected chambers and angled side branches. Branches A central chamber with turning arms and rooms along their growth. Irregular loops Deformed closed passage loops, attached chambers and links between loops. 

These descriptions give the default placement patterns. Configured spatial distributions can replace or perturb room positions; size distributions control their dimensions. 54 How the code generates and records a world 

The entry point is scripts/run_pipeline.py . Python constructs the world, derives its occupancy grid and selects sample points. A standalone Rust program computes visibility. The workflow is configuration −→ layout −→ continuous world −→ grid and queries .

4.1 Read the configuration and sample parameters 

The JSON configuration specifies the dimension, geometry class, construction family, world size, grid resolution and random seed. python/wgv/parameters.py interprets a scalar as a fixed value, a two-value range as a uniform law, or a named distribution as a sampling rule. This fragment specifies uniform room positions and dimensions: 

> "rooms": {"count": [7, 10], "width": [6, 9], "length": [7, 11], "placement": {"type": "independent", "x": [12, 38], "y": [12, 38]}}

The room count is drawn from the integers 7 through 10. For each room, wi ∼ U (6 , 9), and its two position coordinates are independently drawn from U(12 , 38). Joint Gaussian and mixture laws can express correlated or clustered placement. World settings are drawn per candidate; dimensions are drawn per room, path or obstacle. 

Example: Gaussian room placement 

Keep the same uniform count and size ranges, and change only the placement: 

> "rooms": {"count": [7, 10], "width": [6, 9], "length": [7, 11], "placement": {"type": "gaussian", "mean": [25, 25], "std": [6, 6]}}

For each room center ( xi, y i), this gives independent coordinates 

xi ∼ N (25 , 62), yi ∼ N (25 , 62).

Room centers concentrate around (25 , 25), with standard deviation 6 world units along each axis. The Gaussian has no hard coordinate bounds; world fitting and acceptance still apply. A covariance 

matrix can replace std to specify correlated coordinates. Room counts, dimensions and placement, as well as the exposed passage and obstacle parameters, can each be controlled through fixed values, uniform ranges or other supported distributions. Different parameters can use different laws; one distribution need not govern the entire world. The following tables list the implemented choices. Integer controls and the selected construction family determine which specifications are valid.

4.3 Construct the continuous geometry 

Next turn that layout into actual free space with walls (Figure 4, middle). python/wgv/generation.py 

creates rooms in the selected geometry class, gives passages finite width, combines their regions and subtracts obstacles. In 2D, realize_2d() uses orthogonal, polygonal or tessellated curved boundaries. In 3D, realize_3d() uses boxes, polyhedral prisms or ellipsoids, with box, prism or overlapping-ball passages. Fitting is recorded; fit_mode="none" preserves explicit positions and rejects geometry outside the domain. 

4.4 Check the candidate and accept or regenerate 

Finally decide whether the candidate meets the requirements (Figure 4, right). The checks require valid, connected continuous free space, safe obstacle placement and the configured grid connectivity. In 3D, primitive-overlap certificates establish connections. The grid is derived by Section 4.5’s rule; narrow passages may disappear and cause rejection. A passing candidate is saved. Otherwise generate() retries with a new attempt seed, records the reason and raises an error at max_attempts . Checking does not reshape a candidate. Acceptance conditions the requested distributions; saved attempts and resolved settings document this selection.

4.6 Choose continuous samples or discrete grid centers 

python/wgv/sampling.py prepares two target sets (Figure 6). For continuous visibility, prepare() de-faults to 1500 uniform interior targets and eight observers. Sampling uses a separate seed, independently of grid resolution; observers follow a deterministic farthest-point sequence on the targets. For discrete visibility, prepare_discrete() uses one target at the center of every free cell or voxel . Its target count equals the number of free grid entries. Saved origin and spacing convert indices to world coordinates. Each continuous observer is assigned its nearest distinct free grid center. Matched queries use these same center coordinates. Continuous sampling also supports gaussian , clearance , poisson_disk , lattice , boundary (2D), 

explicit and a Python callback . Discrete targets come from the occupancy grid.

## 5 Generation time and reproducibility 

This section measures the cost of generating representative worlds and checks whether the same configuration and seed reproduce their saved geometry and occupancy. The experiment uses one configuration from each of the six classes in Section 3. These are six distinct worlds; repeated runs reconstruct each world rather than draw new seeds. 

5.1 Measured generation time 

For each configuration, the study performs one excluded warm-up followed by five timed calls to 

generate(config, output) . Every call writes to a new directory. The runs execute serially on 11 an Apple M2 Max with 12 CPU cores and 64 GiB of memory, using macOS 26.3, Python 3.11.15, NumPy 1.26.4, SciPy 1.16.3, Shapely 2.1.2 and GEOS 3.13.1. BLAS and OpenMP thread limits are set to one. The measured wall-clock interval begins immediately before the generation call and ends when it returns. 5.2 Exact reproduction of the saved world 

After the five timed runs, each configuration is generated once more in a fresh Python process. Across these 36 saved outputs, each world’s continuous geometry, occupancy array and construction records match its first timed run byte for byte. SHA-256 hashes are calculated for every generation artifact except metadata.json ; the metadata is compared as a JSON object after removing only 

generation_seconds . Directory names and measured times can differ while the generated world remains identical. The generator creates a new random stream from the world seed and candidate-attempt index. Repeating the full configuration with the same code and dependency versions therefore repeats parameter draws and candidate decisions in these examples. 8.1 Replayable sensing contract 

A Rust query records the dimension, mode, ordered observers and targets, numerical tolerance and sensor settings. It includes polygon rings for continuous 2D queries, positive and negative primitives for 3D queries, or the array shape, origin, spacings and flattened mask for discrete queries. The standalone executable accepts –input , –output and –threads ; it replays these coordinates and settings independently of world generation. Every showcase retains its continuous input JSON. An online algorithm can submit newly selected observer positions against the same world and target convention, and retain its accumulated observations. 

8.2 Visible and uncovered target sets 

The compact CSR starts with the eight bytes WGVCSR01 , followed by little-endian unsigned 64-bit row, column and nonzero counts: M observers, N targets and K visible pairs. Then M + 1 offsets delimit 

K target indices. Every stored pair is true, so the file occupies 32 + 8( M + 1 + K) bytes .

This layout stores explicit visible sets. It can cost more bytes than a small dense Boolean matrix when many targets are visible; sparsity and bytes per index both matter. Python validates the CSR structure and saves a sparse .npz , row counts, the visible union and its unobserved complement. Coordinate arrays identify every row and column. Timing reports separate the Rust query loop from subprocess and conversion time. 

## 9 Discussion and conclusion 

For vision-based online tasks, the framework separates the room-and-passage layout, continuous free space, conservative occupancy, observation targets and saved visibility results. Six construction families can be realized as orthogonal, polygonal or polyhedral, and curvilinear worlds in two or three dimensions. Fixed values, uniform ranges and supported probability laws control the exposed generation parameters. 24 Requested settings, realized draws, fitting transforms and accepted attempts remain inspectable in the saved artifacts. Continuous geometry and discrete occupancy describe the same generated world at different levels of resolution. Whole-cell or whole-voxel certificates protect the free grid from admitting wall-crossing cells. Continuous sensing uses sampled target coordinates; current discrete sensing uses every free cell or voxel center. Saved query inputs identify the wall representation, target positions, observers and numerical conventions needed to replay a visibility result. The timing experiment in Section 5 measures complete generation calls for six configurations. Five timed repeats and an additional fresh-process replay per configuration reproduce the geometry, grids and construction records exactly in the recorded environment. Generation times vary even when the resulting world is identical. Source hashes, configurations and dependency versions are therefore retained alongside measured times. The restored atlas and comparisons show how wall geometry, obstacles, observer placement, grid resolution and physical scale affect the representations and measured visibility. These examples preserve their recorded target sets. Target fractions from different sampling conventions or different worlds require their denominators and coordinates to be considered explicitly. Appendix A explains the quadtree and octree measure of world complexity, then uses POCGAGP as an example with illustrative worlds and prior experimental evidence. The generated environments and replayable sensing inputs support online algorithms that choose observations and retain the information accumulated during an experiment.

## A Complexity of worlds 

World complexity depends on what is being measured. A larger open room can require more travel or more sensing positions under a finite sensor range. More corners can increase the number of boundary elements processed by a geometric algorithm. These descriptors can therefore be relevant in some settings, but neither determines difficulty for every task. We use the size of a region quadtree in 2D or a region octree in 3D as a measure of spatial detail at a declared resolution [3, 1]. The hierarchy records how much subdivision is needed to represent free and blocked space. It gives a reproducible ranking under a common domain, pitch, root and orientation convention. Its relationship to task outcomes is evaluated separately: Section A.3 uses POCGAGP as an example, with Figure 26 illustrating why physical size and corner count alone are insufficient for its visibility component. 25 A.1 Constructing the hierarchy 

Start with one square containing the 2D occupancy mask, or one cube containing the 3D mask. If all entries in a region are free, that region becomes a free leaf. If all are blocked, it becomes a blocked leaf. Otherwise, split the region into four equal squares in 2D or eight equal cubes in 3D, and repeat. Subdivision stops when each retained region is homogeneous; an individual finest-grid cell or voxel is always homogeneous. This hierarchy preserves the saved Boolean mask exactly. Large uniform regions need few nodes. Boundaries, small obstacles and narrow passages can require finer subdivision and therefore more nodes. The classification uses the conservative occupancy defined in Section 2: a blocked entry can include a cell that is partly free in continuous space. The hierarchy ranks this discrete representation of the world. 

A.2 The score and ranking rule 

Let I be the number of subdivided internal nodes and L the number of leaves. Count every retained node, including free leaves, blocked leaves and internal nodes. The complexity score is 

Ch(W) = T = I + L =

where h is the finest cell or voxel pitch. These identities hold because each subdivision retains all four or all eight children. A homogeneous root has score 1; one split into homogeneous children gives score 5 in 2D or 9 in 3D. Sort worlds by increasing Ch, from the least to the most complex. Equal scores receive the same rank. For two comparable worlds, 

Wa ⪯ W b ⇐⇒ Ch(Wa) ≤ Ch(Wb).

Here, ⪯ means “has no greater spatial complexity under this representation.” Use the same dimension, physical domain, pitch, root placement and orientation, and padding convention for the comparison. For arrays that do not fit a dyadic square or cube, our implementation pads only the high-index sides with blocked entries to the smallest enclosing power-of-two square or cube. Padding and resolution affect the score, so 2D and 3D worlds are ranked separately.

A.5 Construction of the illustrative worlds 

Figure 26 uses three deterministic polygons constructed for this comparison, rather than random generator outputs. All three use a 64 × 64 root, origin (0 , 0), pitch h = 0 .5, whole-square containment and the same quadtree counting rule. Thus the smaller maze’s higher score is measured under the same representation convention as the larger room’s lower score. The large room is the square [8 , 56] × [8 , 56]. The small maze starts with [8 , 28] × [8 , 28] and removes five alternating wall strips of width 1.5, leaving gaps of width 3.5 at the top or bottom. Its smaller free area (276 .25 < 2304) and smaller bounding box coexist with a larger tree (717 > 69). The stepped room has 16 treads of width 2, with successive heights 9 through 24, translated by (8 , 8). Its boundary has 34 corners, more than the maze’s 24, yet one observer at (39 , 8.5) sees its entire interior with unlimited omnidirectional line of sight. To see why, use local coordinates relative to (8 , 8), so the observer is (31 , 0.5). For a point to its left and above it, the sight segment moves rightward and downward toward the observer, while the stepped ceiling never decreases. Points below the observer remain below the lowest ceiling. Points to its right share the final tread. In every case the segment stays inside the room. This establishes visibility of the whole interior, independently of target sampling. For numerical replay, targets are all free-cell centers, checked against continuous polygon walls. The Rust results agree with independent segment containment checks for every target: the large room and stepped room have 100% target coverage; the selected maze observer has 16.6%. Aseparate triangle-fan check verifies whole-region visibility for the large and stepped rooms. These percentages refer to the stated observer positions; no optimal observer count is inferred for the 29 maze. A testable volumetric application 

The measured octrees establish a reproducible spatial descriptor for the new 3D worlds. Testing its relationship to connected deployment requires a 3D motion and sensing model, declared deployment starts, policy implementations and termination conditions. Vertical motion, shafts, sensor directionality and connectivity across elevations can introduce effects beyond the node count. Use a common physical pitch and root convention, retain physical extent and geometry as covariates, and compare completion steps and agent populations separately. Use the ranking convention in Section A.2. Keep repeated starts of a world together when estimating uncertainty or testing on held-out worlds. The 2D result motivates this experiment; a 3D deployment relationship has not been measured here. 31
