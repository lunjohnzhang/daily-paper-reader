# Failure-Resilient Multi-Robot Coordination with k-Connectivity Constrained Submodular Maximization 

Yoonjae Hwang 1, Hojune Kim 1, Ellen Novoseller 2, Guangyao Shi 1,and Gaurav S. Sukhatme 1

Abstract — Multi-robot coordination is essential in applica-tions such as coverage, target tracking, and information gath-ering, where robots must jointly select actions to maximize team performance. Submodular maximization provides an effective framework for such coordination, and resilient variants further account for robot failures or adversarial removals. However, existing methods typically overlook the impact of robot loss on the communication network, which may disconnect the surviving team and prevent continued coordination. To address this issue, we introduce Resilient k-Connected Submodular Maximization, which jointly considers task performance, re-silience, and communication connectivity. The resulting feasible family is not a matroid, preventing a direct application of clas-sical curvature-greedy guarantees. We overcome this challenge through a Menger-based connectivity reduction, a connectivity-aware greedy algorithm with a feasibility-preserving fallback, and a new curvature-based analysis that captures the cost of enforcing connectivity through a single factor ρ. Our method achieves a worst-case post-failure objective within a factor [max(1 − νf (I), h (N, β ))] 2ρ 1

(1 − e−κf ) of the optimal connectivity-respecting resilient assignment, while recovering the classical unconstrained guarantee in the appropriate lim-its. Experiments on multi-robot target tracking validate the proposed method and characterize the practical impact of connectivity constraints. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Multi-robot systems provide scalable solutions to appli-cations such as environmental monitoring, target tracking, search and rescue, and collaborative exploration [1]–[4]. In many of these settings, the robots must coordinate their sensing or motion decisions to maximize a common team objective [5]. Submodular set functions provide a natural mathematical framework for such coordination by capturing the principle of diminishing returns: the marginal benefit of adding an additional robot decreases as more robots are already selected. Many commonly used objectives, including mutual information [6], area coverage [7], number of ob-served targets [8], and sensor-measurement entropy [9] are monotone submodular [10]. This structure enables compu-tationally efficient greedy algorithms with well-established constant-factor performance guarantees, as reviewed in Sec-tion II. Classical submodular maximization, however, typically assumes that the selected robots remain available throughout execution. In practice, robots may be lost due to hardware

This has motivated resilient submodular maximization, where the team is selected so that the objective remains large even after a bounded number of robots are removed [8], [11], [12]. Existing resilient formu-lations primarily focus on preserving submodular objective performance after such removals. Yet, critically, robot loss can disrupt the communication network among surviving robots. A team might therefore retain high sensing or cover-age capacity after failures while becoming disconnected and rendered unable to continue coordinated operation. Communication connectivity and resilience have both been studied extensively, but largely as separate requirements. In this work, we consider them jointly and ask how to select robot actions so that the team achieves a high submodular objective while remaining connected even after adversar-ial robot removals. We refer to this problem as Resilient 

k-Connected Submodular Maximization (see Fig. 1). The joint treatment is substantially more challenging than simply combining two existing constraints. Resilience requires ac-counting for the worst-case subset of removed robots, while connectivity is a global property whose feasibility depends on which robots survive. This interaction also breaks key structures used in existing approximation analysis. In the resilient maximization framework of Tzoumas et al. [11], robustness is achieved by constructing a sacrificial “bait” set of β high-value robots together with a greedily selected remainder. Under a connectivity requirement, however, the arXiv:2610.13049v1 [cs.RO] 9 Oct 2026 adversary determines which robots are removed, and the worst-case removal need not coincide with the bait set. Consequently, enforcing connectivity only on the non-bait robots does not guarantee that the surviving team remains connected. Moreover, the constraint of selecting one tra-jectory per robot together with a global vertex-connectivity requirement does not form a matroid. As a result, classical curvature-based greedy guarantees cannot be applied directly and must be re-established for the connectivity-constrained resilient setting. We make the following contributions:  

> •

We formulate resilient submodular maximization under a (β+1) -vertex-connectivity constraint and show that the family of partial, connectivity-feasible selections is not a matroid (Proposition 1), identifying precisely why the classical greedy analysis fails to transfer.  

> •

We introduce a connectivity filter with a rigid-shift fallback across both bait and greedy stages, show it guarantees a feasible, (β+1) -connected output that sur-vives any removal of size at most β regardless of com-position (Proposition 2), and re-derive the curvature-greedy bound against a connectivity-respecting bench-mark (Lemma 1), isolating the filter’s entire cost into a single interpretable constant ρ ∈ [0 , 1] . 

> •

B. Communication and Failure Model 

Let P (s) denote the endpoint of trajectory s, and for a selection S let P (S) = {P (s) : s ∈ S} be the joint config-uration. Two robots communicate when their endpoints lie within range rc. We define the proximity graph G(P (S)) = (R, E ), where (i, j ) ∈ E ⇐⇒ ∥P (si) − P (sj )∥ ≤ rc.Recall that the vertex connectivity κ(G) of a graph G is the size of the smallest set of vertices whose removal disconnects 

G. By Menger’s theorem [22], κ(G) is the largest k such that every pair of vertices is joined by k internally vertex-disjoint paths. In our setting, each vertex is a robot, so κ(G) is exactly the minimum number of robot failures that can disconnect the network. We assume that in any deployment, at most β robots may fail or be attacked [8], [11]. As in prior work, the identities of the failed robots are unknown at the time of trajectory selection, and a failure removes the affected robot’s trajectory contribution. It also removes the corresponding vertex of our communication graph G. Because every robot acts, the number of selected trajectories α := |S| = N , and the number of greedily selected “resilient” robots is at least 

α − β = N − β.

A. The Algorithm 

Algorithm 1 (RES-CON) states the procedure precisely. 

a) Stage S1 (bait A1): The robots are sorted in non-increasing order of their maximum single-trajectory cover-age. The top β robots are then processed, each assigned its highest-coverage trajectory subject to keeping the running shadow configuration (β+1) -connected (line 5), with the stay-in-place fallback (i.e., returning the robot to its As-sumption 1 initial endpoint P0(r)) invoked when no such trajectory exists (line 7; see Proposition 2). Ordering by standalone rather than marginal value may assign overlap-ping bait trajectories, but this affects neither feasibility nor Theorem 1 as the redundancy is priced into the guarantee through νf (I) → 1. Because the fallback leaves the shadow configuration unchanged at that robot’s coordinate, it trivially preserves whatever connectivity level the configuration held prior to the attempted commit. 

b) Stage S2 (resilient set A2): Each of the remaining 

α − β robots greedily selects the connectivity-feasible trajec-tory of maximal marginal gain (line 13), executing the rigid fallback when no feasible candidate exists (line 15). 

c) Output: ARES = A1 ∪ A2 (line 19).

At and above threshold, the tracking curves of all methods coincide, consistent with the connectivity constraint being inactive there (Section V-B). VI. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We introduced resilient submodular maximization under a (β+1) -vertex-connectivity constraint, jointly capturing ad-versarial robustness and communication network integrity for multi-robot teams. Several directions remain open. First, the squared resilience factor is established under the intra-stage hypothesis of Proposition 3 and corroborated empirically. A proof that this hypothesis holds unconditionally for disk-graph geometries under bounded motion would bolster the idea. Second, deriving a geometric lower bound for ρ would convert the penalty into a computable quantity, making (9) a fully unconditional guarantee on non-degenerate instances. Finally, a hardware demonstration on an aerial or ground fleet would validate the connectivity guarantee under real communication models. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS
