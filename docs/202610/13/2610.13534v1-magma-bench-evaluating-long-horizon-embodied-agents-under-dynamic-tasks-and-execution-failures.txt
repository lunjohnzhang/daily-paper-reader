Abstract 

Long-horizon embodied agents must maintain task-relevant information, coordinate heterogeneous robots, and adapt their decisions as execution unfolds. Existing benchmarks investigate dynamic task execution and embodied multi-agent coordination, but provide limited evaluation of their intersection. We introduce MAGMA-Bench, an architecture-agnostic benchmark for persistent task reasoning in dynamic multi-robot environments. MAGMA-Bench evaluates agents across increasing task horizons and controlled online perturbations, including execution failures, task interruptions, and mission updates, with scenarios requiring coordination across robots with heterogeneous capabilities. It uses a structured skill-level interface to separate task-level reasoning from perception and low-level control. We evaluate locally deployable open-weight planners, multi-agent state-tracking strategies, and frontier models. Even the strongest evaluated models fail on a substantial fraction of clean episodes, and their success rates decrease further under online perturbations. Supervised adaptation greatly improves a small model, but performance remains substantially lower on longer and multi-robot missions. We release the benchmark, 261,646 task-level training examples, and an architecture-agnostic expert-trace generation pipeline. *

1 Introduction 

Autonomous agents increasingly use language models to inter-pret instructions and select actions over extended interactions. In embodied missions, a task-level system may coordinate robots with different skills while both the instructions and the physical world change. An initial request may be incomplete, requiring information from an earlier interaction or clarifica-tion from the user. Later, a grasp may fail while another robot remains active, or a new goal may invalidate work that has not yet been completed. Every subsequent decision depends on what remains unknown, what has already been achieved, and which robot can act next. The agent must therefore maintain

An agent must therefore track evolving goals, constraints, and execution progress across multiple robots, preserving valid commitments while revising those that are no longer applica-ble. Evaluating this capability requires long-horizon episodes with controlled task and execution perturbations, rather than isolated planning or replanning decisions. We introduce MAGMA-Bench , which instantiates this setting in 12 scenario families with one to three robots. A single decision may dispatch skills to multiple robots and their outcomes update a shared scene while other actions remain in progress. The interface leaves the policy architecture unspecified. The benchmark varies nominal horizon, online task and execution perturbations, and generalization from new in-domain instances to novel compositions and a held-out scenario family. We evaluate locally deployable language-model planners, state-tracking strategies, and task-specific adaptation, and compare selected models with frontier systems on a common 215-episode subset. In summary, our primary contribution is an architecture-agnostic, skill-level benchmark for persistent task reasoning in single- and multi-robot missions under controlled online perturbations. We establish the benchmark’s utility through an empirical analysis of task horizon, state tracking, supervised adaptation, perturbation robustness, generalization, and multi-robot coordination, including frontier-model comparisons on a common subset. Additionally, we release a dataset of 261,646 task-level training examples and an expert-trace generation pipeline that can be projected into architecture-specific training formats, to facilitate extension and reproducibility. 

2 Related Work 

Memory and dynamic task execution in language agents. 

Several benchmarks establish the need to act on information that persists across decisions. ToolSandbox [12] tests tool use with stateful dependencies, and 𝜏 -bench [13] evaluates interactions with state-changing APIs under domain poli-cies. MemoryAgentBench and MemoryArena [1, 2] focus on retaining and using information over extended interactions. Multi-Mission Tool Bench, InterruptBench, and Gaia2 [3–5] move closer to our setting by introducing mission switches, user revisions, or dynamic environments during execution. These works make persistent task state and online adapta-tion measurable in digital settings. MAGMA-Bench instead asks how these demands change when actions have physical consequences, skill execution is stochastic, and the available capabilities depend on the selected robot. 

Long-horizon reasoning in embodied agents. 

VLABench [8] evaluates language-conditioned manipulation across diverse multi-step tasks, while RoboMME [7] targets temporal, spatial, object, and procedural memory in robotic policies. RoboCerebra [9] combines planning, reflection, and memory with adaptation to unexpected execution condi-tions. These benchmarks retain visual perception and motor execution in the evaluated loop. PARTNR [6] is closer to task-level coordination: it exposes high-level skills and eval-uates planning under spatial, temporal, and heterogeneous human–robot capability constraints, including task tracking and recovery. Recent work such as CoMuRoS [14] further demonstrates event-driven replanning in heterogeneous robot teams, including responses to execution failures and changing user intentions. However, its systematic replanning evaluation focuses on the correctness of plans revised after individual events, rather than task completion across extended interactive episodes with controlled perturbations. MAGMA-Bench fo-cuses on whether ongoing work remains coherent when goals, rules, or robot assignments are revised after execution begins, while skills may fail and observations may be incomplete. 

Positioning of MAGMA-Bench. 

These works establish complementary challenges: agentic benchmarks examine evolving user intentions and persis-tent interactions, while embodied benchmarks study physical constraints, heterogeneous capabilities, and execution recov-ery. MAGMA-Bench brings these dimensions together in a controlled, skill-level evaluation of long-horizon multi-robot missions. Unlike evaluations centered on fixed task specifi-cations or isolated replanning events, it measures whether an agent can maintain coherent task execution as goals, rules, and execution conditions evolve. By separating task-level decisions from perception and low-level control, the bench-mark enables direct comparison of reasoning architectures and models under reproducible perturbations. 4 Persistent Task Reasoning Benchmark 

To measure persistent task reasoning in robotics, MAGMA-Bench evaluates whether an agent can retain, revise, and apply task-relevant information across extended and stochastic executions. The benchmark systematically varies three com-plementary dimensions: execution horizon , from short to extreme missions; online perturbations , including mission updates, execution failures, and goal interruptions; and gener-alization , from new in-domain instances to novel compositions and a fully held-out scenario family. 

4.1 Task-Level Evaluation Setting 

An episode combines natural-language instructions, a set of available robotic skills, and observations returned during execution. A policy acts at the task level by invoking skills through a common API. Skill execution and perception may be perturbed, and their outcomes are returned to the policy through 𝑞 𝑡 . At each decision, the policy either selects skill calls 𝑎 𝑡 

> m

, issues a textual response 𝑎 𝑡  

> u

to report completion or clarify an unresolved requirement, or waits for further events. Agents interact with the environment exclusively through exposed robotic skills, including perception primitives and physical manipulation or navigation actions. Each skill is grounded in the simulated environment: action skills modify the physical scene through motion-planned robot execution using mplib [15], whereas perception skills return obser-vations derived from the current scene. The benchmark thereby preserves a closed-loop coupling between decisions, physical execution, and subsequent observations. We use ManiSkill3 [16] as the simulator. Tasks include direct instructions (“Do X”), ordered com-binations (“Do X, then Y”), persistent rules (“Remember that A must be stored in B by default”), rule updates, and under-specified requests whose missing assignment must be inferred from an earlier rule. They also include embodied questions, such as “How many X are in storage?”, and interruptions (“Stop X, do Y, and continue X later”) or (“Do Y when you are done”). Action stages are evaluated through executable simulator-state predicates and, when applicable, constraints over the ordered action log. 4.2 Robotic Interaction Model 

This abstraction makes the task-level policy independent of a particular robot controller while retaining characteristic sources of uncertainty from robotic deployment. Perception is imperfect: the detection pipeline may miss objects, yielding a partial observation. Physical skills are also stochastic: motion-planned trajectories can be perturbed to induce failures such as unsuccessful grasps or placements. These perturbations affect the underlying environment rather than only the textual feedback. A failed grasp leaves the object unmoved, whereas an undetected object remains physically present and may become observable later. Agents must therefore reason about the consequences of physical execution across subsequent decisions. Most skills correspond to physical actions, e.g., take ,

put , clean , or press . Other skills support observation and execution management: 

• detect and inspect return structured information about the current scene. They abstract the outputs of task-specific perception systems, such as segmentation mod-els or VLMs. 

• cancel stops an ongoing skill so that the agent can revise a robot’s assignment or prioritize another objective. It is the only skill accepted while that robot is running .

4.3 Benchmark Composition Tasks. 

Table 2 summarizes the 12 scenario families, which involve one to three robots. Each scenario defines a physical environment and the skills available to each robot; these skill sets may differ across robots. In multi-robot scenarios, skills execute asynchronously, so an interruption or task update may affect one robot without invalidating the ongoing work of the others. 

Variants. 

The scenarios contain 99 nominal task specifications. We derive controlled variants from each specification, sharing its initial environment configuration and task context: 

• Clean (CE): Reference condition without online pertur-bations. Instructions may still require clarification. 

• Mission Update (MU): Assignments, rules, or user preferences change after execution begins. 

• Stochastic Execution (SE): Skills may fail and percep-tion can be incomplete. 

• Interruption (IN): Goals must be suspended, repriori-tized, and resumed. 

• Combined (CO): Multiple applicable perturbation fam-ilies occur within the same episode. Each specification has between two and five conditions, in-cluding CE, and each condition is instantiated with three semantically equivalent language variations. These varia-tions change object, robot, and skill names or descriptions while preserving the environment configuration, task seman-tics, success criteria, and nominal skill requirements. The resulting evaluation set contains 1,254 episodes. For paired evaluation, an execution-perturbation variant shares the clean episode’s initial state, stage sequence, instructions, and seman-tic mapping: it differs only through frozen, stage-scoped error specifications. This fixes the scheduled perturbations across policies, but not their realized exposure: a fault is triggered only if the policy reaches the targeted stage and invokes a compatible skill. 

Horizon. 

We define nominal task horizon as the number of successful high-level skill executions required by the nominal expert solution. This quantity measures the amount of task-level work required to complete a task independently of how an agent schedules it. We consider short (10–15), medium (16– 30), long (31–45), very long (46–65), and extreme (66–90) horizons. In single-robot tasks, the nominal horizon generally coincides with the number of skill-dispatch decisions. In multi-robot tasks, however, one agent decision may dispatch skills to several robots concurrently. Online perturbations, failures, or interruptions may increase the number of executed 4skills and decisions beyond the nominal horizon. Horizon is always estimated from the clean variant. 

Generalization regimes. 

Beyond new task instances, the benchmark distinguishes three regimes relative to the released training distribution for fine-tuned models: 

• In-domain (ID) tasks use scenario-specific task struc-tures represented during training, while changing the concrete objects, goals, and episode configuration. 

• Compositional generalization (CG) tasks recombine task mechanisms that are individually observed during training but never instantiated in that combination within the corresponding scenario family. For example, a scenario may be evaluated with an assignment rule observed during training only in another scenario. 

• Held-out (HO) evaluation uses the Packaging scenario family, which is entirely excluded from training. This separation allows us to distinguish robustness to un-seen instances from transfer through recombination and transfer to an unseen task family. 

4.4 Released Dataset 

Alongside the benchmark, we release a supervised training dataset and the off-policy procedure used to generate it. Train-ing and evaluation are produced by separate pipelines from distinct seeds and independently constructed task configura-tions. The two sets deliberately share scenario ontologies, object and skill vocabularies, and individual request primitives. The resulting generalization claim therefore concerns new task instances and compositions rather than lexical or embodiment transfer. Each sample represents the next task-level decision condi-tioned on the current interaction state and can be rendered in the format required by a particular model or architecture. The generation procedure is architecture-agnostic and supports centralized policies as well as multi-agent architectures. For each task, state-valid user requests and their parameters are sampled from the current symbolic state, and scenario-specific programs synthesize an expert trace from the corresponding state transition. The generation procedure keeps sampling requests until the number of decisions required to complete the task exceeds 40. Targets are therefore programmatic rather than teacher-LLM labels. The traces are projected into next-decision SFT rows, with two augmented variants per source row produced through instruction paraphrasing and consistent renaming of entities, robots, tools, and parameters. A grouped 95/5 split keeps all decisions and augmentations derived from the same task definition, arguments, and seed in a single partition. The final export contains 261,646 training rows and 13,574 validation rows across the 11 training scenario families. The released training data include interruptions and mis-sion updates, but no stochastic skill failures or partial ob-servations. Robustness to execution failures and incomplete observations is therefore evaluated without direct supervision from recovery trajectories.

Our first hypothesis is that performance depends on the agent’s capacity to maintain an accurate estimate 𝑚 𝑡 of 𝑧 𝑡 (Equa-tion (5)). We then compare three dual-agent variants built from two zero-shot gpt-oss-20b instances: a state manager produces a represen-tation for an action dispatcher, whose prompt matches the single-model policy except for instructions describing that representation. • reflection : a free-form paragraph produced at every step; 

• explicit : a structured state maintained through oper-ations such as adding facts and adding or removing goals;

5.4 VQ4: Does MAGMA-Bench Expose Multi-Robot Coordination Failures? 

Multi-robot scenarios require a policy to maintain several con-current commitments while robot availability, assignments, and goals evolve asynchronously. For this comparison, we run additional experiments with GPT-6.1-SOL to obtain compara-ble numbers of single- and multi-robot episodes and compare its performance with that of Qwen3-4B-FT and gpt-oss-20b. All three models are evaluated using the same linguistic re-alization: 34 single-robot clean episodes from the common subset and all 35 multi-robot clean specifications. Table 4 shows a pronounced model-dependent contrast. On clean episodes, Qwen3-4B-FT decreases from 67.7% SR on single-robot tasks to 11.4% on multi-robot tasks, whereas GPT-6.1-SOL remains nearly unchanged (73.5% versus 74.3%). GPT-OSS:20B also decreases, although its low single-robot SR makes the contrast less informative. Equal weighting over the five horizon buckets leaves a −57 .2 point multi-minus-single gap for Qwen3-4B-FT, compared with +1.4 points for GPT-6.1-SOL. Strong single-robot performance therefore does not uniformly transfer to the benchmark’s concurrent multi-robot tasks. A deterministic trace audit of multi-robot episodes, in-cluding variants, provides more direct evidence about the associated coordination errors. Qwen3-4B-FT makes state-dependent assignment errors on 2.04% of evaluable calls, compared with 0.09% for GPT-6.1-SOL, and incorrectly re-assigns or cancels robots in 22 of 27 evaluable interruption decisions. None of Qwen’s 12 evaluable resume obligations reaches successful completion, whereas GPT-6.1-SOL suc-ceeds on 12 of 21. Although the scenario populations differ, the benchmark reveals a substantial separation between policies with com-parable single-robot success rates. The trace audit further identifies assignment and resumption errors within the multi-robot regime. 

6 Limitations 

MAGMA-Bench evaluates task-level decisions through struc-tured observations and skill calls. It does not measure visual recognition or low-level motor control, and its results do not directly characterize end-to-end VLM or VLA policies. The evaluated persistence concerns goals, rules, assignments, and execution progress rather than spatial scene understanding. Also, the frontier-model subset contains a single semantic realization and is a cost-controlled coverage set rather than a statistical sample of the full benchmark. The paper focuses on presenting the benchmark and char-acterizing centralized language-model policies, selected state-tracking strategies, online perturbations, and multi-robot co-ordination. Although the interface also supports symbolic planners and decentralized architectures, identifying the best architecture for these tasks is outside the scope of this paper. 

7 Conclusion 

MAGMA-Bench evaluates whether robotic agents preserve and revise task commitments across long, asynchronous ex-ecutions. The results show that task-specific supervision can substantially improve a small planner, while leaving pronounced weaknesses in multi-robot assignment and task resumption. Frontier models retain substantial headroom and exhibit different sensitivities to mission updates, interrup-tions, and execution failures. In particular, strong single-robot task performance does not necessarily extend to maintaining concurrent commitments under online task revisions. These findings motivate evaluating persistent task reasoning through both task outcomes and trace-level diagnostics of evolving commitments. We release the benchmark, training data, and expert-trace generation pipeline to support reproducible progress in this setting. 8References
