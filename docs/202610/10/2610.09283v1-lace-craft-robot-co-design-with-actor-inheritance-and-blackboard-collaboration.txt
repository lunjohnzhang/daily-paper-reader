Abstract— Robot co-design couples morphology search with
policy learning, yet training every new design from scratch dis-
cards acquired control experience. We present LACE–CRAFT,
which compares continued learning on the current robot with
policy adaptation to new morphology–reward pairs. LACE
resumes the incumbent’s full learning state and initializes com-
patible challengers with its actor parameters and observation
statistics. A fixed task metric selects among both branches
and the frozen incumbent. CRAFT coordinates Feedback,
Morphology, Reward, and Integration roles through shared
experimental records and behavioral replays to generate and
cross-review paired proposals. A generative extension converts
generated meshes into editable articulated models with con-
figured joints, actuator interfaces, and consistently updated
simulation assets. Across five locomotion benchmarks, mean
scores over three evaluation seeds are 6.4–91.9% higher than
D2C. Both methods train 30 new morphology–reward pairs over
five rounds; LACE additionally uses four continuation training
units. Five-task ablations examine policy inheritance and replay-
derived feedback. A fabricated prototype demonstrates indoor
walking and illustrates the geometry-to-hardware workflow.
I. Introduction
Robot morphology determines the dynamics a controller
must exploit, while the training reward shapes the behavior
it learns. Co-design therefore couples body design, reward

design, and policy optimization [1], [2]. Large language
models (LLMs) provide flexible operators for this search:
Eureka generates executable rewards from training feed-
back [3], and Debate2Create (D2C) uses multi-agent debate
to refine morphology–reward pairs before simulation-based
evaluation [4].
The search must also decide how to use the control
experience acquired during candidate evaluation. In our D2C
reproduction, design records guide subsequent proposals,
while each new policy is trained from scratch. Prior co-
design methods already reuse experience or transfer con-
troller parameters across morphologies [1], [5]. We focus
on how to select between further training the current design
and adapting its policy to a new body and reward. A
learned policy can provide a useful initialization after a
design change, whereas value estimates and stored transitions
depend on the dynamics and reward under which they were
obtained.
LACE–CRAFT addresses this decision through two com-
peting training branches (Fig. 1). LACE initializes new
designs with the incumbent’s actor parameters and obser-
vation statistics, while resetting other learner components.
A continuation branch resumes the incumbent’s complete
learning state on its unchanged morphology and reward. A
fixed external task metric compares both branches with the

frozen incumbent, allowing search to select either further
learning on the current design or adaptation to a new design.
CRAFT uses the resulting experimental evidence to guide
subsequent proposals. Its shared blackboard links tested
morphology–reward pairs to checkpoints, scores, and be-
havioral replays. Feedback proposes diagnoses and improve-
ments; Morphology and Reward independently draft and then
cross-review changes; Integration constructs complete paired
candidates. New evaluations update the blackboard for the
next round, preserving the association between each result
and the robot that produced it.
We further extend the morphology representation from pa-
rameterized benchmark bodies to generated meshes. Follow-
ing the geometry-to-hardware approach of Text2Robot [6],
we process generated shapes into editable links, configured
joints, and actuator interfaces. Geometry edits update the
articulated model and its simulation assets while preserving
LACE’s policy interface. Alongside the five-task benchmark
study, we document this processing workflow and a fabri-
cated walking prototype. Our contributions are:
• Continuation-aware co-design. LACE compares full-
state continuation of the incumbent with actor-initialized
morphology–reward challengers under a fixed task metric,
while retaining the incumbent as a selection option.
• Evidence-informed proposal generation. CRAFT coor-
dinates four specialist roles through shared experiment
records, diagnostic feedback, reciprocal morphology–
reward review, and integration.
• A generative morphology extension. Editable links,
configured joints, and actuator interfaces connect gener-
ated geometry to the co-design representation; processing
records and a walking prototype illustrate its use.

B. LACE: Learning-State-Aware Continuation Evolution
After initialization of y0, LACE runs for at most K
iterations, indexed by k = 0, . . . , K −1. Each iteration
trains one continuation candidate and Nch design challengers.
Subscripts identify the current state’s components in (1);
superscripts cont and ch identify the two branches. Thus
K counts post-initialization iterations, not the initialization
round.
a) Transfer boundary: Within each task, morphology
edits preserve kinematic connectivity, actuated-joint order-
ing, and the dimensions and meanings of observations and
actions. Direct actor inheritance also requires the same
policy-network architecture; matching vector dimensions
alone does not establish compatibility. Geometry and training
rewards may change within this interface. Topology changes
and changes to observation or action semantics require a
different transfer mechanism. Full-state continuation retains
the morphology, reward, and learner configuration.

D. Extension to Generative Robots
The generative extension constructs an articulated mor-
phology from a shape reference c and user-specified hardware
requirements, following the geometry-to-hardware approach
of Text2Robot [6]. For an arm-equipped quadruped, the
reference image guides body shape and the arm requirement
specifies a torso mounting interface. Scale, orientation, and
joint topology are configured alongside hardware η, which
specifies motor dimensions, torque and speed limits, joint
limits, control frequency, and payload. Hyper3D Rodin [46]
generates a static mesh from c; the model components in
Fig. 2 provide articulation and hardware interfaces. Task τ
defines the evaluation objective.
a) Part decomposition and articulation: BANG [37]
decomposes the Rodin mesh into components. Scripts repair,
merge, and scale them into body and limb meshes {gi}n
i=1,
preserving part identifiers. Alignment scripts place the links
in a common body frame, and an assembly template assigns
them to a configured kinematic tree Gm = (V, E) [11]. Each
link stores its mesh, mass, center of mass, and inertia; each
joint stores its type, parent-relative pose, axis, and limits
under URDF conventions [47]. The illustrated geometry
model has two actuated joints per leg. R17 denotes the
hardware-adapted quadruped version used in the walking
demonstration, with three actuated joints per leg.
b) Actuator interfaces and export: Scripts cut hip and knee
motor cavities into aligned meshes using mesh Boolean
operations and configured motor dimensions. Clearances
separate mating surfaces; paired slot faces determine joint
origins. The interface record Σ stores motor poses, cavities,
and mating surfaces for actuated joints. Screw holes and
fastening details are designed manually. The torso (base link)
incorporates an SO-101 mounting interface [48]; the arm
links, motors, and gripper follow the existing SO-101 design.
Export scripts construct the morphology package
mgen = (Gm, Σ, Φ),
Φ = (URDF, Ccoll, Pinert, L, Γ).
(22)
Here Ccoll and Pinert contain collision and inertial assets
computed from the processed geometry and configured ma-
terial and motor masses. The fields L and Γ specify joint
and actuator limits, ordered action-to-joint mapping, action
scaling, and control frequency. The training adapter loads
this package into Isaac Gym for locomotion learning. In this
backend, reward revisions update the task configuration’s re-
ward terms and parameters, which the PyTorch environment
evaluates during training. For the arm-equipped robot, Γ
maps policy actions to the leg joints, while prescribed arm
postures define the configurations under which walking is
trained. Arm joints remain fixed during locomotion training.
c) Consistent morphology updates: Morphology pro-
posals can modify link dimensions and associated joint
and mounting fields. Geometry scripts apply these edits,
refit affected joint origins and motor slots, and regenerate
collision and inertial assets, keeping the assembled robot
model consistent with its simulation assets. Hardware η,
kinematic connectivity, and the policy interface remain fixed,
preserving compatibility with LACE’s transfer rule. Fasten-
ing details require renewed manual checking after geometry
changes.
IV. Experiments
A. Experimental Setup
We evaluate on Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Swimmer,
and Walker2D using the D2C benchmark settings [4], [49].
Methods share each task’s morphology and policy interfaces,
learner, per-design training budget, horizon, and evaluation


metric. Morphology search changes link geometry within a
fixed kinematic tree. D2C is the primary co-design baseline;
Default trains the original morphology and hand-designed
reward from scratch.
D2C, LACE–CRAFT, and the ablation variants use GPT–
5.6-sol and propose six morphology–reward pairs per round
for five rounds, including initialization. Each search therefore
generates 30 new designs. LACE additionally trains one
continuation candidate in each of the four later rounds, giving
34 training units compared with D2C’s 30.
Final performance is evaluated with seeds 0, 1, 2, each
using 128 parallel episodes, the task horizon, and the
metric in (4). We report the mean and sample standard
deviation of the three seed-level mean scores. Morphol-
ogy, policy parameters, and observation statistics remain
frozen; actions are sampled from the learned policy. D2C
returns a morphology–reward pair, from which a policy is
trained with the task-specific budget; LACE–CRAFT returns
a morphology–policy pair. These tests measure fixed-policy
evaluation variability, not variation across training seeds or
independent searches.

V. Limitations and Future Work
a) Interpreting replay evidence: Behavioral replays can
support multiple explanations, so task measurements should
corroborate proposed diagnoses. The three evaluation seeds
quantify rollout variability of fixed policies; they do not
quantify variation across complete searches or training runs,
or disentangle errors in evidence interpretation from variation
in proposal generation.
b) Fabrication and hardware integration: Fabricating
the platform requires manual screw-hole and fastening design
(Fig. 5). The printed parts underwent no structural simula-
tion, and thin connections were prone to fracture. Structural
analysis under expected loads and durability testing are
needed to improve mechanical reliability.
c) Closing the sim-to-real loop: Differences between
the simulated model and fabricated robot remain a challenge
for policy transfer. Incorporating hardware video and mea-
sured joint motions into CRAFT’s shared blackboard would
allow subsequent design revisions to draw on both simulation
and physical execution.
VI. Conclusion
LACE–CRAFT combines policy reuse with morphology–
reward search guided by behavioral evidence. LACE com-
pares adapted challengers, full-state continuation, and incum-
bent retention under a fixed task metric. CRAFT coordinates
proposals and cross-review through shared experimental
records and replays. The selected LACE–CRAFT policies
achieve higher three-seed evaluation means than D2C on all
five tasks under the stated protocol. The generative extension
connects editable geometry to articulated models and simu-
lation assets; the fabricated prototype demonstrates indoor
walking with the arm removed. Future work will address
diagnostic reliability, mechanical validation, and quantitative
sim-to-real evaluation.

Training-State Transfer
A. Continuation, inheritance, and incumbent retention
LACE carries three distinct states into candidate com-
parison. The incumbent retains its morphology, reward,
checkpoint, and recorded evaluation score without further
training. Continuation restores the complete learner on that
morphology and reward. A design challenger initializes a
new learner from the incumbent’s Actor and observation
statistics, then trains on the proposed morphology–reward
pair. Table S1 lists the component-level operations.

This rule makes retained evaluation scores nondecreasing

can still vary.
B. PPO continuation state
The serialized PPO state is represented by
zPPO = (θπ, θV , ν, o, ce, ξ).
(A.1)
Here θπ and θV
are Actor and value-network parame-
ters, ν contains optimizer variables, o contains observation-
normalization statistics, and ce counts environment tran-
sitions. The collection state ξ contains the wrapped en-
vironment state and the random-generator states used by
learning, rollout collection, and evaluation. The continuation
implementation serializes these variables around the Brax
PPO learner [44], [49].
Continuation restores all components of (A.1). Challenger
initialization copies θπ and o, creates a new value func-
tion and optimizer, resets collection state and counters,
and collects trajectories on the new body. Value estimates
are therefore learned under the challenger’s dynamics and
training reward.
C. SAC continuation state
For SAC [45], the serialized state is
zSAC = (θπ, θQ, ¯θQ, α, νπ, νQ, να,
o, ce, cg, D, ξ).
(A.2)
The critics and target critics have parameters θQ and ¯θQ; α
is the entropy-temperature state. The variables νπ, νQ, and
να are the corresponding optimizer states. The counter cg
records gradient updates, and D is the replay-buffer state.
The remaining variables have the meanings given for PPO.
SAC continuation restores the critics, targets, entropy
state, and collected replay data together with the Actor. A
challenger initializes these components anew and starts with
an empty replay buffer. Thus its critic updates use transitions
collected under its own morphology and reward.
D. Policy-interface compatibility
The benchmark morphology adapters preserve observation
dimensions, action dimensions, and actuated-joint ordering
within each task. Inheritance uses a direct parameter copy
between compatible policy networks. All six challengers in
the current protocol receive the same Actor and normal-
izer transfer; Conservative, Medium, and Aggressive denote
design-risk groups, not transfer coefficients.
When initialization starts from six untrained designs, all
six use fresh learners. Actor inheritance begins after selection
of the first trained robot. Task-specific initialization settings
are detailed in Appendix C.

Appendix C
Evaluation and Training Budgets
A. Selection metric and diagnostic measurements
Candidate
selection
uses
eval/episode_distance_from_origin:
the
root-position norm in the task coordinates listed in Table S7.
Values are summed over the episode and averaged across
evaluation episodes. This metric measures accumulated
distance from the origin, rather than path length, signed
forward displacement, or training return.
The diagnostic replay is generated separately. It loads the
selected morphology, reward, and policy parameters and runs
deterministic inference with a reset seed derived from the
checkpoint identifier. The rollout ends at termination or the
task horizon. Pipeline-state frames, including the initial state,
are saved for the HTML replay.
The numerical replay summary records executed steps,
cumulative reward, the first and last recorded root x co-
ordinates, their absolute difference, and a finiteness flag.
These positions are sampled after environment steps; the
initial rendered frame is stored separately. The coordinate
difference is a displacement diagnostic. Numerical finiteness
checks valid numerical values and does not establish balance
or task success. Replay video frames and these numerical
measurements are available as behavioral evidence; Feedback
produces the textual diagnoses passed to the design roles.
B. Learner settings
Tables S3 and S4 give algorithm-specific settings. Obser-

C. Networks and software
PPO uses an actor with four 32-unit hidden layers and
a value network with five 256-unit layers, both with Swish
activation. SAC uses two 256-unit ReLU layers for the actor
and each of two critics. Actors parameterize a tanh-squashed
Gaussian. The five-task benchmark uses Python 3.10.20,
Brax 0.12.4, JAX/JAXlib 0.6.2, Flax 0.10.6, MuJoCo/MJX
3.3.3, and Optax 0.2.8. This software configuration applies
to the five-task benchmark; generated robots use the Isaac
Gym adapter described in Sec. III-D.
D. Default baseline and reported checkpoint scores
Default uses the task’s original morphology and built-in
hand-designed reward, with morphology and reward gener-
ation disabled and no LLM calls. Each policy is initialized
from scratch and receives one task-specific training budget:
8 million transitions for Ant, 5 million for HalfCheetah,
400,000 for Hopper, 350,000 for Swimmer, and 500,000 for
Walker2D. The PPO/SAC hyperparameters and task horizons
follow the D2C-aligned settings above; Default is not a 30-
candidate search.
The reported Default checkpoints use seeds 0, 0, 1, 0, and
2 for Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Swimmer, and Walker2D,
respectively. The historical Walker2D value 10,125.74 is the
stored seed-2 checkpoint score, not the three-seed evaluation
mean reported in the main text. Its implementation uses
NaN-safe masks for inactive evaluation episodes and rejected
MJX line-search updates. Morphology, reward, and transition
budget remain unchanged; these numerical repairs are part
of the implementation configuration.
Training and evaluation use separate simulator rollouts. At
the training endpoint, the evaluator holds the policy fixed and
runs 128 parallel episodes with independently reset environ-
ments, the configured task horizon, and the task termination
rule. Policy sampling follows the learner’s evaluation set-
ting; the PPO implementation uses stochastic evaluation by
default. The evaluator logs the episode mean of the fixed task
metric as eval/episode_distance_from_origin.
This value is stored in the candidate result and used for
selection. A retained incumbent keeps its recorded score.
The historical search traces in this appendix report these
stored selection scores. The main comparison and ablation
tables instead report post-selection evaluation: the mean and
sample standard deviation across evaluation seeds 0, 1, 2,


each with 128 parallel episodes and frozen policy parameters,
morphology, and observation statistics. These evaluations do
not retrain the selected LACE–CRAFT policies.
These evaluation episodes estimate the performance of one
trained policy. Repeating the complete proposal–training–
selection search measures a different source of variation.
D2C reports multiple search seeds and separate retraining
studies [4]; adopting its per-candidate learner settings does
not imply those repetitions in the present main tables. The
Ant search-level analysis below reports the complete searches
available for that comparison.

F. Model calls and failed candidates
The recorded model identifier is GPT-5.6-sol with temper-
ature 1.0, low reasoning effort, and low verbosity. The client
requests a JSON object through a streaming Responses-
compatible interface, with a Chat-Completions-compatible
fallback. No explicit maximum output-token count is set.
The CRAFT role pipeline allows three attempts per role; the
transport client also has retry handling. A six-role-call round
is therefore a logical call count, not a limit on metered API
requests.
Validation checks the morphology vector, generated XML,
reward syntax and interface, candidate identifiers, and six-
candidate portfolio. Invalid outputs retain their error records
for correction; exhaustion halts the round. A training excep-
tion or non-finite evaluation score leaves the round unse-
lected. The pipeline does not assign a synthetic zero score to
a failed candidate. Resumption preserves already completed,
checkpoint-bound results. Training budgets count completed
units in the reported search.
G. Design counts and total training
D2C and LACE–CRAFT generate six new designs per
round for five rounds, giving 30 designs per search. LACE
trains six initial designs, then six challengers and one con-
tinuation in each of four later rounds:

B. Dependencies of a morphology edit
A link edit changes geometry within Gm. Affected joint
origins and motor placements must remain consistent with
the revised shape and local coordinate frames. Cavity dimen-
sions are constrained by the selected motors; mating surfaces
must remain aligned between the connected links. Collision
geometry, masses, centers of mass, and inertia tensors are
updated with the compiled model.
The action mapping Γ binds policy outputs to an ordered
set of actuated joints and defines their scaling and control
frequency. Fixed observation and action dimensions and con-
sistent joint ordering are required for direct Actor inheritance.
Changes in articulation that alter these interfaces require a
compatible controller specification before training.
Hardware attachment remains partly manual. Screw-hole
placement and mating details are designed for the selected
components. The printed parts were fabricated without prior
structural simulation, and thin connections were prone to
fracture. Structural analysis and load-aware redesign remain
necessary for more reliable fabrication.
C. Raspberry Pi controller and physical recording
The indoor walking recording uses the R17 hardware-
adapted PPO policy at training epoch 650. It was trained
with the Isaac Gym robot task and exported from the PyTorch
checkpoint to a NumPy parameter archive. This controller be-
longs to the generative-robot hardware adaptation, separately
from the five Brax benchmark policies. Its hardware-specific
training uses three actuated joints per leg (12 actions),
whereas the illustrated geometry-stage model uses two per
leg. The exported actor has hidden widths 512, 256, and
128 with ELU activations and a 12-dimensional output. The
recorded export check reports a maximum absolute NumPy–
PyTorch output difference of 4.17 × 10−7.
Policy inference runs locally on the Raspberry Pi at 20
Hz. The 58-dimensional observation contains 12 measured
joint positions, 12 joint velocities estimated by one-step finite
differences, 12 previous actions, eight sinusoidal gait-clock
signals, three command entries, and 11 fixed placeholder
entries. The placeholders comprise unavailable base linear
and angular velocities, a fixed gravity direction, and un-
available planar position-error signals. No IMU is required.
Observation normalization statistics are exported with the
actor; normalized inputs are clipped to [−5, 5]. The runtime
reproduces the one-control-frame observation delay used in
the adaptation.
Actions are clipped to [−1, 1], low-pass filtered with co-
efficient approximately 0.49646, and scaled by 0.63 radians
about the reference pose. Joint-target changes are limited to
0.145 radians per control step and clipped to joint limits.
Calibrated zero offsets, signs, and radians-per-unit mappings
convert targets into servo-bus commands. The policy joint
ordering maps to servo IDs 1, 5, 9, 2, 6, 10, 3, 7, 11, 4, 8, 12.
The internal gait clock runs at 1.05 Hz. These settings
describe the R17 deployment; the arm is absent during the
recorded walking trial.
The figure uses frames at 24, 28, 32, and 36 seconds from
one continuous indoor recording. Equal-sized crops follow
the robot to keep its body and feet visible. They docu-

benchmark task scores. Quantitative comparisons in the main
tables concern the simulated benchmark tasks.
D. Manipulation interface
A mount is incorporated into the base link for an SO-101
arm, while the arm’s existing links, motors, and gripper are
reused. The assembled photograph shows the arm installed;
the walking recording shows the platform without it. Simu-
lation controls the leg joints while holding the arm at pre-

poorly. Effective mobile manipulation requires additional
control and mechanical validation. Measured joint motion
and real-world video could subsequently supply blackboard
feedback.
