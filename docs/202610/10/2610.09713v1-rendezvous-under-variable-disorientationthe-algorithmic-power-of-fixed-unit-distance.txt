Rendezvous under Variable Disorientation:
The Algorithmic Power of Fixed Unit Distance
Toshimitsu Masuzawa1, Yuichi Sudo2, and Koichi Wada2
1Notre Dame Seishin University, Okayama, Japan
2Hosei University, Tokyo, Japan
Abstract
We study deterministic Rendezvous of two robots with lights under weak geometric and syn-
chronization assumptions.
Our focus is on Variable Disorientation (VD), where each robot may
arbitrarily rotate, reflect, and rescale its local coordinate system at every Look, and on VD+Fixed-
Unit-Distance (FUD), where each robot’s local unit distance remains fixed. We consider the one-sided
visibility models FSTA and FCOM, and schedulers ranging from the energy-restricted Rsynch and
R-Rsynch to fully asynchronous executions.
Under VD, we prove color-independent impossibility results for FSTA under Ssynch and for
FCOM under Asynch, and establish sharp two-color boundaries under Rsynch and R-Rsynch.
Under VD+FUD, we introduce periodic distance classes, which turn multiplicative changes of the
physical distance into predictable cyclic phase shifts. This technique yields a two-color self-stabilizing
non-L-Rendezvous algorithm for both FSTA and FCOM under Rsynch and R-Rsynch, even with
Non-Rigid movement.
Under Rigid movement, periodic distance classes further yield an optimal three-color non-quasi-
self-stabilizing algorithm for FSTA under Ssynch, a four-color FSTA algorithm under M-atomic
Asynch, and a four-color FCOM algorithm under CM-atomic Asynch. By guarding the latter
phase mechanism with two additional colors and replacing its initial swap by passive initialization,
we obtain a six-color FCOM algorithm under full Asynch, improving the previous twelve-color
upper bound by a factor of two.
These results show that a fixed local metric can serve as persistent cyclic memory, and clarify how
metric stability, one-sided visibility, and scheduler atomicity jointly determine Rendezvous solvability
and color complexity.

Introduction
Robot Models. The computational issues of autonomous mobile robots have been extensively studied in
distributed computing [AP06, BDT13, CFPS12, DKL+11, IBTW11, KLOT11, SDY09] since the seminal
work of Suzuki and Yamashita [SY99]. In the standard setting, a robot is modeled as a point in the
plane, and robots are anonymous, uniform, and deterministic. They operate in Look-Compute-Move
(LCM) cycles: each activated robot observes the current configuration, computes a destination and a
new internal state, and then moves accordingly.
To overcome the limitations of oblivious robots, persistent lights have been introduced, leading to the
models OBLOT [SY99], FSTA and FCOM [FSVY16], and LUMI [DFP+16]. In this paper we focus
on the two one-sided visibility models: FSTA, where a robot observes only its own light, and FCOM,
where it observes only the other robot’s light. Although these models differ in the direction of visibility,
many of our arguments depend only on the fact that exactly one color is visible at each Look. This
motivates our unified use of the one-sided visibility model.
Geometric Assumptions and Schedulers. We study weak geometric settings. In particular, under
Variable Disorientation (VD), the adversary may arbitrarily rotate, reflect, and rescale each robot’s local

coordinate system at every Look phase. We also consider its refinement VD+Fixed-Unit-Distance (FUD),
where the local unit distance remains fixed. These assumptions capture situations in which robots do
not share a stable geometric reference frame.
We consider both the energy-restricted schedulers Rsynch and R-Rsynch [BFK+25] and increasingly
asynchronous settings: Ssynch, CM-atomic and M-atomic Asynch, and full Asynch [FSSW24]. This
allows us to study both phase-structured activation and the gradual loss of atomicity between Compute
and Move.
Rendezvous and L-Algorithms. Rendezvous is the two-robot version of Gathering: two initially
separated robots must meet at an a priori unknown location in finite time. It has been extensively studied
under various geometric and scheduling assumptions [DFP+16, DPBRP20, FSW19, IKIW07, ISK+12,
LMA07, OWD23, SDY09]. A central class of Rendezvous algorithms is formed by L-algorithms. In such
algorithms, each robot always computes a destination on the line connecting the two robots, and both
the movement parameter and the light update are determined solely by the observed color, independently
of the distance between the robots. Under the one-sided visibility model considered in this paper, this
means that the robot reacts only to the single observed color at each Look.
Because L-algorithms
abstract away from metric information, they provide a natural reference class for studying Rendezvous
under weak geometric assumptions.
Related Work and Landscape. Previous work has established several sharp possibility and impos-
sibility results for L-algorithms under geometrically stable settings. In particular, a number of classical
lower and upper bounds were obtained under fixed disorientation and fixed unit distance [FSVY16,
Vig13]. The present paper revisits this landscape from the viewpoint of VD and VD+FUD. Under VD,
some of the known impossibility results for L-algorithms remain valid, and in the scheduler/model com-
binations considered here they can even be strengthened beyond the L setting. Under VD+FUD, by
contrast, the recovered metric stability enables new positive results. In this sense, our results provide a
geometric reclassification of the known L-Rendezvous landscape. Tables 1 and 2 make this relationship
explicit. In these tables, SS, QS, and NS mean self-stabilizing, quasi-self-stabilizing, and non-quasi-self-
stabilizing algorithms, respectively. A single number k indicates that the exact color complexity is k. An
entry k–ℓindicates a lower bound of k and an upper bound of ℓ, whereas k–? indicates that the current

FCOM under CM-atomic Asynch, both under VD+FUD and Rigid movement.
Our Contribution. Our results are organized around the contrast between VD and VD+FUD, and
around the use of a fixed local unit as a source of persistent cyclic metric information.
First, under VD, we establish impossibility results for FSTA under Ssynch and for FCOM under
Asynch, without restricting the number of colors or the algorithm to the L-class. We also determine the
role of two colors under Rsynch and R-Rsynch in the one-sided visibility model. Under Rsynch, two
colors suffice for quasi-self-stabilizing L-Rendezvous but not for self-stabilization. Under R-Rsynch, no
deterministic two-color quasi-self-stabilizing algorithm exists, whereas under Rigid movement a two-color
non-quasi-self-stabilizing L-algorithm does exist.
Second, under VD+FUD, we introduce periodic (b, m)-distance classes: logarithmic distance bands
whose successive boundaries differ by a factor b, labeled cyclically with m classes. Consequently, multiply-
ing or dividing the physical distance by b shifts the class by one modulo m, allowing a robot’s fixed local
unit to serve as finite cyclic memory. A related use of metric periodicity appears in a three-robot Distinct
Gathering algorithm under FUD, where a golden-ratio-based periodic partition is employed [FW26].
The (4, 2)-classes break the mixed-color cycle and yield a two-color self-stabilizing non-L-Rendezvous
algorithm for both FSTA and FCOM under Rsynch and R-Rsynch, even with Non-Rigid movement.
Under Rigid movement, the (2, 2)- and (2, 3)-classes yield an optimal three-color FSTA algorithm under
Ssynch, four-color algorithms for FSTA under M-atomic Asynch and for FCOM under CM-atomic
Asynch, and a six-color FCOM algorithm under full Asynch, improving the previous twelve-color
upper bound to six.

Robot Model
2.1
Robots and Configurations
We consider two anonymous robots r and s operating in R2. Robots execute deterministic
Look –Compute –Move (LCM) cycles [FPS12]. Robots have no identifiers and execute the same algo-
rithm. At Look, a robot records the peer’s position and the light information visible in its model. At
Compute, it determines its light update and movement; at Move, it follows the computed trajectory.
Only light values persist between LCM cycles. All algorithms presented below use straight-line move-
ments. Movements of robots are said to be Rigid if the robots always reach the computed destination.
They are said to be Non-Rigid if there exists a constant δ > 0 such that whenever the computed desti-
3


nation is at distance at least δ from the current position, the adversary may stop the robot before the
destination but only after it has moved at least δ; if the destination is closer than δ, the robot reaches it.
Each robot has its own local coordinate system in which it sees itself at the origin.
The local
coordinate systems (i.e., the direction, the orientation, and the unit of distance) of different robots need
not be consistent. The absence of this consistency is called disorientation. Under Variable Disorientation
(VD), at every Look phase the adversary may independently apply an arbitrary rotation, reflection, and
scaling to each robot’s local coordinate system. Hence, directions are not globally consistent, orientation
may flip, and distances are not metrically reliable. Under VD+Fixed-Unit-Distance (FUD), the scaling
factor of each robot’s local coordinate system remains fixed throughout the execution.
Thus, while
orientations may still vary, each robot has a consistent notion of unit distance. This additional geometric
stability strictly increases computational power.
Robots may be equipped with persistent lights whose joint values are taken from a finite set Γ of
colors. A light update is committed atomically at the end of Compute, before the corresponding Move
begins. The standard LUMI model allows a robot to observe both its own light and the other robot’s
light, whereas OBLOT robots have no lights. These two models appear in Tables 1 and 2 only to place
our results in the known landscape.

2.2
Schedulers
Executions are sequences of activations chosen by an adversarial fair scheduler.
Fsynch: both robots are activated simultaneously in every round.
Round Robin (RR): exactly one robot is activated in each round, and the two robots are activated
alternately.
Rsynch: an execution is either a pure Fsynch execution or consists of a finite, possibly empty,
prefix of Fsynch rounds followed by an infinite RR suffix.
R-Rsynch1: an execution is either a pure RR execution or consists of a finite, possibly empty, prefix
of RR rounds followed by an infinite Fsynch suffix.
Ssynch: in each round, a nonempty subset of robots is activated.
Thus, both Rsynch and R-Rsynch include pure Fsynch and pure RR executions and are restricted
subclasses of Ssynch. The two schedulers Rsynch and R-Rsynch are incomparable.
The following asynchronous schedulers differ in their atomicity constraints.
1R means reverse.
4


CM-atomic Asynch: once a robot begins its Compute operation, no other robot performs a Look
until that robot completes the corresponding Move operation.
M-atomic Asynch: no robot performs a Look while another robot is executing its Move operation.
Asynch: robots execute LCM cycles asynchronously, with no global notion of rounds and no atom-
icity constraint between their operations.
Every CM-atomic execution is M-atomic, and every M-atomic execution is an Asynch execution.
For FSTA, CM-atomic and M-atomic Asynch are observationally equivalent, so the same algorithm
with the same number of colors works under both. For FCOM, they are observationally distinguishable
but have the same computational power; the known simulation may require additional colors [FSSW24].2
2.3
Rendezvous and Stabilization
Rendezvous is achieved if there is some time t such that for any t′ ≥t, pr(t′) = ps(t′).
Let A be a Rendezvous algorithm.
It is self-stabilizing if the two robots conforming to A solve
Rendezvous from any configuration, regardless of their initial colors. It is quasi-self-stabilizing if they
solve Rendezvous from any configuration in which the two robots have the same color. It is non-quasi-
self-stabilizing if there exists a color c such that they solve Rendezvous from any configuration in which
both robots have color c.
A specific category of algorithms, L-algorithms, requires each robot to determine both the movement
parameter λ and its light update solely from the colors observed in the snapshot. Neither may depend
on the distance between the robots. For the one-sided visibility model considered here, both depend only
on obsx(t).

3
Impossibility Results for Rendezvous
3.1
Impossibility under VD
We first show that under VD, even with rigid movements, Rendezvous is impossible in certain sched-
uler/model combinations, regardless of the number of colors and without restricting to L-algorithms.
Lemma 3 (VD Normalization). Let C be any configuration in which the two robots occupy distinct
positions. Under VD, at every Look operation, the adversary can ensure that each robot observes the
other at position (0, 1) in its local coordinate system.
6


Proof. Under VD, at every Look, the adversary may independently choose the local coordinate system
of the observing robot, up to translation, rotation, reflection, and scaling. Thus the observing robot can
be placed at the origin, the direction toward the other robot aligned with the positive y-axis, and the
distance scaled to 1.
The normalization lemma removes the essential distinction between general Rendezvous algorithms
and L-algorithms for the purpose of the following impossibility argument. Indeed, although a general
algorithm may use the measured distance to determine its destination and its light update, under VD
the adversary can normalize every non-Rendezvous snapshot so that the other robot is always observed
at (0, 1). Thus, as long as the robots remain distinct, the deterministic behavior of a robot depends only
on the observed color.
We can therefore lift the corresponding impossibility argument for L-algorithms [FSVY16] to arbitrary
deterministic algorithms.
Theorem 1. Under VD, there exists no deterministic non-quasi-self-stabilizing Rendezvous algorithm,
regardless of the number of colors, (i) for FSTA under Ssynch, or (ii) for FCOM under Asynch.
Proof sketch. By Lemma 3, from every non-Rendezvous same-color configuration the adversary can make
both robots obtain the same normalized geometric view as in the corresponding L-algorithm impossibility
argument. If the resulting move is not to the midpoint, a simultaneous activation preserves distinct
positions and produces another same-color configuration. If it is a midpoint move, the adversary uses
sequential activations under Ssynch for FSTA, and a stale-snapshot execution under Asynch for
FCOM, to obtain another non-Rendezvous same-color configuration. Repeating the construction gives
a fair infinite execution without Rendezvous. The detailed proof is deferred to Appendix B.
Under VD, no Rendezvous algorithm exists for FCOM robots in Asynch, even without restrict-
ing the algorithm to be in L.
In contrast, in the Non-Rigid Ssynch model, there exists a self-
stabilizing L-Rendezvous algorithm with 3 colors for FCOM robots, and this algorithm works even
under VD [DFP+16].
The following known lower bounds under Ssynch hold even under Fixed Disorientation, common
chirality, and a common unit distance [OFW26].
Theorem 2 ([OFW26]). Under Ssynch and Rigid movement, even with Fixed Disorientation, common
chirality, and a common unit distance:
1. no two-color non-quasi-self-stabilizing Rendezvous algorithm exists in either FSTA or FCOM;
and
2. no quasi-self-stabilizing Rendezvous algorithm exists in FSTA, regardless of the number of colors.
Consequently, any positive Rendezvous result for FSTA under Ssynch in our VD+FUD setting
must rely on a designated initial color. Combined with Theorem 8, this yields a sharp separation: quasi-
self-stabilization is impossible with any number of colors, whereas non-quasi-self-stabilizing Rendezvous
is solvable with exactly three colors.

prescribed upon observing c is the midpoint, and a move-to-other color if it is the current position of
the peer.

5.1
Reduction Principles
We use two reduction principles whose formal statements and proofs are given in Appendix E. First,
correctness under both Fsynch and RR yields correctness under Rsynch and R-Rsynch, with the
standard additional condition for quasi-self-stabilization. Second, for the algorithms considered below,
the Non-Rigid distance eventually becomes at most δ. It therefore suffices to verify their finite-state
behavior in the effective Rigid regime D ≤δ.
5.2
A Two-Color Quasi-Self-Stabilizing L-Algorithm
Theorem 3 shows that two colors are insufficient for self-stabilization under Rsynch. We now show that
two colors nevertheless suffice for quasi-self-stabilization.
The algorithm QS-L-2-color, shown in Figure 1(a), uses the rules A 7−→(B, H),
B 7−→(A, O). The pseudocode for QS-L-2-color is shown in Appendix F. The graph is interpreted
with respect to the observed color and therefore applies to both FSTA and FCOM.
Theorem 5. QS-L-2-color is a two-color quasi-self-stabilizing L-Rendezvous algorithm for both FSTA
and FCOM under Rsynch in the Non-Rigid setting.
Proof sketch. Under Fsynch, a same-color configuration remains same-colored: from (A, A), both robots
move to the midpoint, whereas from (B, B), they first exchange positions and then move to the midpoint.
Thus no mixed-color configuration is generated.
Under RR, once the distance is at most δ, Rendezvous is achieved from any color configuration within
at most three activations in FSTA and at most two activations in FCOM.
8


For larger distances, the standard distance-decreasing argument shows that every Non-Rigid execution
eventually reaches distance at most δ. Combining the Fsynch and RR executions then gives the result
under Rsynch.
Details are given in Appendix G.
Corollary 1. Under R-Rsynch and Rigid movement, QS-L-2-color, with B as the designated initial
color, is a two-color non-quasi-self-stabilizing L-Rendezvous algorithm for both FSTA and FCOM.
Proof. If the execution begins with an RR activation, the activated robot observes B and reaches the
stationary peer. If it begins with an Fsynch round, the robots exchange positions and enter (A, A),
after which the next Fsynch round moves both robots to the midpoint.
5.3
An Optimal Three-Color Self-Stabilizing L-Algorithm for FSTA
The algorithm SS-L-3-color, shown in Figure 1(b), uses A 7−→(B, H), B 7−→(C, H),
C 7−→(A, O). The pseudocode for SS-L-3-color is shown in Appendix H.
In the FSTA interpretation, the three colors form a private cycle. Under RR, every robot reaches
color C after at most two of its own activations and then performs a move-to-other action.
Under
Fsynch, all color pairs are resolved in at most three rounds once the distance is at most δ.
Theorem 6. SS-L-3-color is a three-color self-stabilizing L-Rendezvous algorithm for FSTA robots
under both Rsynch and R-Rsynch in the Non-Rigid setting.
Proof sketch. Correctness under Fsynch follows by examining the nine possible ordered color pairs;
correctness under RR follows from the private color progression A −→B −→C −→A. The standard
distance-decreasing argument extends these effective-Rigid executions to Non-Rigid movement, and the
Fsynch and RR properties imply correctness under both Rsynch and R-Rsynch. The complete proof
is given in Appendix I.
Corollary 2. Under VD and Rsynch, the self-stabilizing color complexity of Rendezvous for FSTA
robots is exactly three.

FUD Breaks the Two-Color L-Barrier
The previous results identify a two-color barrier within the L framework. Indeed, the forced-transition

mixed-color Fsynch execution halves the distance forever. We now show that FUD allows this barrier
to be overcome once the L restriction is dropped. The key is to use the locally measured distance as an
additional cyclic bit.

Proof sketch. The algorithm satisfies the reduction principles proved in Appendix E. In particular, under

the first M is consistent with this unchanged distance.

M, whereas offset 2 produces N, after which the peer eventually observes N and enters M. If both robots
publish phase colors, phase complementarity ensures that at least one has offset 0 or 1.

completed, every cycle of the peer s whose Look occurs after M becomes visible generates a null action.
At most one cycle of s, whose Look occurred before the first M, may remain incomplete.

symmetry, O/S, H/H, H/S. Indeed, if the first M-move is triggered by observing N, it forms an O/S
pair. Otherwise, it is triggered by a phase color consistent with the unchanged distance. The peer’s
possible pre-M cycle is either a null initialization action, yielding H/S or O/S, or another phase-based
action. In the latter case, phase complementarity and the mapping 0 7→H, 1 7→O, 2 7→S yield H/H,

In the O/S case, the Rigid move reaches the stationary robot.
In the H/H case, both actions have the same midpoint as their destination. After the first robot
reaches it, every subsequent activation of that robot is null: it either observes M, or observes the still-
visible phase color at distance D/2, obtains offset 2, and enters N. It therefore remains at the midpoint
until the other Rigid midpoint move arrives.

move, the mover’s distance class decreases from j to j −1, so its offset from Cj is 2. It therefore enters N

prevents fresh non-null interference during this move, and rigidity completes Rendezvous.

7
Concluding Remarks
Together with known results, our bounds complete the VD classification in Table 1. Under VD+FUD,
periodic distance classes turn a fixed local metric into cyclic memory, yielding two-, three-, four-, and six-
color algorithms. These gains require neither agreement on unit distance nor stable orientations: the key
14


geometric resource is the consistency of each robot’s own unit over time. For full Asynch, the six-color
construction combines this metric phase mechanism with movement guards and passive initialization to
handle stale observations.
Open questions include closing the 3–6 gap for non-quasi-self-stabilizing FCOM Rendezvous under
full Rigid Asynch, resolving the remaining stabilization and FSTA cases, and extending the Rigid
constructions to Non-Rigid movement and larger robot populations.

Color-Independent Impossibility under VD
Theorem 1 (Reprint of Theorem 1 on page 7). Under VD, there exists no deterministic non-quasi-
self-stabilizing Rendezvous algorithm regardless of the number of colors (i) in the FSTA model under
Ssynch, or (ii) in the FCOM model under Asynch.
Proof. Assume, for contradiction, that such an algorithm A exists. Consider an initial non-gathered
configuration in which both robots have the same color.
By Lemma 3, each robot always sees the other at (0, 1) in its own local coordinate system. Hence, in
both models, the two robots compute the same light update and the same destination.
We show that from any non-gathered same-color configuration, the adversary can construct a finite
legal execution fragment that ends in another non-gathered same-color configuration.
By repeating
this fragment forever, the adversary obtains an infinite fair execution with no Rendezvous, yielding a
contradiction.
Case 1: the computed destination is not the midpoint.
The adversary lets both robots perform one cycle based on identical snapshots.
If the destination lies on the segment connecting the robots but is not the midpoint, then after the
moves the robots remain distinct. If the destination lies off the segment, the adversary chooses the local
coordinate systems so that the two computed destinations become mirror images, and hence are distinct
as well.
Thus Rendezvous does not occur. Since both robots executed the same rule, they also obtain the same
resulting color. Therefore the resulting configuration is again a non-gathered same-color configuration.
Case 2: the computed destination is the midpoint.
We distinguish the two models.
Case 2a: FSTA under Ssynch. The adversary activates the robots sequentially in two rounds. First,
one robot moves to the midpoint and changes its color. Then the other robot, still having the original
color, observes the same situation and executes the same rule. After these two activations, the robots
remain distinct but have the same new color. Hence the resulting configuration is again a non-gathered
same-color configuration.
Case 2b: FCOM under Asynch. The adversary executes the following asynchronous schedule:
1. activate robot r for Look only,
2. activate robot s for two complete LCM cycles,
3. complete the pending Compute–Move of r.
18


Since r computes using a stale snapshot, while s performs two cycles observing the same color each time,
the robots end in distinct positions but with the same new color. Hence the resulting configuration is
again a non-gathered same-color configuration. Robot s applies the same componentwise update in both
cycles; since this update is idempotent, its second application does not change the resulting color. Robot
r later applies the same update to the original color, and hence both robots obtain the same resulting
color.
In all cases, the adversary can transform any non-gathered same-color configuration into another
non-gathered same-color configuration. Repeating this construction indefinitely produces an infinite fair
execution in which Rendezvous never occurs, contradicting the correctness of A.
Therefore no deterministic non-quasi-self-stabilizing Rendezvous algorithm exists.

Scheduler Composition and Non-Rigid Distance Reduction
Self-stabilization under both Fsynch and RR yields self-stabilization under Rsynch and R-Rsynch.
For quasi-self-stabilization, additional compatibility conditions are needed, as stated below. The following
lemma can be derived from the definitions of self-stabilization and quasi-self-stabilization.
Lemma 7.
(1) A Rendezvous algorithm A is self-stabilizing under Rsynch if and only if it is self-
stabilizing under both Fsynch and RR. The same equivalence holds for R-Rsynch.
(2-1) If a Rendezvous algorithm A is quasi-self-stabilizing in RR
and it is quasi-self-stabilizing in
Fsynch with the property that it does not reach a configuration of differently colored robots during
any execution of A (in Fsynch), then A is quasi-self-stabilizing in Rsynch.
(2-2) If a Rendezvous algorithm A is self-stabilizing in RR (resp. Fsynch), and it is quasi-self-stabilizing
in Fsynch (resp. RR), then A is quasi-self-stabilizing in Rsynch (resp. R-Rsynch).
In the following, we obtain self-stabilizing Rendezvous algorithms in Rsynch and R-Rsynch and a
quasi-self-stabilizing Rendezvous algorithm in Rsynch by using this lemma. The two-color L-algorithm
presented here is not self-stabilizing in Fsynch, hence a quasi-self-stabilizing L-Rendezvous algorithm
for R-Rsynch is not obtained.
In what follows, we will consider Rendezvous algorithms that operate in the Non-Rigid setting. The
following lemma reduces the Non-Rigid correctness analysis to configurations whose distance is at most
δ.
Lemma 8 (Distance Decreasing Lemma). Suppose that, whenever the robots occupy distinct positions,
every activated robot computes a move toward either the midpoint or the other robot. Rendezvous occurs
or there exists t′ > t such that dis(t′) ≤δ under either of the following conditions:
(a) under Fsynch, every maximal consecutive block in which both robots move to the other’s position
has length at most a fixed constant;
(b) under RR.
Proof. We only need to consider rounds with dis(t) > δ.
(a) In Fsynch, if we start from time t, the possible behaviors of r and s are as follows:
(1) Both move towards the midpoint.
(2) One moves towards the midpoint and the other moves towards the other robot.
(3) Both move towards the other robots.
In Non-Rigid movement, for (1), if they do not rendezvous, dis(t + 1) is reduced by at least 2δ, because
both robots move at least δ. For (2), we can show that the distance is reduced by at least δ/2 as follows.
If the robots do not cross, it is reduced by at least 2δ. Otherwise, it is reduced by at least dis(t)/2 > δ/2;
the minimum reduction occurs when one robot, say r, reaches the midpoint and s reaches the location
of r at t.
In case (3), the distance may remain unchanged.
However, every consecutive block of case (3)
has bounded length, whereas every intervening round of case (1) or (2) either achieves Rendezvous or
decreases the distance by at least δ/2. Hence the distance becomes at most δ after finitely many rounds
unless Rendezvous occurs earlier.
Therefore, there is a time t′ such that dis(t′) ≤δ for any possible behavior.
(b) Under RR, if dis(t) > 2δ, every non-null move decreases the distance by at least δ. If
δ < dis(t) ≤2δ,
a midpoint move reaches the midpoint and leaves distance at most δ, whereas a move-to-other action
moves at least δ and also leaves distance at most δ. Thus, after finitely many activations, the distance
becomes at most δ, unless Rendezvous occurs earlier. Thus, there is a time t′ such that dis(t′) ≤δ.

Lemma 9. QS-L-2-color is a quasi-self-stabilizing L-Rendezvous algorithm with 2 colors in the one-

Proof. Recall that in QS-L-2-color, the rule associated with the observed color A is “move to the midpoint
and change to B,” and the rule associated with the observed color B is “move to the other robot and

In a same-color Fsynch execution, a simultaneous move-to-other round changes (B, B) to (A, A), so

transition, their colors are always updated simultaneously to the same next color. Therefore no mixed-
