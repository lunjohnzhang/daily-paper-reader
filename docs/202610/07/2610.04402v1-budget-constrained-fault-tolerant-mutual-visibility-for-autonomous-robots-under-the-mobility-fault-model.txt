Abstract 

We investigate the mutual visibility problem for a swarm of n ≥ 3 autonomous mobile robots under the budget-constrained mobility fault model. The robots are opaque, so if three robots are collinear, the middle robot obstructs the visibility between the other two. Each robot is assigned a finite movement budget, reflecting its limited energy, that bounds the total distance it may traverse during the execution. Moreover, an arbitrary number of robots may become permanently immobile due to mobility faults. The objective is to design a distributed algorithm that enables the non-faulty robots to coordinate their movements so that, within a finite time, every non-faulty robot attains unobstructed visibility of all robots in the system, including the faulty ones, while respecting the prescribed movement budget. We consider luminous robots operating under the SSYNC model with non-rigid movements, without any agreement on their local coordinate systems, and equipped only with a common fixed reference point . We present a deterministic distributed algorithm that solves the problem despite an arbitrary number of mobility faults. The algorithm guarantees mutual visibility for the non-faulty robots, respects the movement budget of every robot, provides collision-free movements for the robots, and uses only 12 light colors. 

Keywords: Autonomous mobile robots; Mutual visibility; Mobility faults; Budget-constrained movement; Luminous robots; Semi-synchronous model; Non-rigid movement; Common reference point. 

# 1 Introduction 

We consider a finite set R of n anonymous, homogeneous, opaque, and luminous point robots deployed in the Euclidean plane. Robots have unlimited visibility, but their line of sight is obstructed by opaque robots. Each robot has its own local coordinate system, with no agreement on the coordinate axes or chirality, and executes the same deterministic algorithm. Communication is achieved only by observing the positions and light colors of visible robots. Robots are oblivious except for persistent lights operating under the FALL model. Each robot is equipped with a persistent light that can assume a color from a finite set shared by all robots. The selected color remains unchanged across subsequent activations until the robot explicitly changes it. Thus, the light serves both as persistent local memory and as a means of communication among the robots. The robots operate under the semi-synchronous ( SSYNC ) model. Under the SSYNC model, time is divided into discrete rounds. In each round, the scheduler activates an

Look phase, each activated robot takes a snapshot containing the positions and light colors of all robots visible to it, together with its own current light color. During the Compute phase, it uses the obtained snapshot to compute a destination point according to the algorithm and may also update its light color. Finally, during the Move phase, the robot moves toward its computed destination. The scheduler is fair, meaning that every robot is activated infinitely often. An epoch is the shortest sequence of consecutive rounds during which every robot is activated at least once. A robot may be activated multiple times within the same epoch. By the fairness of the scheduler, every epoch is finite. Robot movements are non-rigid, i.e., a robot may be stopped before reaching its computed destination after moving at least an unknown positive distance δ. In addition, every robot can detect a common fixed external reference beacon P ∗ and obtain its relative position in its local coordinate system during each Look phase. Each robot is initially assigned the same finite movement budget B, which bounds the total Euclidean distance it may traverse during the execution. A robot may suffer a mobility fault at any time, after which it becomes permanently immobile while retaining its sensing, computation, and light capabilities. Faulty robots cannot distinguish themselves from fault-free robots. Throughout the paper, we consider the budget-constrained mobility fault model, in which an arbitrary number of robots may become mobility faulty. 

# 1.1 Related Work 

The mutual visibility problem for autonomous mobile robots was formally introduced by Di Luna et al. [9] in the classical oblivious robot model. Under obstructed visibility, robots are considered opaque, and hence a robot lying on the line segment joining two other robots prevents them from seeing each other. The objective is therefore to transform an arbitrary initial configuration into one in which no three robots are collinear, while avoiding collisions. Di Luna et al. studied the problem for oblivious robots under the 

SSYNC scheduler and proposed a convex-hull-based solution. Closely related to mutual visibility is the problem of forming a general position . Bhagat et al. [4] studied this problem for asynchronous opaque robots under one-axis agreement and provided a collision-free algorithm that removes collinearities. Early investigations also focused on the efficiency of mutual-visibility algorithms. Sharma et al. [18] analyzed bounds on existing mutual-visibility algorithms, while subsequent work improved the running time for oblivious robots; in particular, Sharma et al. [21] obtained an O(n)-time algorithm in the fully synchronous setting. A major line of research considers the luminous robot model , in which each robot carries a persistent externally visible light whose color can be used both as a small amount of persistent memory and as a means of communication. Di Luna et al. [10] gave fundamental results for this model, showing that mutual visibility can be solved using two colors in the rigid SSYNC setting and three colors in ASYNC ;they also considered non-rigid movements and established solutions under additional assumptions such as one-axis agreement. Sharma et al. [19] subsequently studied the number of colors required and obtained color-efficient solutions. In parallel, Vaidyanathan et al. [24] investigated the time complexity of complete visibility and proposed a logarithmic-time algorithm for robots with lights. Sharma et al. [23] further reduced the running time to O(1) rounds in synchronous settings. Bhagat and Mukhopadhyaya [5] considered an energy-oriented variant and proposed an algorithm that restricts the number of movements performed by each robot. For the asynchronous setting, constant-time complete visibility was subsequently obtained by Sharma et al. [22]. Thus, much of the luminous-robot literature has concentrated on reducing either the number of colors, the number of movements, or the overall running time. Mutual visibility has also been investigated under alternative computational models and geometric 2domains. Bhagat and Mukhopadhyaya [6] considered the FSTATE model, in which robots have a constant amount of persistent internal memory rather than externally visible lights, and showed that one bit of memory is sufficient in the semi-synchronous setting. This direction was further developed by Bhagat [3], who studied optimum mutual visibility with finite-state robots. The problem has also been extended from point robots to robots having physical extent. Sharma et al. [17] studied complete visibility for fat luminous robots, whereas Sharma et al. [20] investigated the corresponding problem for non-transparent fat robots without lights. More recently, Bose et al. [8] considered a more realistic sensing model in which a fat robot is equipped with a slim omnidirectional camera and proposed a mutual-visibility algorithm under the SSYNC scheduler. Another line of work restricts robot movements to discrete environments. Adhikary et al. [1] studied asynchronous luminous robots on an infinite grid, where movements are restricted to grid edges, and Kim et al. [11] subsequently investigated two-color complete-visibility algorithms on the grid under stronger coordinate-agreement assumptions. These results demonstrate that the difficulty of mutual visibility depends strongly on the robots’ geometric representation, movement domain, memory, and degree of coordinate agreement. Fault-tolerant mutual visibility constitutes another important branch of the literature. Aljohani and Sharma [2] considered crash faults and gave a complete-visibility algorithm tolerating a faulty robot under coordinate-agreement assumptions. Poudel et al. [12] studied the more general mobility-fault model, in which a faulty robot loses its ability to move but can continue to execute the other phases of its Look–Compute–Move cycle. Their algorithm works under one-axis agreement and tolerates multiple mobility faults. A different fault model was considered by Sharma [16], where faults affect the lights of the robots rather than their mobility. More recently, Pramanick et al. [13] investigated mutual visibility in the presence of angular inaccuracies in robot movements and also considered the simultaneous presence of mobility faults. Their algorithms work for luminous robots under one-axis agreement and show that mutual visibility can still be obtained despite bounded directional errors. The assumption of a common axis was subsequently relaxed in fault-tolerant settings. Pramanick et al. [14] studied mobility failures for completely disoriented robots, i.e., robots having no agreement on their local coordinate systems. They established fault-tolerant algorithms for multiple faults in the 

FSYNC setting and for a single fault in the SSYNC and ASYNC settings. More recently, Pramanick et al. [15] further investigated asynchronous fault-tolerant mutual visibility for disoriented robots. Along a different direction, Bhagat et al. [7] studied the min-move mutual visibility problem under the strong combination of disorientation, ASYNC scheduling, and non-rigid movements. They showed that the problem is impossible without additional persistent information and proposed a luminous algorithm in which every robot moves at most once. From the viewpoint of solution techniques, a large fraction of the above algorithms are convex-hull-based :robots progressively expand, contract, or otherwise manipulate the convex hull until all relevant robots occupy suitable hull vertices [9, 10, 24, 23, 19, 22, 17, 20, 14]. Although this approach is powerful, it generally requires robots to travel to geometrically prescribed hull positions and therefore provides limited control over the amount of movement performed by an individual robot. Non-convex-hull techniques instead remove collinearities by carefully selecting local destinations without requiring all robots to become vertices of a common convex hull. Such approaches have been used for general-position formation and movement-efficient mutual visibility [4, 5, 7]. However, existing non-convex-hull solutions either rely on additional coordinate agreement, do not tolerate mobility failures, or address substantially different fault assumptions. To the best of our knowledge, no existing algorithm simultaneously provides a non-convex-hull-based 

solution for disoriented robots under mobility faults while also enforcing an explicit budget on robot movement. In this paper, we address this gap by studying the mutual visibility problem under a 

budget-constrained mobility fault model . Our algorithm follows a non-convex-hull strategy and is 3designed so that the motion of every non-faulty robot remains compatible with the prescribed movement budget. This additional constraint fundamentally distinguishes our setting from earlier mobility-fault algorithms [12, 14, 15]: directly moving robots toward convex-hull vertices may require an arbitrarily large displacement relative to the available budget, and hence the existing convex-hull-based techniques cannot be applied without substantial modification. 

# 1.2 Motivation and Our Contribution 

We study fault-tolerant mutual visibility under a finite movement budget, where each robot can traverse a total Euclidean distance of at most B. Existing fault-tolerant solutions either rely on one-axis agreement or use convex-hull based relocation strategies. In contrast, we assume no agreement on local coordinate axes or chirality. Instead, every robot can identify a common fixed reference point P ∗ lying outside the convex hull of the initial configuration. The point P ∗ provides only a common geometric landmark; it does not induce a common direction, orientation, or coordinate system. We consider a strong fault-tolerant visibility requirement: every non-faulty robot must eventually have unobstructed visibility of every robot in the system, including mobility-faulty robots. We present a deterministic non-convex-hull-based algorithm for luminous robots operating under SSYNC with non-rigid movements. The algorithm uses the relative distances of robots from P ∗ to coordinate movements and resolve cyclic dependencies. Each movement is chosen using local geometric clearances and the robot’s remaining movement budget, so no robot is required to reach a prescribed distant target. Our algorithm tolerates an arbitrary number of permanent mobility faults, guarantees collision-free movements, respects the movement budget of every robot, uses 12 light colors, and terminates in O(n)

# 3 Algorithm Mutual-Visibility-Mobility-Fault() 

The algorithm consists of four logical stages encoded by the light colors. These stages need not start or terminate simultaneously for all robots. In particular, a robot never needs to infer that a phase has been completed by all robots in the system. Instead, it advances according to its own light state and the states of the visible robots on which its current movement depends. Such robots are adjacent or friend robots and hence are visible by definition. Therefore, all state transitions used by the algorithm are locally detectable.

# 3.2 Algorithm Description 

Initially, all the robots have the lights off . During Phase I , both terminal and non-terminal robots move to break initial collinearities. During Phase II and Phase III , only non-terminal robots move to break newly created collinearies, including the cyclic dependencies, and during Phase IV , only terminal robots move to break newly created collinearities with faulty non-terminal robots. 

Phase I (Both terminal and non-terminal robots move): Let ri, rj , rk, rl and rm be collinear robots on a line L such that robots ri and rm are terminal, other robots are non-terminal, rj is adjacent to ri, and rl is adjacent to rm. As the terminal robots move first, initially only ri and rm are eligible to move. Without loss of generality, let us consider the following order of activations of the robots: ri

is activated first, followed by rj , then rm is activated, followed by rl, and lastly rk is activated. Upon activation, robot ri turns its light moving-1 and moves. The moving-1 keeps the information that robot 

ri has made its initial move. In the next activation, robot ri finds itself as the terminal robot with light 

moving-1 and changes its light to moved-T-1 to let others know that it is currently a terminal robot that has made its initial movement. Now, after the initial movement of ri, robot rj may become terminal or may remain non-terminal (due to a fault in ri). If rj becomes terminal, then it acts like ri. Otherwise, 

rj finds one of its friends with light move-T-1 (ri and rk are friends of rj ) has light moved-T-1 . We allow robot rj to move while changing its light to moving-1 . If it becomes terminal, it changes its light to 

move-T-1 . Otherwise, it changes its light to move-NT-1 to indicate that it is currently a non-terminal robot that has made its initial movement. Now suppose that both rj and rl are initially faulty. According to the activation schedule and moving rule, when rk becomes active, it finds both of its friends with light 

moved-NT-1 , this let rk know that all the collinear robots on L have moved. We allow the robot rk to move like a non-terminal robot. Now, a non-terminal robot may find one of its friends as moved-T-1 and another as moved-NT-1 . In this case also, we allow the non-terminal robot with the light off to move. During Phase I, every eligible robot computes its destination according to Section 3.1 and moves toward it. We summarize these movement strategies as follows: 

ri is a physically terminal robot: The robot ri finds itself as a physically terminal robot and 1. its light is off : it changes its light to moving-1 and moves. 92. its light is moving-1 : it changes its light to moved-T-1 and does not move. 

ri is a physically non-terminal robot: Robot ri finds itself as a physically non-terminal robot and 1. its light is off , and at least one friend robot in each friend-pair of ri has light moved-T-1 or 

moved-NT-1 : it changes its light to moving-1 and moves. 2. its light is moving-1 : it changes its light to moved-NT-1 and does not move. After completing its Phase I action, every robot has light either moved-T-1 or moved-NT-1 . A robot proceeds to its next logical stage according to its own light state and the states of the visible robots on which its subsequent action depends; no global Phase I completion test is required. The movements performed by the robots during Phase I satisfy several useful properties. For ease of presentation, we first state these properties, without proofs, as observations to facilitate understanding of the algorithm. 

Lemma 1. During the whole execution of Phase I, each non-faulty robot moves exactly once. Proof. A robot with light off performs at most one Phase I movement, because after moving it changes its light to moving-1 and then to either moved-T-1 or moved-NT-1 . To show existence, observe that terminal robots are eligible immediately, and their completed movements make the adjacent non-terminal robots eligible. Repeating this argument propagates eligibility from the two ends of every initial line towards its interior. By fairness, every non-faulty robot is eventually activated when eligible and hence performs exactly one Phase I movement. 

Lemma 2. During the whole execution of Phase I, at most two robots from the same initial line of collinearity move in a single round. Proof. According to movement strategies of Phase I, on any line of collinearity, eligibility propagates from its two terminal ends towards the interior. Initially, only the two terminal robots can move. After a robot completes its Phase I movement, at most its next non-terminal neighbor on that side can become eligible. Since all robots activated in an SSYNC round compute from the same pre-movement configuration, eligibility cannot propagate by more than one position from either end in the same round. Hence, at most two robots from a line of collinearity move in any round.

# 4 Conclusion 

We studied the mutual visibility problem under the budget-constrained mobility fault model for opaque luminous robots operating in the SSYNC model with non-rigid movements. Unlike existing algorithms, our solution neither relies on convex-hull decomposition nor assumes one-axis agreement. Instead, we showed that access to a common fixed external reference beacon is sufficient to coordinate the robots without requiring axis agreement. We presented the first deterministic non-convex-hull-based algorithm for this model that guarantees collision-free mutual visibility from every admissible initial robot configuration satisfying the reference-beacon assumption while respecting the prescribed movement budget and tolerating an arbitrary number of mobility faults. The algorithm uses only 12 light colors and terminates in O(n) epochs. Future work includes extending the proposed algorithm to the ASYNC model and investigating whether the mutual visibility problem can be solved under the budget-constrained mobility fault model without assuming a common fixed reference point and any form of local axes agreement.
