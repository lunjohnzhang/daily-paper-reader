# Energy-Aware Distributed Task Allocation via Local Submodular Maximization 

Jing Liu, Fangfei Li, Member, IEEE , Xin Jin, Member, IEEE , Yang Tang, Fellow, IEEE ,Ruggero Carli, Member, IEEE , and Luca Ballotta, Member, IEEE 

Abstract — This paper studies distributed task allocation under time-varying agent sets, task sets, and communi-cation graphs. At each decision epoch, the stage alloca-tion problem is formulated as monotone submodular max-imization over a time-varying, energy-filtered admissible ground set subject to a partition-matroid constraint. We develop a Distributed Adaptive Greedy Bidding Algorithm (DAGBA) that combines local admissible-set construction, conditional marginal bidding, and neighbor-based con-flict resolution under limited communication. We prove that DAGBA guarantees per-epoch feasibility, finite-round convergence, and residual-energy safety. Theoretically, we identify a sequential-greedy regime where DAGBA recov-ers the classical 1/2-approximation guarantee. For gen-eral executions with partial information, we derive a local-information-factor approximation bound and conservative bounds computable from the execution trace that quan-tify the degradation caused by local estimates and par-allel fixing. Simulations on cooperative observation mis-sions demonstrate that DAGBA achieves task-completion progress and utility close to centralized and complete-communication references, while improving communica-tion efficiency and maintaining strict energy feasibility. 

Index Terms — Open multi-agent systems (OMAS), distributed task allocation, submodular maximization, resource-constrained coordination.

In these applications, agents must repeatedly coordinate to accomplish spatially and temporally evolving tasks, including target tracking, area coverage, and information gathering. In practice, the participating agent set, the active task set, and the communication topology may all change over time due to mobility, failures, energy depletion, and asynchronous task arrivals and completions [5]– [12]. These time-varying features challenge the assumptions underlying many classical distributed coordination methods, which typically require fixed agent teams, stable communica-tion networks, and sufficient time for bidding or consensus to stabilize [13]–[20]. Recent studies on dynamic multi-agent coordination have made important progress in distributed optimization, online re-source allocation, and open multi-agent systems (OMAS) [5]– [12]. However, these works primarily address continuous-valued optimization problems with convex or linear objectives. The task allocation problem studied in this paper is combi-natorial in nature. Its admissible decisions are discrete agent-task pairs, the utility is nonadditive due to cooperative sensing effects, and feasibility is coupled with local motion constraints and residual energy budgets. In particular, assigning additional agents to the same task often improves observation or tracking performance but with diminishing marginal benefit, a structure naturally formalized by monotone submodularity [21]–[23]. The present problem introduces several difficulties that are not simultaneously captured by existing distributed submod-ular maximization frameworks. First, the admissible ground set is time-varying: at each decision epoch, candidate agent-task pairs depend on the currently active agents, active tasks, residual energies, and motion-feasibility conditions. Second, each agent carries a finite onboard energy reserve over the mis-sion horizon. Energy consumed at the current epoch reduces the residual energy available at subsequent epochs and may therefore restrict future admissible ground sets. Third, the com-munication graph determines which bidding and confirmed-assignment information is locally visible to each agent. Under neighbor-limited communication, different agents may main-tain different estimates of the accepted assignment, so the exact centralized greedy sequence used in classical matroid-constrained submodular maximization cannot in general be enforced. Online optimization methods such as regret mini-mization, bandit allocation, and budgeted utility maximization [24]–[26] address submodular objectives in isolation, but they arXiv:2611.34293v1 [cs.MA] 25 Nov 2026 2 GENERIC COLORIZED JOURNAL, VOL. are not designed for this communication-limited, multi-agent setting with cross-epoch energy coupling. Existing distributed submodular maximization frameworks face the same limitation from a different angle: consensus-based bundle methods [17], [20] and distributed sequential greedy algorithms [21], [27], [28] typically assume a static agent set or a stable communica-tion structure, while continuous-relaxation methods attaining the tight (1 − 1/e )-approximation of the multilinear extension [29], [30] rely on oracle access and multiple rounds of gradient-consensus iterations per decision epoch, rendering them computationally prohibitive for per-epoch deployment in time-critical missions. Motivated by these observations, this paper studies dis-tributed task allocation under time-varying feasibility, residual-energy constraints, and local communication. At each decision epoch, the currently available information defines a stage allocation problem formulated as monotone submodular maxi-mization over an energy-filtered admissible ground set subject to a partition-matroid constraint. Since future agent arrivals, task completions, and communication changes are unknown at decision time, we evaluate the proposed algorithm against the offline optimum of the same-epoch problem, which provides a natural and tractable per-epoch benchmark. To solve the stage problem, we develop the Distributed Adaptive Greedy Bidding Algorithm (DAGBA). Even against this per-epoch benchmark, establishing an approximation guarantee is nontrivial. Under neighbor-limited communication, each agent maintains only a local estimate of the accepted assignment and multiple pairs may be fixed in parallel, deviating from the exact sequential greedy order required by the classical 1/2-approximation [31]. Our contributions are as follows: 1) We formulate distributed task allocation in OMAS with time-varying agent sets, task sets, and communication graphs as a sequence of stagewise monotone submodular maximization problems over time-varying, energy-filtered admissible ground sets subject to partition-matroid con-straints. Unlike recent dynamic multi-agent optimization methods that address continuous-valued convex objec-tives [8]–[10], [12], this formulation simultaneously cap-tures the combinatorial decision structure, cooperative di-minishing returns, neighbor-limited communication, and cross-epoch coupling through residual energy constraints. 2) We propose DAGBA, a distributed greedy coordination algorithm based on local admissible-set construction, conditional marginal bidding, and neighbor-based conflict resolution. The central design challenge is to realize the greedy marginal-selection principle without a global co-ordinator: because the marginal gain of each candidate de-pends on which agents have already been assigned, every agent must bid against a locally maintained assignment estimate that is generally neither identical to nor syn-chronized with those of other agents. Unlike consensus-based bundle methods [17], [20] that require a stable agent set for bidding to converge, DAGBA accommodates time-varying agent and task sets by reconstructing local admissible subsets and local assignment estimates at each decision epoch, without requiring global consensus. 3) We establish per-epoch feasibility, finite-round conver-gence, and residual-energy safety of DAGBA, and de-rive per-epoch approximation guarantees. In a sequential-greedy regime, DAGBA recovers the classical 1/2-approximation with a curvature-dependent refinement. For general local-information executions, we derive a local-information-factor approximation bound and con-servative bounds computable at runtime that quantify the degradation caused by stale local estimates and parallel fixing. The analysis is nontrivial because, under sparse communication, different agents evaluate marginal bids with respect to different local estimates and multiple pairs may be fixed in parallel, deviating from the exact sequential greedy order assumed by classical matroid-constrained submodular maximization analysis [31]. The proof requires a new charging argument that verifies local estimates are subsets of the final assignment, establishes the submodular charging inequality for arbitrary, not necessarily nested, pairs of assignments, and exploits the partition-matroid structure to prevent double-charging.

Section II formulates the per-epoch allocation as monotone submodular maximization over the energy-feasible ground set 

Ωt under the partition-matroid constraint St. Solving this problem in a distributed manner is challenging for three reasons. First, the admissible candidate space is time-varying because agents, tasks, residual energies, and motion-feasibility conditions all change across epochs. Second, due to submodu-larity, the marginal contribution of an agent-task pair depends on which agents have already been assigned to the same task, so bids cannot be treated as fixed edge weights [21], [22]. Third, under sparse and time-varying communication, no agent can maintain a globally synchronized view of the accepted assignment [27], [28]. To address these difficulties, we propose the Distributed Adaptive Greedy Bidding Algorithm (DAGBA) , a dis-tributed implementation of marginal-greedy submodular max-imization under local information constraints. At each inner round, agents locally evaluate conditional marginal gains, exchange only locally visible bidding information, resolve conflicts among agents that preselect the same task, and update their local views of fixed assignments. Thus, the bidding mechanism is the communication-efficient realization of the greedy marginal-selection rule [21], [22]. C. Results and Discussion 

Figure 1 summarizes the performance under the three problem scales. Figure 1a reports the distribution of the normalized average utility over 10 independent trials. DAGBA with sparse communication achieves utility distributions close to DAGBA with complete communication and CSG, while generally outperforming GCAA. This indicates that the pro-posed local marginal-gain bidding rule preserves high-quality stage allocation under limited communication and provides a utility advantage over the auction-type distributed baseline in the tested scenarios. Figure 1b shows the corresponding task-completion process. DAGBA under sparse communica-tion follows completion trajectories close to DAGBA with complete communication, CSG, and GCAA, whereas the random baseline progresses substantially more slowly. Hence, energy-feasible random selection alone is insufficient for ef-ficient mission execution. Marginal-gain-based coordination improves both normalized stage utility and finite-horizon task-completion progress. Figure 2 and Table I compare the communication efficiency of DAGBA and GCAA across the three problem scales. The metric measures the workload-normalized average utility obtained per communication edge and therefore captures the utility gained from each unit of communication connectiv-ity. DAGBA under sparse communication achieves higher communication efficiency than its complete-communication counterpart, showing that increasing the communication radius improves information availability but also introduces addi-tional communication edges that do not always yield propor-tional utility gains. Compared with GCAA, sparse DAGBA attains competitive or higher utility per communication edge across the tested scales. This indicates that the proposed local marginal-gain bidding mechanism uses limited communication more effectively and does not rely on dense connectivity to obtain high allocation utility. Figure 3 compares the average utility and the empirical utility ratio relative to CSG across different problem scales. The average utility directly measures the normalized stage allocation quality, whereas the empirical utility ratio reports the fraction of CSG utility recovered by each method under the same scale. A value close to one indicates that the method attains utility comparable to the centralized sequential greedy reference. DAGBA remains close to the CSG reference across the tested scales and generally achieves a higher ratio than GCAA. Figure 4 and Table II quantify the effect of communication sparsification. Reducing Rc substantially decreases the number of communication edges and the per-epoch allocation runtime. For moderate sparse communication, average utility and final completion rate remain close to the complete-communication reference. Figure 5 and Table III isolate the two main algorithmic ingredients. Removing the submodular marginal-gain rule sub-stantially reduces average utility and final completion rate, showing that DAGBA is not merely a generic bidding pro-tocol but relies on the greedy marginal-selection principle underlying Theorem 1 and Theorem 2. Removing the energy-aware admissible-set construction can keep utility high in some LIU et al. : ENERGY-AWARE DISTRIBUTED TASK ALLOCATION VIA LOCAL SUBMODULAR MAXIMIZATION 11 

runs, but it produces energy violations and therefore infea-sible assignments. This result is consistent with the energy-safety guarantee in Proposition 1. When the admissibility filter ̂ Cij,t ≤ Ei,t is enforced, all returned assignments satisfy Ei,t +1 ≥ 0. When it is removed, this guarantee is violated. Together, the full DAGBA combines marginal-gain-based coordination for high-quality assignments with energy-aware admissible-set construction for execution feasibility. 

VI. C ONCLUSION 

This paper developed DAGBA for energy-constrained dis-tributed task allocation under time-varying agent sets, task sets, and communication graphs. We established per-epoch feasibility, residual-energy safety, finite-round convergence, and approximation guarantees for both the sequential-greedy and local-information regimes. Simulation results demon-strated that DAGBA achieves competitive allocation utility and task-completion performance under sparse communication, while the ablation study verified the complementary roles of marginal-gain bidding and energy-aware admissible-set construction. Future work will investigate non-myopic energy planning and distributed task allocation under more general submodular objectives and communication constraints.
