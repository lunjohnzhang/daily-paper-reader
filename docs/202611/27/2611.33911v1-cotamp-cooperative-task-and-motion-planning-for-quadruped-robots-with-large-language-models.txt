# CoTAMP: Cooperative Task and Motion Planning for Quadruped Robots with Large Language Models 

Woojin Kim 1 and Jemin Hwangbo 1,∗

Abstract —Some environments cannot be traversed by a single quadruped robot, for example a ledge that one robot can climb only from its partner’s back or a gap that must be bridged with boxes. Solving them requires cooperative task and motion planning (TAMP). Previous works are limited to the motions of a single robot or only select actions from a predefined set of skills. We propose a framework with two parts: task planning that invents motions not defined in advance, and motion generation that realizes the invented motions. Both parts consist of large language model (LLM)-based modules and code-based modules. Task planning divides the trajectory of the whole task into steps and fixes the robot poses at the step boundaries. Motion generation produces keyframes that describe the motion of each step and turns them into a continuous trajectory through kinematic synthesis. The code-based modules check every plan and motion and return failures to the LLM-based modules for correction. The whole trajectory is then refined by trajectory optimization to satisfy the dynamics. On three simulated courses with two RAIBO2 robot models, our framework produced kinematically valid references in all 30 trials, all of which converged in trajectory optimization. Compared with several baselines, our framework showed advantages in success rate, dynamic feasibility, execution time, and generation time and cost. Index Terms —Legged robots, multi-robot systems, task and motion planning, large language models, trajectory optimization. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# TASK and motion planning (TAMP) has enabled legged robots to complete long-horizon tasks in cluttered envi-ronments, combining decisions about what to do with motions that respect contacts and geometry [1]. Many environments, however, cannot be traversed by a single robot: a ledge higher than its legs can reach, a gap between two platforms, or a path blocked by boxes that must first be pushed into place. Two robots can overcome such obstacles if one serves as a step (Fig. 1) or a bridge for the other, or if they rearrange objects together. Planning such behavior requires a cooperative TAMP that decides who supports whom and in which order, and at the same time where each foot lands on the partner’s back. Existing approaches to TAMP fix the set of available actions in advance. Classical TAMP requires hand-designed action schemas with preconditions and effects, and computes the motion of each action with a motion planner [2], [3]. Large language models (LLMs) have made it possible to plan cooperation directly from a description of the scene; Delfaki et

Recent work shows that LLMs can also generate motions directly, by writing trajectories or programs that produce them [12], [13]. These methods, however, generate the motion of a single robot and do not produce motions in which two robots physically support each other. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. LLM-Based Planning 

Classical TAMP combines discrete action search with con-tinuous feasibility reasoning [2], [3], but assumes hand-designed action schemas. LLM-based planners replace the symbolic search with a language model that selects learned skills [5], writes policy code [14], checks skill feasibility [6], or coordinates several manipulators [7]. For quadrupeds, Ouyang et al. [1] use LLM agents to turn long-horizon locomotion and manipulation tasks into code that calls a set of skills trained with reinforcement learning, and Delfaki et al. [4] let one robot help another over a high obstacle with 

GoTo , Sit , and Stand commands. All of these planners select actions from a set defined before planning. Our task planner instead describes each step’s motion freely, including motions in which two robots support each other. 

B. LLM-Designed Controllers 

A second line of work lets an LLM design the objective of a controller instead of the motion itself. Yu et al. [10] translate instructions into reward parameters for model-predictive con-trol, and Ma et al. [11] write reward functions for reinforce-ment learning. Wang et al. [15] propose tasks and rewards to learn new skills, and Huang et al. [16] compose value maps that a motion planner optimizes. Tang et al. [17] map language to foot-contact patterns that a learned locomotion controller executes. These approaches use rewards, value maps, or con-tact patterns to specify robot behavior, and they consider a single robot. Our focus is on generating mutually consistent contacts and whole-body trajectories for two quadrupeds that physically support one another. 

C. Direct Motion Generation 

Other works let a model output the motion directly. Kwon 

et al. [12] showed that LLMs can generate dense end-effector trajectories for manipulation zero-shot, and Nayak 

et al. [13] generate quadruped reference trajectories from prompts through planning, code generation, and repair. Other works use generative motion models to produce reference motions from text, human motion, or video that are then tracked by a quadruped controller [18]–[21]. None of these methods produces motions in which two robots physically interact, such as one robot climbing onto another whose back moves under the load, where the contacts of both robots must be generated consistently and the supporting robot’s motion depends on its partner. Our motion generator produces such coupled motions of both robots. III. S YSTEM OVERVIEW 

A. Problem Setting 

We use two RAIBO2 quadrupeds with 12 actuated joints each, distinguished by color (blue and red). An environment consists of static obstacles and movable boxes; each box slides along a rail with one degree of freedom and moves only when a robot pushes it. Each robot must reach the goal region marked in its own color, and the task is achieved when both robots stand inside their goal regions. The three courses used in this paper (Fig. 2) each contain obstacles that a single robot cannot overcome, so every course requires cooperative motions. Given the environment and the initial states, our framework generates whole-body trajectories that bring both robots to their goals while satisfying the dynamics.

Task planning consists of the task planner, an LLM module that writes the plan, and code-based modules that validate it. 

A. Inputs 

The task planner receives  

> •

Environment: a JSON file with the environment and its obstacles.  

> •

Robot models: the URDF models of both robots.  

> •

Images: renderings of the initial state of the environment.  

> •

Output format: a JSON file that defines the output format.  

> •

Prompt: instructions on what the plan must respect. The full prompts are available on the project page. 

B. Plan Representation 

The task planner returns a plan consisting of  

> •

a step sequence per robot, where a step is either solo 

(the motion of one robot) or coupled (the motions of both robots, generated together, with one role each and a stated coupling reason);  

> •

a free-form behavior family per step;  

> •

precedence constraints between steps of different robots, i.e., a step may start only after a given step of the other robot has finished;  

> •

required object states and object coordinate changes per step;  

> •

the robot positions at the start and end of each step, at which the robots stand with nominal joints and a level base. No skill set is defined in advance: the task planner invents the motions each step needs. The prompt instructs the task planner to consider cooperative motions where an obstacle cannot be overcome by a single robot. 

C. Plan Validation 

The code-based modules check the plan in two ways: (1) structural checks verify that the step identifiers and robot assignments are consistent, that the precedence constraints contain no cycles, and that the boxes are moved in a consistent order; and (2) geometric checks place both robots and the boxes in the scene at every step boundary and verify that all four feet of each robot rest on a support, that nothing collides, and that both robots reach their goals at the end. The failed checks are returned to the task planner, together with renderings of the boundary states that fail a geometric check. The task planner revises the plan based on this feed-back, and once the plan passes, it becomes the input of motion generation. This validation covers only the boundary poses; whether the motion between them is feasible is checked during motion generation. V. M OTION GENERATION 

Motion generation consists of the motion generator, an LLM module that writes the motion of each step, and code-based modules that synthesize and validate it. The plan divides the long trajectory of the whole task into short steps. Because the start and end states of every step are fixed by the plan, the steps are independent of each other, so the motions of all steps are generated in parallel, which shortens the generation time.

B. Motion Representations 

The motion generator chooses one of two representations: 

Walking route. Ordinary walking is comparatively simple and can be generated reliably by rules. For a solo step that only walks or turns on connected horizontal surfaces, the motion generator therefore returns only body waypoints, and a predefined, rule-based walking trajectory generator turns them into a periodic gait at a reference speed of 0.8 m /s and 

1.4 rad /s.

Keyframes. For all other steps, the motion generator pro-duces the intermediate states of the motion in the form of keyframes. Each keyframe specifies, for the participating robots, the torso position and orientation, and the world coordinates and contact label of each foot. Keyframes are generated only at the intermediate points of the motion where all four feet are in contact. The code-based modules solve the joint angles of every keyframe with inverse kinematics, and check joint limits, foot support, and collisions. 

C. Kinematic Synthesis Interpolation. The torso position and orientation are inter-polated with cubic Hermite curves through the keyframes. Stance feet are kept fixed to their supports, and moving feet are interpolated to swing along half-elliptical paths. When a robot pushes a box, the box moves according to the motion of the link in contact with it. 

Timing. The time interval between consecutive keyframes is set adaptively from the body displacement and the swing distance of the feet within the interval. This keeps a natural motion speed throughout the trajectory. 

Kinematic optimization. Interpolation between valid keyframes can still violate constraints in between, for example when a swing foot grazes a step edge, an intermediate pose is unreachable, or a knee enters the partner’s body. In that case, the code-based modules set an interval around the failed part and generate a path that avoids collisions and other violations through B-spline-based optimization (Fig. 4). 

D. Validation, Correction, and Replanning 

The synthesized trajectory is checked every 10 ms for joint limits, foot support, collisions, and support-edge margins; the dynamics are handled later by the TO stage (Sec. VI). When a check fails, the failed items are returned to the mo-tion generator together with renderings of the failed keyframes or trajectory segments. The motion generator then regenerates the keyframe sequence; this correction is repeated at most twice within the same session. If a step still fails after two corrections, the plan itself may have specified a motion that cannot be realized, for example a single robot that is planned to climb an obstacle higher than it can reach on its own. In that case repeating motion generation does not help, so the failure and its diagnostics are returned to the task planner, which revises the plan, for instance by replacing the solo climb with a cooperative motion in which the partner provides support; this replanning is performed at most once. VI. T RAJECTORY INTEGRATION 

The validated step motions are integrated into one trajectory for the whole task in two steps. 

Composition. Each step trajectory is generated separately, so the step trajectories are placed on one shared timeline to form the trajectory of both robots for the whole task (Fig. 5a). Each robot executes its own steps one after another without pause, and it waits only in two cases: when a precedence constraint requires a step of the other robot to finish first, and when its next step is a coupled step and the partner is not yet ready. While waiting, the robot stands still in its last pose. 

Trajectory optimization. The composed kinematic reference fixes the contact sequence and serves as the initial guess of a TO over both robots, formulated with Pinocchio [22] and CasADi [23] and solved with IPOPT [24]. The TO finds a trajectory that stays close to the reference while satisfying the constraints of both robots and the pushed boxes. The constraints include the articulated rigid-body dynamics, contact forces within friction cones, the reciprocal forces between robots in contact and between robots and boxes, joint and torque limits, and collision avoidance. The boxes are modeled as passive bodies sliding along their rails. Because a long trajectory has too many variables to be solved at once, the course is divided into time windows that are solved in sequence, and consecutive windows are joined by matching their boundary conditions. VII. E XPERIMENTAL SETUP 

A. LLM Settings 

All LLM modules use Codex CLI (version 0.167.2) [25] with gpt-6.1-sol at medium reasoning effort. Our pro-grams call Codex in its non-interactive mode ( codex exec ), with approval requests disabled and file writes restricted to the module’s workspace, and correction requests resume the same session. Within its workspace, a module can freely write and run code while solving its part, and it returns a JSON file in a prescribed format. Each call is additionally wrapped in a bubblewrap sandbox [26] that hides everything outside the module’s workspace, so a module cannot read the repository, the code that checks it, or the results of other steps and trials. Every trial starts fresh sessions; trials do not share responses or generated artifacts, and all inputs are frozen and verified by hashes.

C. Metrics Kinematic success means that the composed reference passes the dense validation of Sec. V-D and both robots reach their goals. TO convergence means that, for a kinematically successful reference, the TO of Sec. VI converges to atrajectory that satisfies its constraints. Convergence suggests that the generated reference is dynamically plausible. The generation time is the wall-clock time from the start of a trial until a valid kinematic reference exists, excluding the TO. The steps are generated in parallel, so the motion generation stage counts as the duration of its longest step. Cost is computed from the token counts at OpenAI’s published standard-tier API prices for gpt-6.1-sol : $2.00, $0.10, and $10.00 per million uncached input, cached input, and output tokens [27]. To show that our framework is better than simply calling an LLM, we set up a minimal baseline, Barebone . It receives the same inputs as the task planner (Sec. IV), but with only a minimal prompt, it generates the full trajectory of the general-ized coordinates of both robots and all boxes in a single LLM call. Table II compares the success rates of our framework (Ours) and Barebone. Barebone produced kinematically valid trajectories in 6/10, 7/10, and 10/10 trials on the three courses. E. Effect of the Kinematic Optimization (Q5) 

Q5 examines how the optimization in the kinematic synthe-sis affects the success rate of the interpolated reference. For the keyframes of the six steps of Q4, we run the kinematic synthesis twice, once without the optimization (interpolation only) and once with it, and compare how often the dense validation passes. Table IV shows that the synthesis without the optimization succeeded in 30/60 cases and the synthesis with the optimization in 60/60. The effect concentrates on steps with height changes and robot–robot contact: on the cooperative and platform ascents, the synthesis without the optimization never succeeded, failing with IK failures and penetrations, whereas the maze walk and the box push were already valid. This shows that the kinematic optimization is highly effective for difficult motions with many contacts and a high risk of collision. IX. D ISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

The results show the benefit of letting the LLM modules and the code-based modules each do what they do best. The LLM modules make the high-level decisions, the plan and a few keyframes per step, while the code-based modules enforce the dense consistency through interpolation, optimization, and validation; Q4 and Q5 show the benefit of this split, and Barebone (Q2) shows what happens without it. Because the plan fixes the start and end states of every step, the steps are independent and their motions can be generated in parallel (Q1), and the solo/coupled decomposition additionally short-ens the execution (Q3). Finally, kinematic validity does not imply dynamic feasibility: 13 of the 23 kinematically valid trajectories of Barebone did not converge in TO, whereas all references of our framework converged. Checking the dynamics with TO is therefore necessary. The framework also has limitations. It currently handles only two robots, and cooperation among more robots would require coupled steps that involve more than two of them. Parallel generation greatly reduces the generation time, but obtaining a kinematic reference still takes about 15 min per course, mostly spent in LLM calls, which limits its use for fast replanning. Moreover, we have evaluated the framework only on three simulated courses, so its behavior in more diverse environments and on real hardware remains to be validated. X. C ONCLUSION 

We presented a framework for cooperative task and motion planning of two quadruped robots. LLM modules make the high-level decisions, namely the plan and the keyframes of each step, and code-based modules handle validation, inter-polation, kinematic optimization, and composition. On three cooperative courses, our framework produced a kinematically valid trajectory in all 30 trials, taking about 15 min per course. A single LLM call that generates the whole trajectory rarely succeeded on the same courses. By reducing the time the robots wait for each other, the solo/coupled decomposition shortened execution time by approximately 25–43% compared with the All-coupled baseline. Compared with generating dense trajectories directly, keyframes achieved the same suc-cess rate while reducing generation time by approximately 38% and cost by approximately 41%. The kinematic opti-mization raised the success of the kinematic synthesis from 30/60 to 60/60, mainly on motions with height changes and many contacts.
