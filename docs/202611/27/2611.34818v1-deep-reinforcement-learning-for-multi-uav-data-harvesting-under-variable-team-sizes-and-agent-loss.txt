# Abstract 

Collecting data from wireless sensors can be chal-lenging when limited communication infrastruc-ture prevents them from offloading their measure-ments. In such settings, unmanned aerial vehi-cles (UAVs) can exploit their mobility to harvest data locally, while coordinated UAV teams can pro-vide efficient and redundant coverage of remote ar-eas. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) enables such teams to learn decentralized coordi-nation policies. However, training separate policies for different team and sensor populations is costly, and fixed-population policies may be vulnerable to agent failures. This work proposes F-MADDPG, a flexible variant of Multi-Agent Deep Determin-istic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) that supports data harvesting with varying numbers of wireless sensors and UAVs. The framework also accom-modates unexpected catastrophic failures that per-manently remove agents during a mission. We compare F-MADDPG with a MADDPG baseline that uses multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) and eval-uate training strategies based on team-size sam-pling and exposure to agent terminations. The re-sults show that F-MADDPG learns across the eval-uated population sizes, completes missions faster than the baseline on average, benefits from inverse-cardinality team-size sampling compared with uni-form sampling, remains effective after permanent agent losses, and gains no consistent benefit from exposure to failures during training. 

Keywords: Distributed systems, data harvesting, re-inforcement learning, fault tolerance

# 1 Introduction 

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) provide a mobile platform for tasks performed over large or difficult-to-access areas [5, 20, 21, 17]. When deployed as a team, they can divide spatially distributed work and continue operating without relying on permanent infrastructure. One relevant applica-tion is data harvesting from wireless sensor net-works: UAVs travel between ground sensors and collect their measurements through short-range air-to-ground communication [3, 4, 8]. Using a UAV swarm can shorten a mission, but they must coordi-nate their actions to complete the task efficiently, avoiding redundant sensor visits and distributing the remaining workload. Designing coordination rules by hand becomes difficult as the number of UAVs and sensors in-creases and as their spatial configuration changes. Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) offers an alternative in which coordinated behavior is learned through interaction with a simulated en-vironment. Deep reinforcement-learning methods have consequently been applied to multi-UAV data collection, jointly addressing decisions such as tra-jectory planning, sensor selection, and task alloca-tion [11, 26, 27, 14]. The flexibility obtained through learning comes at the cost of an expensive training process. Deep reinforcement-learning policies may require mil-lions of environment interactions, and a policy trained for one scenario configuration may perform poorly when deployed under substantially different conditions. Training a separate model for every possible team size in a multi-agent problem would multiply this cost and require the system to main-1arXiv:2611.34818v1 [cs.LG] 25 Nov 2026 tain and select among several population-specific policies. A single policy capable of operating across team sizes can instead reuse training across config-urations and makes the learned controller easier to deploy. Population variability is also a fault tolerance concern: UAVs may become unavailable because of energy depletion, communication problems, ad-verse weather, or equipment failure. Consequently, solutions that can adapt to dynamic agent popula-tions are better equipped to handle such conditions. A permanent failure during an episode abruptly changes the number of participating agents and requires the survivors to redistribute unfinished work. However, conventional MARL implemen-tations commonly represent inputs using fixed-dimensional vectors tied to a predefined number of agents. Merely filling an absent agent’s slot with a fixed value does not explicitly distinguish it from valid input and does not eliminate the relationship of the network architecture with a maximum pop-ulation. In our previous work [9] we proposed two algo-rithms that addressed population variability. The first was successful in producing a model that works across a range of sensor populations. The second attempted the same for UAV populations, but ul-timately failed to achieve consistent performance across team sizes. We now propose F-MADDPG, a flexible variant of Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG) [16] for cooperative UAV data har-vesting under variable team sizes and permanent agent losses. F-MADDPG integrates a Flexible En-coder into a shared decentralized actor and central-ized critic. The encoder uses MLP heads for fixed-size fields and masked attention heads for variable-cardinality fields, producing a fixed-size representa-tion for each field. Explicit validity masks exclude absent agents and unavailable sensors, allowing one policy to operate across the evaluated populations. We evaluate F-MADDPG in a simulated data-harvesting task with varying numbers of UAVs and sensors and with stochastic, permanent UAV losses. Our experiments compare F-MADDPG with a MADDPG baseline that uses multilayer per-ceptrons (MLPs) in terms of learning efficiency and final task performance. We also formulate an inverse-cardinality strategy for sampling initial team sizes during training and compare it with uniform sampling to evaluate its effect on perfor-mance across team sizes. Finally, we compare poli-cies trained with different failure probabilities to investigate whether explicit failure exposure pro-vides benefits beyond training across initial team sizes. F-MADDPG learns effective policies across the evaluated configurations, completes missions faster than the MLP baseline on average, and ben-efits from inverse-cardinality team-size sampling. For each policy, performance remains similar across the evaluated failure probabilities. Exposure to failures during training does not consistently im-prove performance. 

# 2 Related Work 

Multi-agent reinforcement learning has been ap-plied to UAV data harvesting to coordinate move-ment and collection decisions under objectives such as energy efficiency and information freshness [11, 26, 27, 14]. These studies address important op-erational constraints, including limited energy and communication range. A further question concerns coordination across changing team sizes: the num-ber of UAVs may differ between missions, or the active team may change during a mission. Per-manent UAV loss is one important instance of the latter, requiring the surviving agents to complete work that would otherwise have been performed by the missing teammate. Ramezani et al. [19] enable surviving UAVs to continue a coverage mission after permanent losses by switching among population-specific configura-tions. This accommodates within-mission changes but requires training, maintaining, and selecting among several population-specific policies. Our prior work [9] explored training models with a fixed team size and deploying them in scenarios with different UAV counts. In our experiments, this strategy was effective when the deployment pop-ulation was at least as large as the training popu-lation, although transfer to larger teams incurred a completion-time penalty. Authors in [2, 22] repre-sent a rectangular collection area as a discrete grid, absorbing population variability into a task-specific spatial representation. They explore varying pop-ulations between episodes but not within episodes. The spatial representation imposes restrictions on the shape of the environment, limits the spatial res-2olution with which UAV positions are represented, and requires a task which can be represented in a spatially structured manner. Looking beyond data harvesting, the cooperative MARL literature offers several strategies for accom-modating varying agent populations. Some studies test transfer between fixed training and deployment populations without changing the underlying archi-tecture [10]. Such transfer is useful when local ob-servations remain comparable, but it does not ad-dress recovery from agent losses. Other methods construct bounded local views. K-nearest MARL [12] forms fixed-size neighborhoods within a larger variable population. Tong et al. [24] use a spatial representation, similar to those discussed above, for cooperative patrolling, evaluating continued oper-ation after catastrophic agent failures and subse-quent additions. Their shared actor incorporates teammate locations into a fixed-size map, while their centralized critic retains explicit team infor-mation padded to a maximum training population. In the broader reinforcement learning litera-ture, some authors explore neural architectures that explicitly accommodate variations in agent population. Long et al. [15] propose apopulation-invariant MADDPG architecture with two attention-based aggregation stages in the critic. The first aggregates observation entities to form each agent’s observation–action embedding, while the second aggregates agent embeddings into a fixed-size representation for the centralized critic. The actor also uses attention over observation entities. F-MADDPG instead centralizes corre-sponding observation and action fields before field-specific encoding and shares parameters across agents rather than maintaining independently pa-rameterized agents. Long et al.’s main objec-tive is to scale MARL training to larger popu-lations through Evolutionary Population Curricu-lum (EPC). Across stages of increasing popula-tion size, EPC combines candidate agent sets, fine-tunes them, and selects those that adapt best to the enlarged population. The reported experiments show better performance at larger populations than training from scratch, and an extrapolation test de-ploys cloned policies at twice the largest training population without further training. Some of the experiment environments also include game-driven agent deaths, although operational fault tolerance is not the study’s focus. Our work investigates a shared policy across team sizes and continued UAV data harvesting after permanent agent losses. Self-attention is also used in the MA-POCA al-gorithm [7], which was designed for cooperative en-vironments in which agents can spawn or termi-nate during an episode. Its centralized value and counterfactual-baseline networks aggregate infor-mation from currently active agents, avoiding ex-plicit absorbing-state inputs for terminated agents. The authors also address posthumous credit as-signment, which allows an agent’s earlier decisions to receive credit from team rewards obtained af-ter its termination. Our work also investigates attention-based aggregation of variable-cardinality inputs, applying it to both the actor and critic. Our experiments did not reveal evidence that posthu-mous credit assignment was a practical issue in the evaluated data-harvesting setting. Finally, Alarcon Granadeno et al. [1] use recur-rent neural networks to process observations from a variable number of teammates in a multi-UAV plume-tracing task. Their Action-Specific Dou-ble Deep Recurrent Q-Network (ADDRQN) archi-tecture combines separate embeddings of the act-ing UAV’s current observation and previous ac-tion with a fixed-size teammate representation pro-duced by a gated recurrent unit (GRU). These em-beddings are passed to a long short-term mem-ory (LSTM) network, followed by an output net-work estimating Q-values for seven discrete ac-tions. All UAVs share the same network param-eters. Whereas the teammate GRU accommodates population variability, the temporal LSTM main-tains memory under partial observability. Our method instead uses attention-based entity encod-ing within a continuous-action actor–critic formu-lation. Their described training randomizes ini-tial team sizes without explicit within-mission fail-ure injection, and evaluation includes permanent UAV dropout and communication blackouts. Our experiments focus on permanent losses and ad-ditionally compare different training-time failure-exposure conditions. These studies also address how different team sizes are represented during learning. Alarcon Granadeno et al. partition replay experience into team-size buckets and uniformly select a bucket be-fore sampling training sequences, while Tong et al. [24] allocate additional parallel training episodes to single-agent teams. Our inverse-cardinality 3strategy instead changes the initial-team sampling distribution to compensate for larger teams con-tributing more agent-level experience per envi-ronment step. This concerns experience collec-tion, whereas ADDRQN’s buckets act during re-play sampling. Episode duration and agent losses also affect the amount of collected experience, so initial-team sampling alone does not guaran-tee a balanced final distribution. In Section 5.2, we compare inverse-cardinality sampling with uni-form initial-team sampling and an adaptation of ADDRQN’s bucketed replay strategy within F-MADDPG. These experiments do not compare the complete F-MADDPG and ADDRQN algorithms. Overall, these studies highlight two related con-cerns: training policies that generalize to variable agent populations and adapting to within-episode changes in active agent count. Our evaluation brings these concerns together in UAV data har-vesting by examining the training efficiency of our proposed actor–critic representation, the distribu-tion of initial team sizes during training, and expo-sure to permanent within-mission losses.

# 3.2 Flexible Encoder 

Conventional deep reinforcement-learning net-works typically flatten observations into fixed-dimensional vectors. Padding variable entity sets to a maximum size provides regular tensors, but an 4MLP cannot inherently distinguish padding from valid values or model interactions among the enti-ties. The Flexible Encoder instead decomposes an ob-servation o into H semantically distinct fields. As shown in Figure 1, a field-specific head fh maps the input xh to a fixed-size representation. For variable-cardinality fields, the head also receives a validity mask bh that identifies the valid entries in 

xh. The resulting representations are concatenated and mixed: 

zh = fh(xh, b h) ∈ Rnh ,E(o) = fmix (z1 | · · · | zH ) ∈ Rnmix , (4) where | denotes concatenation and bh is omit-ted for fixed-size fields. Consequently, the encoder output dimension is independent of the number of valid entities. The encoder does not prescribe a re-inforcement learning update rule. Its output can be used wherever a conventional neural network would otherwise consume a flattened observation. The MLP heads encode fixed-size fields such as scalars or single-entity feature vectors. Masked at-tention heads encode variable-cardinality fields by projecting each element to the Transformer em-bedding dimension, applying self-attention using bh

to exclude padding, and performing masked mean pooling. If bh contains no valid elements, the head returns the zero vector. Padding therefore affects neither attention nor pooling. Entity identity em-beddings may be added before attention when en-tity roles must be retained. The pooled represen-tation is independent of the ordering of entities. In the data-harvesting implementation, the ac-tor uses an MLP head for the UAV identifier and masked attention heads for the relative positions of sensors and other UAVs. Their validity masks, de-scribed in Section 3.4, exclude collected or nonex-istent sensors and inactive or nonexistent agents. # 5 Results 

# 5.1 Training Efficiency 

Training a deep reinforcement learning model can be highly resource intensive, depending on the com-plexity of the environment and model architecture [18, 25]. Consequently, improving sample efficiency can substantially reduce training costs. The ap-proach introduced in Section 3.2 uses specialized heads for each observation field, potentially increas-ing training efficiency. To evaluate training efficiency, we compare F-MADDPG with a MADDPG baseline that uses an MLP to process all observations. Because this baseline cannot mask inactive observation entries or agents, these entries are padded with a fixed value of −1. Both models use inverse-cardinality team-size sampling and are trained without agent failures. Both of these details will be explored in sections 5.2 and 5.3, respectively.

An episode is considered successful if all sensors are visited within the allotted time. The models were periodically evaluated by pausing parameter updates and running 100 episodes with uniformly sampled agent counts, sensor counts, and sensor lo-cations. Both F-MADDPG and the baseline achieved near-100% success rates by the end of the 25 million training steps. The first sequence of three consec-utive evaluations with at least 90% success began at 5.3 million training steps for F-MADDPG and 9.8 million for the baseline. These results suggest that F-MADDPG learned to process the variable-cardinality observation fields more quickly than the baseline. # 5.2 Team Size Sampling 

Our previous work found that policies trained with larger UAV teams would perform poorly when de-ployed with fewer agents [9], but even policies trained with smaller teams still suffer from task performance degradation. Here, we expose mod-els to all initial team sizes during training and in-vestigate how the per-episode team size sampling distribution affects performance. Because F-MADDPG uses parameter sharing and all agents are homogeneous, each step involving

# 5.3 Robustness to Agent Failures 

As discussed in Section 5.2, reinforcement learning models generally struggle in situations not encoun-tered during training. F-MADDPG is designed to produce policies that are robust to changes in team size. # 6 Conclusion 

We introduced F-MADDPG, a multi-agent rein-forcement learning framework designed for cooper-ative tasks with variable team sizes and permanent agent losses. Its Flexible Encoder uses masked, field-specific representations to process variable-cardinality observations and centralized critic in-puts without treating absent entities as conven-tional values. Consequently, a single shared policy can operate across different UAV-team and sensor-population sizes and can continue a mission after failed UAVs are removed from the system. In the evaluated data-harvesting scenarios, F-MADDPG achieved competitive success rates while completing missions faster than the MLP-based baseline. Inverse-cardinality team-size sampling yielded higher aggregate success rates than uni-form team-size sampling with either ordinary or bucketed replay, with the largest gains observed for smaller teams. For each trained policy, perfor-mance remained similar across the evaluated failure probabilities. Exposure to failures during training yielded no consistent performance gains. This sug-gests that, in the considered setting, exposure to varying team sizes can be sufficient for learning behavior that remains effective after mid-episode agent terminations, without requiring failures to be explicitly introduced during training. An abla-tion study supports this hypothesis, as the variable-population policy outperformed a fixed-population baseline in scenarios with increasing probability of agent losses. Moreover, we demonstrate that both F-MADDPG and the MLP baseline generalize well to scenarios with agent populations larger than those observed during training, although the ob-servation structure of the data-harvesting scenario facilitates this. These findings are limited to a simulated en-vironment with simplified UAV dynamics, glob-ally available team information, and fail-stop fail-ures. Future work should consider communication-constrained observations, more realistic mobility and energy models, and other failure modes, in-cluding intermittent communication and degraded rather than complete loss of functionality. Evalu-ating F-MADDPG in additional cooperative tasks and physical UAV deployments would further clar-ify the generality and practical robustness of the proposed architecture. The Flexible Encoder can also be extended to represent other input represen-tations, such as spatial or image data.
