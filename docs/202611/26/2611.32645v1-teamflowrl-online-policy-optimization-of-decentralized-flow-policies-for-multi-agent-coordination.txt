Abstract 

Complex cooperative tasks require coordinated multi-agent behavior. Offline generative policies learn ex-pressive cooperative behaviors from trajectories but remain limited by fixed datasets. Online reinforcement learning can improve them through environment interaction. However, fine-tuning flow policies is difficult because action likelihoods are hard to compute. We propose TeamFlowRL, a two-stage framework that pre-trains multi-agent flow policies offline and fine-tunes them online. We formulate team policy optimization over the complete denoising trajectories of decentralized flow policies, avoiding intractable action likelihoods. The resulting objective weights each agent’s trajectory ratio with a shared team advantage and clips each ratio separately, aligning local policy updates with the common team return. Across 51 paired configurations in seven simulation groups, TeamFlowRL improves every offline initialization, with a 47.3% average gain in normalized score. It also achieves 33.1% higher average success under exploration noise than Gaussian-policy fine-tuning across 6 bimanual tasks. 

1 Introduction 

Cooperative multi-agent tasks, such as robotic manipulation and cooperative control, require agents to co-ordinate their decisions. Multi-agent reinforcement learning, or MARL, learns such coordination through 1arXiv:2611.32645v1 [cs.LG] 24 Nov 2026 interaction [14, 23, 32], while offline generative policies learn expressive action or trajectory distributions from fixed datasets [9, 34]. However, offline policies remain constrained by the quality and coverage of the available data [10]. Online reinforcement learning can further improve coordination through environment interaction and team rewards. Under centralized training and decentralized execution, existing policy-gradient methods typically use categorical distributions for discrete actions or unimodal Gaussians for continuous actions [4, 32]. Such parameterizations cannot represent separated action modes for the same observation, whereas offline flow policies can model richer multimodal behaviors. This motivates fine-tuning expressive offline flow policies with online team feedback. Extending single-agent flow policy methods to multi-agent flow policies faces two severe difficulties. Firstly, a flow policy has no tractable action probability. PPO-class methods need this probability to compute a ratio of the new policy and old policy on the same action for policy update and improvement [27]. As many denoising trajectories of a flow policy reach the same action, its log-likelihood is intractable and computationally impossible for multi-agents. Secondly, supposing the ratio can be obtained, the team update style of existing multi-agent methods, e.g., MAPPO, can no longer be applied to flow policies directly. In MAPPO, each agent updates its policy according to the ratio of its new and old policies by a factor of the shared team advantage [32]. In flow policies, many denoising trajectories of a flow policy can reach the same action, and the team advantage still evaluates the final action. Hence, the ratio together with the factor of team advantage is meaningful only for the final action but not a single trajectory. Even if a scheme uses the ratio between trajectories instead of between actions, it is unclear whether this scheme optimizes the team objective or not, since the relation between a trajectory and a final action is not one to one, but many to one. To address these challenges, we propose TeamFlowRL, a framework for online fine-tuning of offline-trained flow policies of multi-agents. Figure 1 illustrates this framework. We represent each agent’s policy through a stochastic denoising trajectory with tractable likelihoods. We then formulate a team objective that couples these local trajectories through a shared advantage from a centralized critic. Each agent’s complete trajectory ratio is clipped separately, rather than clipping the product of all agents’ ratios. With exact team advantages, this surrogate matches the gradient of the joint surrogate at the collection policy, up to normalization (Appendix A.4). This connects decentralized flow updates to team return optimization. We evaluate TeamFlowRL on 51 task--dataset configurations across five multi-agent benchmark suites and two bimanual manipulation simulators. Online fine-tuning outperforms the offline initialization in all 51 configurations, with an average gain of 47.3% in normalized score. We further compare TeamFlowRL with fine-tuning Gaussian policies. Behavioral analyses show that the fine-tuned flow policies capture multimodal action distributions and exhibit diverse cooperative behaviors. On six bimanual tasks, TeamFlowRL achieves 33.1% higher average success than BC+MAPPO under exploration noise. Our contributions are threefold: • To our knowledge, TeamFlowRL is the first framework for online RL fine-tuning of offline-pretrained multi-agent flow policies. • We formulate a team policy objective over decentralized denoising trajectories, combining a shared team advantage with separate clipping of each agent’s complete trajectory ratio. With exact advantages, the surrogate matches the gradient of the joint surrogate at the collection policy, up to normalization. • Across 51 configurations, TeamFlowRL consistently improves offline initializations and outperforms Gaussian policies in multimodal action expressiveness and online adaptation efficiency.

Online multi-agent reinforcement learning. Online MARL learns coordination through environment inter-action using centralized critics [4, 14, 18], value decomposition [23, 28, 30], or MAPPO’s centralized value estimates with agent-level PPO updates [32]. Offline pretraining and online fine-tuning. Online RL can improve pretrained generative policies by op-timizing their denoising processes. 3 Multi-Agent Flow Policy Optimization 

TeamFlowRL learns decentralized policies for cooperative tasks in two stages. Figure 2 illustrates the com-plete procedure. It first initializes a shared flow policy from offline input--action pairs. For online fine-tuning, we turn the flow generator into a stochastic policy over complete denoising trajectories, making the likelihood of each agent’s sampled trajectory tractable. A centralized critic then estimates a shared team advantage, which multiplies the agent-wise trajectory likelihood ratios in a PPO objective and jointly updates the shared velocity and noise networks. At deployment, each agent independently generates its action from local infor-mation using the fine-tuned velocity network.

4 Experiments 

We evaluate TeamFlowRL on standard multi-agent benchmarks and cooperative bimanual manipulation. The experiments measure improvement over the offline initialization across tasks and data qualities, examine the action distributions and cooperative behaviors of the fine-tuned policy, compare TeamFlowRL with Gaussian policies, and test the main implementation choices for the trajectory ratio.

In all six robot tasks, the final success under stochastic execution exceeds that of the pretrained initialization by 61.2% on average, as shown in Table 3. 4.5 Ablation Study 

We compare the complete denoising trajectory ratio with per-step clipping and ratio averaging with clamping on three representative configurations. The trajectory ratio achieves the best final performance in two of the three configurations and is never substantially worse than the alternatives. This result supports using the complete trajectory ratio as the default update rule, while detailed curves and configuration results are provided in Appendix C.2. 

5 Conclusion 

We introduced TeamFlowRL, a two-stage framework that pretrains multi-agent flow policies offline and fine-tunes them through online interaction. TeamFlowRL formulates team policy optimization over decentralized denoising trajectories, using a shared team advantage and separate clipping of each agent’s complete trajec-tory ratio to align local updates with the common team return. Across 51 configurations in seven simulation groups, online fine-tuning improves every offline initialization, with a 47.3% average gain in normalized score. The fine-tuned flow policies also represent multimodal action distributions and diverse cooperative behaviors, and achieve 33.1% higher average success under exploration noise than Gaussian-policy fine-tuning across six bimanual tasks. 

Limitations. Our manipulation evaluation is limited to simulated two-arm cube manipulation. Camera observations are used in only two tasks, and we do not evaluate physical robots. We use compact, task-specific policy networks and have not evaluated TeamFlowRL with large VLA models. Finally, offline data quality affects final online performance differently across tasks. We have not identified why lower-quality initializations catch up with higher-quality ones in some tasks but not others, as shown in Figure 3. 10 LLM Usage Statement

B Implementation Details 

B.1 Inputs, Action Map, and Update Control 

Inputs and initialization. The actor conditions only on its local input. Continuous control uses local obser-vation features with the identity encoding supplied by the environment adapter. For StarCraft, the actor conditions on local recurrent features from a pretrained encoder that remains frozen during online opti-mization. The online actor imports the teacher velocity network from the offline checkpoint. All reported configurations use K = 10 denoising steps and β = 0 in Equation 22. 

Action map. The actor stores the sampled denoising trajectory before any action transformation. The executed action is ai = clip( xi,K , −1, 1) for continuous control and the largest legal coordinate of xi,K for StarCraft. 

Valid trajectory ratio. Three implementation requirements make the ratio in Equation 7 valid. First, each denoising trajectory is sampled from the noisy transition chain of Equation 3 itself, and its log likelihood is stored at collection. The update recomputes the likelihood of the same stored trajectory, so ρit = 1 at the collection parameters, which we check at every update. Second, log ρit is the exact sum of the transition log probabilities over all K steps and action coordinates, without averaging or clamping. Third, each agent has 19 its own ratio, clipped against the shared team advantage. The stored trajectory is detached, so gradients enter the velocity and noise networks only through the conditional means and standard deviations at the stored prefixes. 

Update size and execution. Gradient clipping and a conditional trajectory KL check limit the update size: once the trajectory KL exceeds its threshold, the remaining actor updates of the iteration are skipped, and some tasks additionally use KL backtracking, described in Appendix B.2. Bounded transition means define a separate repair variant, described in Appendix B.2. Training samples with transition noise, and deployment follows the mean update.

D.1 Robot Manipulation Setup 

ManiSkill3 tasks. In TwoRobotPickCube, the cube starts within reach of the left arm only, and the goal position lies within reach of the right arm only, so the arms must pass the cube between them to lift it to the goal. An episode succeeds when the cube is within 0.025 m of the goal and the right arm is static. In TwoRobotStackCube, each arm can reach only one of two cubes. The right arm places its cube in a goal region on the midline between the robots, and the left arm stacks the second cube on top. An episode succeeds when the stacked cubes rest in the goal region and neither arm holds a cube. Each arm observes its own joint positions and velocities, its own end effector pose, the poses of the cubes, the goal position, the relative positions between these objects, the remaining time, and an agent indicator, but not the other arm. Object and goal poses are privileged information, because a physical robot would have to estimate them from its sensors, so the state results measure cooperative control with known object poses, and Appendix D.5 replaces these poses with camera observations. 

Pretraining and online training. Offline pretraining uses all successful demonstrations released with Man-iSkill3, which number 983 for TwoRobotPickCube and 1007 for TwoRobotStackCube, split into 90% for training and 10% for validation. Both methods start from the same pretraining run of 50,000 updates, in which a frozen recurrent encoder summarizes the observation history of each arm. TeamFlowRL starts from the ten-step flow policy. BC+MAPPO starts from a one-step policy distilled from the flow policy, with the latent fixed at zero so that the output is the mean of its Gaussian policy. Online fine-tuning runs for 5 million environment steps with 64 parallel environments, and each episode lasts 100 environment steps. 

RoboFactory. In TwoRobotsStackCube from RoboFactory, each arm receives its own joint and end effector state, the cube poses, the goal region, and an agent indicator, but not the other arm. Each action sets joint position changes for one environment step, and each episode lasts 100 steps. Training uses a staged dense reward that we define for reaching, grasping, lifting, aligning, and placing, normalized to a maximum of one, and success follows the evaluation function of RoboFactory. The demonstrations come from a PPO expert trained on the dense reward. Pretraining on the scripted motion planning demonstrations provided with RoboFactory gives 0% closed-loop success. Both methods start from the same pretrained checkpoint. Online fine-tuning uses the optimization settings of the ManiSkill state experiment for 2 million environment steps. 

Release and withdrawal. The main table and the RoboFactory learning curve report hands-away success, which additionally requires both arms to release the blocks and withdraw while the stack stands on its own. Appendix D.6 defines this variant.

D.3 Trajectories under the Deployment Rule 

Figure 11 shows the tool center point paths of both arms when each policy is run 31 times from one initial scene under its deployment rule. TeamFlowRL draws new initial latents in every run and adds no transition noise, and BC+MAPPO executes its Gaussian mean. To separate the variation caused by the simulator, we also run TeamFlowRL with the same latents in all 31 runs. We measure the spread of the paths as the root mean square distance of the tool center points from their mean over the runs, over the first 40 control steps and both arms, and average it over four MS Pick scenes and eight RF Stack scenes. On MS Pick, the spread is 1.6 cm for TeamFlowRL, 0.4 cm with the same latents, and 0.3 cm for BC+MAPPO. On RF Stack, it is 0.9 cm, 0.2 cm, and 0.3 cm. The latent sampling of TeamFlowRL varies the executed path several times more than the simulator alone, whereas BC+MAPPO follows a single path. The variation stays at the centimeter level around one route. At least 98% of the runs succeed in every group.

D.6 Learning to Release and Withdraw 

The hands-away variant of the two-arm stacking tasks in ManiSkill and RoboFactory requires the arms to release the blocks, withdraw, and leave the completed stack standing. An episode succeeds when the original task succeeds and, at the end of the episode, neither arm touches the blocks and both hands are away from them. In ManiSkill, training starts from the pretrained stacking policies and adds a reward term for withdrawal. In RoboFactory, the original demonstrations contain no withdrawal, so the flow policies are pretrained on new demonstrations that include it. The thresholds and remaining settings are provided in the released code. 27
