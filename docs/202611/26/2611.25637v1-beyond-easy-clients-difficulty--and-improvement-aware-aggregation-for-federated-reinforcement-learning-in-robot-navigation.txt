A B S T R A C T 

Federated reinforcement learning enables a fleet of robots to jointly learn a shared navigation policy without exchanging raw experience. In practice, however, the robots rarely operate in the same conditions: an open aisle is far easier to navigate than a cluttered room, and takes less learning to master. Standard federated aggregation rules ignore this. Uniform averaging weights every client equally, whether or not it is still learning, and weighting by episode return is worse, since easy environments permit high returns by construction. Both failures stem from what we call the plateau problem : once a client masters its environment, its updates carry little new information even though its reported return remains high. We propose FedDIA , which scores each client by how much its local training improves the global model it received, amplified by the difficulty of its environment. Difficulty is inferred from the model’s own performance, without requiring any description of the environment. Both signals are measured against the global model, so they are comparable across clients. On a mapless navigation task with eight heterogeneous clients, FedDIA reaches a mean success rate of 75 .5% across training and held-out environments, against 68 .7% for the strongest federated baseline. Its advantage holds when the models are transferred to hand-authored warehouse and living-space layouts, or when one of the clients is defective. 

## 1. Introduction 

Robots increasingly operate autonomously, from ware-houses and hospitals to homes and city streets. Reinforcement learning (RL) [1, 2, 3, 4] provides a way to learn the control policies they need through interaction rather than programming them in advance. Navigation is a common requirement in robotics: reaching a goal while avoiding obstacles. Learning it from onboard sensors [5, 6] avoids the hand-built maps and motion planners [7] that are hard to construct for unknown or changing environments. In practice, robots rarely operate under identical conditions: an open aisle, a cluttered room, and a narrow corridor each demand different behavior. A fleet deployed across such conditions spans environments of varying difficulty [8], and can improve faster by pooling experience than by learning alone [9]. With federated reinforcement learning (FRL) [10, 11], each robot or client trains locally and sends only its policy parameters to a server, which combines these updates into a shared policy. Since only parameters are shared, no client reveals the layout of its workplace. However, not every client’s update is equally valuable to the shared policy. A naive aggregation rule such as FedAvg [12] cannot distinguish whether a client is actively improving, and assigns equal weight to every update. A natural alternative is to weight each client by how well its policy performs [13, 14], treating a higher return as a sign of a better model. Yet this can make the problem worse. Clients in easy environments may master the task early in training, after which their local updates provide little new information even though their returns remain high. Weighting

## 2. Related Work 

2.1. Federated Reinforcement Learning 

Federated reinforcement learning enables multiple agents to collaboratively learn a shared policy while keeping their interactions and data decentralized. Zhuo et al. [10] intro-duced an early FRL approach in which two agents observe the same situations through different sensory windows and learn using a shared fusion head. Since then, FRL has been studied across a range of applications, with Qi et al. [11] providing a broad survey of the field. LFRL [9] and FGRL [15] investigate shared policy learning for robot navigation. Liang et al. [16] apply FRL to autonomous driving, while Chu et al. [14] study coordinated charging of plug-in electric vehicle fleets. In healthcare, Zhou et al. [17] learn treatment policies from offline clinical records. Tian et al. [18] extend FRL to predictive-maintenance policies for manufacturing. 

2.2. Data and Environment Heterogeneity 

In supervised federated learning, heterogeneity typically arises from non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) client datasets [12, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Common approaches address it through model personalization or local regularization to reduce client drift [24, 25]. In federated reinforcement learning, heterogeneity instead arises from differences in the environments in which clients interact. Although clients may share the same task, different environ-ment configurations lead to different dynamics and learning conditions. Jin et al. [8] show that averaging policies across heterogeneous environments converges to a solution whose suboptimality increases with the degree of heterogeneity. Existing approaches to environment heterogeneity can be broadly grouped into personalization, regularization, and aggregation. Our work follows the aggregation approach, focusing on how client updates are weighted at the server. 

2.3. Personalization 

The survey of Qi et al. [11] identifies personalized local updates as one approach to heterogeneous environments. Jin et al. [8] represent each environment with a low-dimensional vector while personalizing the embedding layers on a shared backbone. In FedFormer [26], no weights are averaged; instead, each agent learns contextual relationships with other agents to incorporate information relevant to its environment. Personalization therefore optimizes policies for individual environments, resulting in multiple client-specific policies rather than a single deployable policy [27, 28, 29, 30]. Applying the model to a new environment may require adapting its personalized component. In contrast, we target a single shared policy that clients improve collaboratively. 

2.4. Regularization 

Regularization constrains local training to limit how far a client’s update can drift from the global model [31, 32, 33]. FedKL [34] penalizes divergence from the global model’s output distribution, while FedProx [19], a representative ap-proach in supervised federated learning, penalizes divergence in the model weights. Both modify the local training process by adding a regularization term to the training loss. 

2.5. Aggregation 

Training a single shared policy raises the question of how client updates should be weighted during aggregation. Uni-form averaging (FedAvg [12]) weights every client equally and therefore ignores heterogeneity altogether. A natural refinement is to weight clients by measured performance. In supervised federated learning, FedLBW [35] and Fed-Nolowe [36] weight clients by validation loss. In the RL setting, ACWPA [13] weights clients using a softmax over their returns, while Chu et al. [14] combine reward and loss through an attention mechanism. Because these weights derive from absolute performance, however, they conflate environment difficulty with a client’s actual contribution. A client in an easy environment may maintain a high return and therefore a high aggregation weight even after its local training no longer improves the shared policy. Improvement-based weighting addresses part of this. FGRL [15] weights each client by the growth of its own performance between successive rounds. Yet this signal is self-referential in that each client’s growth is measured from its own prior model, giving clients different starting points. The resulting values are therefore not directly comparable across clients in different environments. Growth relative to oneself also carries no information about environment difficulty. Other approaches learn aggregation decisions or select clients. Using reinforcement learning, FedAA [37] derives aggregation weights from model-parameter distances and validation performance, while FAVOR [38] selects clients based on compressed model weights. FedPOHCS [39] instead scores and selects clients on their potential to improve and on how close their dynamics stay to the population average. To our knowledge, none of these methods weights clients by improvement measured on a common scale, or by how hard

3.2. The Plateau Problem 

As training progresses, clients in easy environments con-verge rapidly, and their local policies reach near-maximum performance early in training. Once saturated, these clients continue to produce high episode returns, but their local updates carry minimal new information. The global model already performs well in their environment, and further local training yields negligible improvement. We refer to this as the plateau problem : a client has plateaued when additional local training no longer meaningfully advances the shared policy, as seen for the easy client in Figure 2.

## 5. Experiments 

5.1. Experimental Setup 

Simulation Setup. We conduct all experiments in Isaac Lab [40, 41], a GPU-accelerated physics simulation frame-work. Each client controls a differential-drive robot navigat-ing a 5 m × 5 m square arena enclosed by solid walls. The task is mapless point-goal navigation in which the robot must reach a randomly placed goal using only its current sensor readings, with no map or prior knowledge of the environment. At the start of each episode, the robot and goal are initialized at random positions on opposite sides of the arena, with a uniformly sampled initial heading for the robot. The physics engine runs at 100 Hz, and the policy is executed once every four simulation steps, yielding a control frequency of 25 Hz (40 ms per decision step). An episode ends when the robot reaches the goal (within 0.20 m), collides with an obstacle

5.6. Robustness to a Defective Client 

A deployed fleet cannot assume every robot is working properly. For example, a drive motor may fail on one wheel, so that the commanded action is no longer the motion the robot executes. The model that client produces therefore drifts away from any useful policy. The client keeps training and reporting, and its update is aggregated along with every other client’s. We simulate such a failure by replacing one client’s actions with uniform random values during training. We use the default configuration of Section 5.1 and make one of the two grid clients defective. In a federated setting the server does not know which client is defective, so any down-weighting has to emerge from the aggregation rule itself. Table 5 reports success rate with the defective client present. FedDIA maintains the highest performance at 75 .6% .Reward-weighted aggregation discounts the defective client automatically by weighting on return, and therefore does better than FedAvg’s uniform weighting in this setting.

## 6. Conclusion 

We identified the plateau problem in heterogeneous federated reinforcement learning: once a client has mastered its environment, its updates carry little new information. Uniform averaging nonetheless keeps weighting it equally, and return-based weighting rewards it further. FedDIA scores each client by how much its local training improved the global model, amplified by the difficulty of its environment. Both signals are measured against the same global model, so they are comparable across clients, and require no description of the environment. Against baselines that also weight clients by measured performance, the extra cost is the evaluation of the global model before local training. FedDIA reaches a mean success rate of 75 .5% over five evaluation environments, against 68 .7% for the strongest federated baseline. Its advan-tage carries over to hand-authored warehouse and living-space layouts, evaluated without further training. FedDIA also holds its performance when a client is defective, and remains the best-performing method. The plateau problem can arise wherever federated clients face environments of varying difficulty. In future work, we plan to evaluate FedDIA on tasks where client difficulty comes from something other than obstacle layout.
