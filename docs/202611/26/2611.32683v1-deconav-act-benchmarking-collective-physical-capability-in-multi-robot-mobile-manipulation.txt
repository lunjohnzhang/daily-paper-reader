# DeCoNav-Act: Benchmarking Collective Physical Capability in Multi-Robot Mobile Manipulation

Robot teams can split workloads or combine complementary bodies, but only if each robot leaves the shared world in a state its teammate can use. Existing multi-robot benchmarks emphasize task allocation and skill sequencing, rarely testing whether the resulting physical states are usable. To evaluate collective physical capability , we introduce DeCoNav-Act, a benchmark for language-guided multi-robot mobile manipulation in a shared physics simulation. Its 350 homogeneous and 150 heterogeneous tasks, each verified by joint reference execution, span two coordination mechanisms: parallel workloads in shared space and physical dependencies in which one robot establishes a condition for a teammate to use. Beyond team success, DeCoNav-Act measures path efficiency and handoff coverage and success, and adds a diagnostic from verified pre-handoff states. Evaluating sensor-grounded adaptations of ASPIRE and RAX and our agent CoMoMa, we find that with GPT-5.6-luna no method completes a task, at most 8.8% of dependencies are reached, and even from pre-handoff states no handoff is completed; with GPT-6.1-sol, CoMoMa establishes the condition in 26.5% of trials, but only 4.1% succeed.

# 1 Introduction 

Household robots increasingly execute long-horizon tasks that couple language, navigation, and manipu-lation (Li et al., 2022; Yenamandra et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025), yet a single robot is bounded by its reach, payload, footprint, and time. Multiple robots can split a workload or combine different bodies: a tall manipulator retrieves a mug from a high shelf and leaves it where a smaller teammate can grasp it, or loads a carrier that transports several items at once. These benefits depend on physical execution: the robots share access to counters and passages, and a released object can settle out of the receiver’s reach. Team success therefore depends on collective physical capability : the ability of team members to change the shared world in ways their teammates can build on. Existing benchmarks cover parts of this problem. Multi-agent mobile manipulation benchmarks emphasize task allocation and sequencing through skill-level interfaces (Chang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Dong et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2024a; Puig et al., 2024). Physically executed dependencies are evaluated between fixed-base arms on a shared tabletop (Qin et al., 2025; Mandi et al., 2024), while single-robot mobile manipulation (Li et al., 2022; Yenamandra et al., 2023; Shukla et al., 2025) and collaborative navigation (Wang et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026) cover complementary aspects. These settings leave a gap in evaluating whether a physical state produced by one mobile robot is usable by its teammate in the same evolving scene. We introduce DeCoNav-Act, a benchmark for language-guided multi-robot mobile manipulation in a shared physics simulation (figure 1). Tasks are constructed around two coordination mechanisms. In parallel workload coordination , robots advance separate goals while sharing sources, destinations, and passages. In physical dependency coordination , one robot establishes a condition for a teammate to use, such as an object released within reach, a payload settled on a carrier, or a cleared route. Homogeneous teams split workloads and clear shared space for each other; heterogeneous teams add relays and carrier transport that build on their different reach, footprint, and payload. Current agents often fail at single-robot navigation or grasping before any cross-robot interaction occurs, so team success alone says little about coordination. DeCoNav-Act therefore evaluates at two levels. End-to-end episodes report team success together with per-subgoal path efficiency, the fraction of handoffs reached, and the handoff success rate once attempted. A handoff-isolated diagnostic starts from verified pre-handoff states, using a reference-producer control to locate failures in establishing usable conditions and completing the consumer’s continuation. We evaluate sensor-grounded, multi-robot adaptations of ASPIRE (Lu et al., 2026) and RAX (Huang et al., 2026a), with and without the scene prior used by CoMoMa, and CoMoMa, a reference agent with dependency-aware scheduling, shared spatial memory, and a skill library built during development. All methods use the same sensing, tools, and time budget, and each comparison uses one backbone model. End to end, no GPT-5.6-luna agent completes a task, and at most 8.8% of physical dependencies are reached. With GPT-6.1-sol and shared Oracle guidance, the handoff diagnostic distinguishes agent systems: CoMoMa achieves a DSR of 33.7%, compared with at most 14.3% for the baselines. Our contributions are: • DeCoNav-Act, a multi-robot mobile manipulation benchmark organized by coordination mechanism, with every task verified through joint physical execution; • a two-level evaluation whose handoff-isolated diagnostic restarts from physically verified states and localizes failures to establishing or using teammate-produced conditions; • an empirical study showing that current agents fail before coordination is tested and that the diagnostic separates coordination designs once low-level inputs are equalized.

# 3 Task Formulation 

We define an episode as 

e = ( s0, R, ℓ, α, G, D, C, B), (1) where s0 is the initial physical state, R the robot team, and ℓ a public language instruction. The instruction names objects, furniture, and rooms, any required handoff surface, and the completion conditions. The final goals G are object-level predicates, D are physical dependencies between actions such as grasps, placements, and traversals, C are validity constraints such as mutual exclusion at a shared counter, and B bounds simulated time, model calls, and tokens. The reference assignment α records which robot performs each action in the Oracle execution that admits the task (section 4.3); it serves construction and diagnosis and is withheld during end-to-end evaluation. An episode succeeds if, within B, its final state satisfies G and its trace satisfies 

D and C, whichever robot performs each action; allocation is thus part of team behavior (Chang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025, 2026). 

Physical dependencies and handoffs. A dependency d = ( p, ϕ, c ) ∈ D links a producer action p to a consumer action c through a physical condition ϕ; under α, different robots perform p and c. In a relay, the producer leaves the object on the handoff surface in a stable pose within the receiver’s reach, and the receiver grasps it. In carrier transport, the manipulator settles the payload in the carrier’s basket, and the carrier keeps it supported until arrival. In clearance, the producer clears a destination or passage for the consumer’s placement or traversal. We record establishment Ed (ϕ holds when the producer finishes and when the consumer starts) and valid use Ud (the consumer completes c while satisfying its task-specific physical requirements); d is satisfied when both hold, whichever robots act. A handoff is the use of ϕ by a robot other than the one that established it; if the producer also performs c, d is self-resolved , which counts toward team success but not toward handoff success (see section D.3) and gives up the team’s time advantage (section 4.2). The 

pre-handoff state s−

is the state just before the producer’s establishing action, e.g. , the producer holding the object next to the handoff surface; it anchors the handoff metrics (section 5.2) and the handoff-isolated diagnostic (section 5.3). 

Coordination mechanisms. Under the reference assignment α, parallel workload coordination arises when robots pursue separate object goals; they may still contend for shared sources, destinations, or passages, which C regulates. Tasks with D̸ = ∅ are labeled as involving physical dependency coordination . Clearance tasks keep a parallel workload, since each robot advances its own objects and only the use of the cleared region waits for ϕ; they exercise both mechanisms. In relays and carrier transport, the robots instead perform successive stages of the same delivery, linked by ϕ. These are the parallel ( D = ∅), both, and dependency classes of figure 2c; labels are fixed from the task definition and α. Mechanism and embodiment are labeled separately: homogeneous pairs face dependencies when one robot vacates a destination that its teammate fills, and heterogeneous pairs can share a workload without any handoff. 

# 4 The DeCoNav-Act Benchmark 

DeCoNav-Act constructs each task for a designated robot team, verifies it through continuous joint execution, and packages the result with its constraints and reference evidence (figure 2). Four principles guide its design. 

Physical grounding : cross-robot physical interactions are executed in continuous physics and judged from robot and object states. Mechanism coverage : tasks span parallel workloads, physical dependencies, and their combination, with homogeneous and heterogeneous teams. Verified feasibility : a task is admitted only after a joint reference execution succeeds. Diagnosability : metrics and controlled conditions locate failures before and during cross-robot interactions. Because generation and qualification are automated, the same pipeline extends the benchmark to new scenes and robot teams.

4.2 Team-conditioned task generation 

Each candidate binds a scene and its object goals to a robot team, initial states, object assignments, and roles (figure 2a). 

Homogeneous tasks. Six household families (figure 2b) follow the task objectives of γ-Scape (Zhang et al., 2026c). These tasks split workloads between two identical robots; object exchange and clear route also introduce physical dependencies. The generator searches jointly over object assignments, the second robot’s start, and waiting or clearance motions around shared workspaces, ordering actions by the task definition: a robot vacates a destination before its teammate fills it, and a route is cleared before the loaded traversal. A decomposition is kept only if a parallel-time model predicts that the team finishes before either robot alone. 

Heterogeneous tasks. Seven families build on differences in reach, footprint, and payload. Five are dependency-only. In a high-to-low relay , the R1 Pro retrieves an object from elevated storage and leaves it on a handoff surface for the TidyBot; a low-to-high relay reverses the roles. In the narrow-passage relay , the R1 Pro transfers an object to the TidyBot, which delivers it through a furniture-lined aisle too narrow for the R1 Pro. In carrier transport , the R1 Pro loads several objects into the Create 3’s basket for delivery, and two-ended loading/unloading adds a TidyBot that unloads them at the far end. The other two split workloads: in clear then deliver , one robot clears a target surface before its teammate fills it, and two-source collection gathers objects at one target. 

Instructions. Public instructions state the objects, sources, targets, any required handoff surface, and the completion condition, e.g. , “Move the lemonade from the study floor to the westernmost cabinet in the dining 5Gamma Robotics / TeleAI 

room. Use the dining table as the handoff surface.” They contain no robot-specific steps or coordinates, so agents must ground every referent from their own observations. 

4.3 Joint qualification 

Complete-team feasibility. Candidates first pass reachability screening for routes, grasps, and placements. During construction, a privileged Oracle executes the whole team in one scene, planning motions with cuRobo (Sundaralingam et al., 2023) and using the same robot and object models as evaluation. A task is accepted only if all required actions complete and every final goal holds after release and a stability interval, as confirmed by an independent audit of the recorded states and events rather than by the controller’s completion flags (see section A.6). 

Dependency realization. For each required dependency, qualification checks both establishment and use. In a relay, the receiver must acquire and deliver the same object after its partner releases it. In carrier transport, the payload must settle inside the basket before departure and remain supported until arrival. In clearance tasks, the vacated region must still be free when the consumer uses it. 

Evidence for cooperation. The evidence for cooperation depends on the coordination mechanism. For parallel workloads, a parallel-time model predicts shorter team completion times than for either robot alone; it filters homogeneous candidates (section 4.2) and uses measured action durations for heterogeneous collection and clear-then-deliver tasks (see table 7). Reference executions verify feasibility and are not optimized for speed. For tasks with a physical dependency, qualification verifies that each required dependency is established and used. For all heterogeneous tasks, Oracle-based qualification verifies both joint feasibility and the necessity of combining complementary robot capabilities within the registered skill set. 

4.4 Statistics and evaluation split 

Figure 2b,c summarizes the 350 homogeneous and 150 heterogeneous tasks; per-family counts, scenes, and room coverage are in section A.1. Of the 500 tasks, 271 exercise only parallel workloads, 95 only physical dependencies, and 134 both; homogeneous tasks fall into the first and third groups (241 and 109), and heterogeneous tasks into all three (30, 95, and 25). The tasks span 118 scenes and nine room types and contain 2,631 object instances. Per task, the Oracle performs 4.8 manipulation actions (grasps and releases) on average (median 4), and its robots drive a median of 27 m in total (figure 2c). 

Test split. The test split contains 100 comparatively easy tasks that keep long-horizon execution tractable for current agents: 75 homogeneous tasks with short carrying legs across all six families, and 25 heterogeneous tasks with at least two per family. Mobile manipulators start close to their first pickup. Each test task has four seeds that slightly perturb robot starts and object poses, each checked for collision-free and reproducible resets (see section A.1); section 5 splits them into withheld and evaluation seeds. The remaining tasks are released as an additional pool for training and evaluation. 

# 5 Evaluation Protocol 

DeCoNav-Act evaluates teams at two levels: end-to-end episodes from the initial state (section 5.2) and a diagnostic that starts from verified pre-handoff states (section 5.3). 

5.1 Execution and information access 

All robots share one scene and simulation clock. Model requests run asynchronously, while physics continues to advance for all robots and objects, so deliberation time has a physical cost. The default observation interface is sensor-grounded. Each robot receives its own RGB-D images, pro-prioception, IMU measurements, camera calibration, and kinematics, together with teammate messages; simulator-provided object poses, scene meshes, traversability maps, reference routes, exact localization, and 6Gamma Robotics / TeleAI 

evaluator predicates are withheld. Some methods additionally receive a scene prior : the room layout and static occupancy derived from the scene assets, without the positions of any task object. CoMoMa uses it in every end-to-end episode, together with a navigation memory built during development (section 5.4). End-to-end episodes provide only the team-level instruction, without prescribed role assignments; roles are specified only in the diagnostic. Recovery is permitted within an episode, but resets are not. 

5.2 Metrics 

Team outcomes. Team success (SR) requires the terminal state to satisfy G and the execution trace to satisfy 

D and C. Goal completion (GC) is the fraction of task checkpoints completed by termination, averaged over episodes. Checkpoints cover navigation (reaching an object or the loading robot) and manipulation (acquiring, carrying, placing, or loading), and each is credited only after its physical criteria are verified (see section D.3). The checkpoint set of a task is fixed and identical for every method; navigation checkpoints already satisfied at reset are excluded. 

Path efficiency. Each navigation checkpoint defines a leg to its interaction target, which is reached when the base of the robot performing the leg arrives near the target (see section D.3). 5.4 Evaluated methods 

Following ASPIRE (Lu et al., 2026), each test task has two withheld seeds, disjoint from its evaluation seeds. CoMoMa evolves its skill library and navigation memory on the withheld seeds and freezes both before testing, whereas the baselines have no development phase. Every method is then tested on one of the two evaluation seeds of each test task (100 episodes). 

ASPIRE-SG and RAX-SG. We adapt ASPIRE 1.0 (Lu et al., 2026; ASPIRE contributors, 2026) and RAX (Huang et al., 2026a; RAX contributors, 2026) to multi-robot teams and to the sensor-grounded interface, replacing privileged inputs of their original implementations, such as exact odometry, traversability maps, and simulator collision worlds, with the shared tools. Each robot runs its own agent with the upstream executor, and the agents divide the work through the same team board and message tools as CoMoMa; no exploration is run before testing (see section C). We evaluate each baseline with and without the scene prior (denoted +prior in table 2). 

CoMoMa. CoMoMa builds on the program-as-policy design of ASPIRE and RAX: its runner follows ASPIRE’s tool registry and program executor, and robot actions are composed from registered tools and revised from execution feedback. Both target single-robot control; CoMoMa adds three components for team execution. (i) Collaboration contract. A shared task graph records the assigned robot and prerequisites of every subtask; a subtask becomes executable only after its prerequisites are verified, and operations on shared objects, workspaces, and handoff surfaces acquire leases. (ii) Shared spatial memory. Measured destinations, maps, and routes are shared with their provenance and kept separate from the task graph, so a teammate’s progress updates only the affected bindings. A navigation memory of measured routes, compiled from development episodes, proposes subgoals to the current-map planner. (iii) Development-time skill library. A skill library is refined offline from development failures (see section C.4). The +prior baselines share CoMoMa’s tools and scene prior, so their gap to CoMoMa reflects components (i)–(iii) together. 

Shared settings. All methods use GPT-5.6-luna with medium reasoning effort, the same tools, and a per-episode budget of 20 min of model time and 500 s of simulated time (see section C.5). We additionally run CoMoMa end to end, and all methods in the diagnostic, with GPT-6.1-sol (table 3).

6.1 End-to-end evaluation 

Team success remains out of reach (Q1). With GPT-5.6-luna, no method completes a single task, and even the strongest configuration, CoMoMa, reaches only 13.2% GC (table 2). A stronger backbone helps without changing the picture: with GPT-6.1-sol, CoMoMa completes one of 100 tasks, a carrier task in which the R1 Pro loads both objects into the Create 3’s basket. DeCoNav-Act thus leaves ample headroom, and its graded metrics separate the methods (CoMoMa over the strongest baseline: +3.3 GC points, 95% CI [0.5, 6.2]). 

Execution stops before most handoffs (Q2). Agents reach navigation targets far more often than they complete manipulation: with the scene prior, about half of the navigation checkpoints are reached, but fewer than 4% of the manipulation checkpoints are completed, so GC mainly reflects navigation. Few dependencies are therefore reached, and consumers attempt only 6 handoffs in 500 GPT-5.6-luna episodes. PE stays within 83–90% of NSR, so navigation fails by not arriving rather than by detours. End-to-end HSR thus rests on few attempts, which motivates the handoff-isolated diagnostic. 

Physical dependencies are harder than parallel workloads. At matched embodiment, homogeneous tasks with physical dependencies roughly halve the GC of the +prior baselines relative to parallel-only tasks (e.g. , from 16.2% to 8.1% for RAX-SG + prior), whereas CoMoMa’s GC is unaffected, consistent with its dependency-aware task graph. Among heterogeneous families, relays are the hardest: no GPT-5.6-luna method credits any checkpoint on them, and only GPT-6.1-sol makes progress, while carrier transport is the most tractable. Per-family results are in table 12. 

The scene prior matters most; the backbone helps manipulation (Q4). The scene prior, the largest single factor, roughly quadruples the baselines’ NSR and triples their GC. Given the same prior and tools, CoMoMa’s further gain concentrates on heterogeneous tasks and dependencies, where its HCov doubles that of the best baseline; this gap is attributable to CoMoMa’s team-level design as a whole, namely its coordination layer, navigation memory, and skill library (section 5.4). Switching to GPT-6.1-sol raises manipulation completion from 3.7% to 12.2% and HCov to 23.7%, and consumers then complete 4 of 11 attempted handoffs.

6.3 Real-world case study 

We test whether these constraints arise on hardware with a shared-counter task for an Astribot and an R1 Pro (figure 3), in which one robot’s presence blocks the other’s access to the counter. With coordination, the Astribot announces its placement and vacates the counter, after which the R1 Pro places its object; without these messages, the Astribot keeps occupying the counter, and the R1 Pro repeatedly repositions without placing. Neither robot fails to grasp or navigate, so releasing a shared resource is itself a physical precondition for the teammate’s progress, which DeCoNav-Act encodes as a validity constraint. 

6.4 Failure analysis 

End to end, single-robot stages fail first. Nearly every GPT-5.6-luna failure occurs before a cross-robot interaction. In the tool logs of CoMoMa and the baselines without the prior, about half of CoMoMa’s navigation requests arrive, against roughly one in eight for the baselines, and only 11 of 451 point-grasp requests yield a held object. Failed grasps stem mostly from motion execution, such as inverse kinematics or rejected arm paths. The diagnostic by dependency type is in table 13. 

Takeaways. End-to-end success is bounded by single-robot navigation and grasping. With low-level inputs equalized, the diagnostic separates coordination designs and localizes their failures to establishing and continuing from teammate-produced conditions; most handoffs remain unsolved even for the strongest agent, which calls for agents that reason explicitly about the states they leave for teammates. 10 Gamma Robotics / TeleAI 

# 7 Conclusion 

We introduced DeCoNav-Act, a benchmark for language-guided multi-robot mobile manipulation in a shared physical world, with tasks organized by coordination mechanism across homogeneous and heterogeneous teams and admitted through continuous joint execution. Its two-level evaluation shows that current agents rarely reach a handoff end to end and that its diagnostic separates coordination designs once low-level inputs are equalized, while establishing and using teammate-produced conditions remain largely unsolved. We hope DeCoNav-Act helps measure progress toward collective physical capability, and we plan to extend it with longer horizons, larger teams, and task allocation. 11 Gamma Robotics / TeleAI

A.3 Public task and private evaluator specification 

Each task has a stable identifier, a scene and asset revision, an embodiment configuration, initial-state seeds, a public instruction, a role assignment, final-goal definitions, required interactions, and budgets. The public task conveys goals; the role assignment serves construction, auditing, and the handoff-isolated diagnostic and is withheld in end-to-end episodes. Neither contains object-instance handles, simulator paths, exact coordinates, an executable reference plan, or evaluator state. Agents may create opaque handles from their own detections. The role assignment specifies an object or action responsibility, not a trajectory. In the diagnostic, a relay receiver is told which object it must take from the handoff surface, but not the exact release pose or when that pose becomes usable. Final goals are object-level outcomes: a mug and a spoon delivered to a desk are two goals, not eight successes for finding, grasping, carrying, and placing. A handoff is required only when the task contract names it, for example through a designated handoff surface.

Object exchange and clearance. One robot vacates a location that its teammate must fill or clears a route before the teammate’s loaded traversal. A.6 Qualification details 

A reference execution uses the same robot and object models as evaluation. Scene geometry, gravity, collision approximations, joint limits, gripper rules, and payload constraints are frozen with the record. The Oracle may plan with privileged information but cannot set target states directly, and an independent check replays the state and event traces rather than relying on controller flags. Heterogeneous candidates run once without retries, and an independent audit re-derives contacts, carrier containment, cooked-hull region clearance, capture-TCP heights, and terminal stability (75 samples below 2 cm/s) from the trace. Table 7 details the qualification evidence by stratum across all 350 homogeneous and 150 heterogeneous 22 Gamma Robotics / TeleAI 

tasks ( 26 + 23 + 46 + 55 = 150 ). For homogeneous tasks, the time model moves each robot at uniform speed along occupancy-grid routes and includes joint-schedule waits and clearance motions, while the single-robot baseline minimizes over either start and all precedence-legal orders. For the 55 heterogeneous clear-then-deliver and two-source collection tasks, qualification enforces two layers: (i) embodiment-specific workstation conditioning, where candidate stances are filtered under each robot’s collision cylinder, vertical reach, and peer endpoint-separation tubes of radius rR1 + rTidy + 2 ϵ, where r is each robot’s planning radius, excluding a single embodiment from covering both the elevated or deep tabletop stances and the tight low-furniture stances within the registered workstation map; and (ii) an exact dynamic-programming lower bound over all precedence-legal serial one-payload orders for either single actor, which retains measured pick/place durations from the joint reference and charges missing inter-station transfers at straight-line chord distance divided by each robot’s native speed cap (favoring the single robot, with turns and preparation relaxed to zero), requiring at least a 2× registered-domain makespan advantage alongside a non-interfering native reference check. Across all four heterogeneous strata, Oracle qualification thus verifies both continuous joint feasibility and the necessity of combining complementary robot capabilities within the registered skill set. 

A.7 Stable final-state predicates 

A placement predicate specifies object identity, target region, physical support or containment, release from the gripper where required, and a stability interval of 0.5 s. Whole-object containment is used when the task requires the object to fit, rather than testing only its center. Thresholds for translational and angular speed, contact tolerance, and overhang are fixed in the released evaluator configuration and were set before any baseline was evaluated. Regression tests cover boundary-straddling objects, momentary contact, wrong-object placement, late payload loss, and goals invalidated by the teammate. 

# B Information Boundary and Conformance 

B.1 Sensor data versus robot metadata 

Privileged Oracle access to scene state is restricted to offline task generation and qualification and to the explicitly labeled diagnostic controls in section 5.3 ( s− 

> d

initialization, reference-producer, and Oracle-guided trials), and is never exposed to policies in end-to-end or unguided runs. At evaluation time, the policy contract specifies the public camera names, resolution, field of view, RGB/depth synchronization, metric-depth convention, invalid-depth encoding, timestamp source, sensing rate, and declared sensor model. Proprioception consists of explicitly listed measured quantities, such as articulated-joint positions/velocities and gripper opening. Base velocity is accepted only with its source stated. A simulator’s world-frame linear velocity, or its virtual prismatic base joints, must not enter under the generic name “proprioception” without being recognized as assistance. IMU acceleration and angular velocity do not provide exact global position. Any orientation estimate has a declared reference frame and estimation/noise assumptions. Public robot metadata includes static camera-to-link extrinsics, kinematic parameters, joint limits, robot geometry, gripper dimensions, controller gains or semantics required to issue valid commands, and the control period. This is distinct from access to furniture meshes and object models in the current scene. For a wrist camera, the base-relative camera transform is computed from measured joints and fixed hand–eye calibration. Neither that transform nor a self-collision model requires an oracle map of the environment.

B.3 Tools and feedback 

Object localization, segmentation, grasp proposal, and progress verification may use pretrained models, but their online inputs must satisfy the public contract. Model identity, checkpoints, and any task-specific training are recorded. A tool may return its own planning failure, controller tracking error, or visual confidence, but never an evaluator predicate. “The gripper commanded closed” and “the intended object is securely held” are distinct observations. A ground-truth terminal score is used for evaluation. In the main test loop, the policy cannot repeatedly query that score or a partial-goal checklist. An episode termination signal, if required by the environment, ends the rollout immediately; it cannot be queried and then used to continue revising the same episode. Richer training feedback, reset-enabled adaptation, and oracle diagnostics are distinct protocol dimensions with separate logs. 24 Gamma Robotics / TeleAI 

B.4 Isolation and audit tests 

The deployment separates the simulator/evaluator process from agent and tool clients. The client exposes a typed public schema rather than a native environment reference. The policy workspace must not mount task-instance source files, evaluator logs, reference trajectories, hidden scene assets, or a simulator object registry. Error messages and visualization exports are filtered through the same boundary. Import restrictions alone are insufficient, since an injected object may still reference a privileged host. An audit records every policy-facing field’s provenance and every planner input’s construction path. Represen-tative tests include: denying direct environment-object access; checking that camera-to-base transforms match FK; verifying that movement changes odometry through the estimator; testing that unknown geometry is not treated as an oracle free-space query; and confirming that train/test feedback permissions differ as specified. A noninterference test evaluates a pure planning or observation tool twice with identical permitted history and random seed while changing a hidden, unobserved object in a shadow world; the tool’s output must stay unchanged. The test detects policy-side access to information outside the declared inputs, whereas later physical outcomes may differ once a robot encounters the changed object. Audit outcomes and test coverage are released with the benchmark. A failed conformance check invalidates the sensor-grounded label until it is fixed.

# D Execution and Scoring Details 

D.1 Shared physical timeline 

A physics step advances all robots and dynamic objects together at 120 Hz, and robot targets are committed every four steps. Model requests run asynchronously and the physics does not pause for them; a robot without a new target holds its measured pose. A long blocking skill of one robot therefore never suspends its teammate. All methods use the same model endpoint and simulator, so simulated-time budgets are comparable. Model calls and tokens per episode are summarized in table 9. 

D.2 Stopping, recovery, and incidents 

An episode stops when all goals hold, the simulated-time or inference budget is exhausted, or a task-defined unrecoverable violation occurs. A local failure of one robot does not terminate its teammate unless a required dependency prevents continuation. The evaluator can record a completed goal and later remove it if a subsequent action invalidates its terminal condition. Test episodes have no reset privilege. Grasp retries, replanning, and program edits within the same evolving scene are permitted recovery actions; restoring an earlier simulator state or rerunning a seed after observing it is not. An infrastructure incident, meaning a host, service, or simulator failure unrelated to any policy decision, receives a logged rerun with the same seed and no transfer of policy knowledge. Policy-caused invalid commands, planner exhaustion, or failure to recover before the deadline remain failures. Incidents and reruns are logged with each episode. 

D.3 Metric details 

Goal completion. GC is computed per episode as the fraction of task checkpoints completed at termination and then averaged over episodes. Checkpoints follow the task program and cover both navigation and manipulation. A navigation checkpoint credits the first arm-equipped robot that brings its base within 27 Gamma Robotics / TeleAI 

r = 1 .0 m of an object before the object’s first acquisition, or the carrier that comes within r of the loading robot. Manipulation checkpoints cover each object’s acquisition, transport, and placement, the carrier’s payload loading and arrival, and fixture goals such as an articulated joint in restoration tasks. A navigation checkpoint whose occupancy-grid path from the robot’s pose at reset is shorter than 0.5 m is already satisfied at reset and is excluded. A checkpoint counts only once its physical criteria are verified from object and robot state. 

Navigation legs. Each navigation checkpoint defines one leg. Because agents allocate work themselves, a leg is attributed to the robot that first reaches its target or, if no robot reaches it, to the arm-equipped robot that comes closest; it starts at that robot’s initial pose or at its previous arrival. A leg is reached when this robot’s base comes within r of the target. The measured length Lj sums the planar displacement of the base over the leg from simulator samples, including retries and corrective motions. The reference length Lref  

> j

is the shortest 8-connected path on the scene’s 2D occupancy grid (0.05 m cells) from the leg’s start to the free cells within r of the target, computed with A*, so it follows the team’s own allocation rather than the Oracle’s. All reported NSR and PE values use r = 1 .0 m. 

Pre-handoff states and handoff predicates. The pre-handoff state is reached when the producer holds the dependency object next to the handoff surface (relays), holds a payload object within reach of the carrier’s basket (carrier loading), or holds the occupying object at the cleared region (clearance). Establishment requires a stable release on the handoff surface within the receiver’s reachable region, a settled payload inside the basket, or a vacated region, respectively, still holding when the consumer starts. Valid use requires the robot that starts using the condition to grasp the same object, depart and arrive with the payload supported, or complete its placement or traversal through the cleared region. Agents allocate work themselves, so every stage is attributed to the robot that physically performs it: a single robot holding the object, or for a grasp not yet closed, a single robot in contact with it. Multiple robots on one object earn no credit, and a takeover discards the first robot’s partial evidence. 

Dependency accounting. HCov keeps every dependency of D in its denominator, including those whose producer was never reached. HSR uses reached dependencies whose condition a robot other than the producer starts to use; a dependency that the producing robot resolves itself is counted as self-resolved and excluded from HSR. A method that avoids difficult dependencies can therefore show a high HSR but a low HCov, and both are reported. Strict team success requires all final goals, all required dependencies, and all mandatory constraints. 

D.4 Statistical reporting 

The unit of analysis is the parent task. Each method runs one evaluation seed per test task, and every rate is reported together with its denominator. End-to-end differences between methods are paired by task and summarized with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval over tasks (10,000 resamples). In the handoff-isolated diagnostic, methods are compared on the same dependencies, and paired differences are tested with an exact McNemar test.
