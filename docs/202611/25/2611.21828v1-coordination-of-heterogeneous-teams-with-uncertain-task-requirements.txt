## Coordination of Heterogeneous Teams with Uncertain Task Requirements 

Jongann Lee JONGANN 2@ ILLINOIS .EDU 

Melkior Ornik MORNIK @ILLINOIS .EDU 

University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, Urbana, IL, USA, 61801 

Abstract 

Coordinating a team of heterogeneous agents to complete tasks with unknown requirements is a challenging multi-agent planning problem. Agents differ in their ability to discover task require-ments and in their ability to engage tasks, so the planner must coordinate task discovery, execution, and staging. This paper introduces a hierarchical planner that uses a graph neural network policy to assign actions to agents, then finds a path to the action’s location using a shortest-path algorithm. The central part of our approach is the CAAT graph, which is a graph representation of the planning context consisting of the agents, tasks, capabilities, and actions. We demonstrate that our learned planner can jointly coordinate a heterogeneous team to simultaneously discover task requirements and complete tasks in a shorter time compared to baselines that optimize task discovery and task engagement independently. Furthermore, we show that our policy can zero-shot plan on out-of-distribution settings by training only on an outdoor map then transferring without finetuning to an indoor map with more agents, tasks, and capabilities than seen during training. 1. Introduction 

Envision a scenario where satellite imagery has identified threats at known locations across moun-tainous terrain. A heterogeneous team is tasked with eliminating these threats. Although the target locations are known, the capabilities required to eliminate each target remain uncertain. Some members possess specialized engagement capabilities but have limited vision, like a bomb defuser wearing a bulky mask. Others are lightly equipped scouts; they can identify threats from distant viewpoints by traveling to vantage points (like the top of a hill), but cannot eliminate them. Commit-ting an agent to an incompatible task can result in its loss, hampering the team’s ability to complete the objective. Coordinating both task requirement discovery and task engagement presents numerous chal-lenges. Attackers have to choose between moving to a known task far away or a closer unknown task. Scouts have to consider the utility of revealing a task with respect to the current attacker states. Hybrid scout-attackers can either reveal unknown task requirements or complete a known compat-ible task. Thus, coordinating such a heterogeneous team requires more than optimizing the scouts and workers independently. Previous research has largely focused on coordinating a homogeneous team with identical task requirements (Choi et al., 2009; Liu and Shell, 2011). More recently, multi-agent planning methods that consider different agent capabilities and unknown task requirements have been proposed (Long and Liang, 2026; Meighan et al., 2026; Rossano et al., 2026). However, no previous work has built

a multi-agent planner that can accommodate a varying set of capabilities (including the ability to scout) and uncertain task requirements that are revealed by sending a scout to a location that can see that task. Our approach is to create a graph representation of the planning context, which is then used by a graph neural network (GNN) policy to allocate agents to actions. We introduce CAAT graph , a graph comprising agent, task, capability, and action nodes that captures the key decision-making variables and their relations. The action nodes represent four types of actions: execute task, observe, stage, and wait and gives the planner a consistent set of actions on different maps and at different time steps. Using the CAAT graph as the representation allows a pretrained policy to transfer to a different map with new agent, task, and capability settings without any retraining. Our planner is hierarchical; the GNN planner specifies the action and a low level path planner finds the shortest path to that action’s location. We pretrain the GNN policy on a large set of simulated rollouts on a single map using reinforcement learning (RL). Our contributions are as follows: 1. We define the heterogeneous team multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) problem with uncer-tain task requirements. Our problem is an extension of MRTA in two parts: (i) a capability 

that each agent can possess and that each task requires but is unknown a priori; (ii) a visibility mapping that defines the set of nodes from which the task requirement can be observed. 2. We create a graph representation of the planning context called the CAAT graph, which pro-vides a consistent set of inputs and actions for the planner. Experimental results demonstrate that using the CAAT graph enables a pretrained GNN policy to zero-shot transfer to out-of-distribution configurations and maps. 3. We show that our method achieves a lower problem completion time than a baseline that opti-mizes scouting and task execution independently, demonstrating that our policy successfully exploits the benefits of jointly coordinating task requirement discovery and task execution. 

2. Related Work 

Multi-Robot Task Allocation (MRTA): The goal of MRTA is to coordinate a team of agents to complete a set of tasks while minimizing the cost (Gerkey and Mataric, 2004). Many solutions to MRTA have been proposed, including optimization-based approaches (Koes et al., 2005; Liu and Shell, 2011; Shorinwa et al., 2023), auction-based approaches (Gerkey and Mataric, 2002; Choi et al., 2009), and heuristics (Parker, 1998). More recently, deep learning has been applied to MRTA using attention mechanisms, GNNs, and RL (Paul et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Altundas et al., 2025). Typically, a GNN processes a graph of agents and tasks to create an embedding, which is then used by a decoder to create a probability distribution of tasks for each agent. The policy is pretrained on a large set of sampled rollouts using RL. While these approaches are not directly applicable to our problem, our method builds on the previously established GNN + decoder + RL method. 

Watchman Routing Problem (WRP): The scout’s objective in our problem is to observe all of the tasks on the map. WRP is a closely related problem, where the objective is to find the shortest path on which every node is observed at least once (Chin and Ntafos, 1986). WRP on grid maps has been solved by formulating it as a search problem on a tree then using A∗ with a heuristic (Seiref et al., 2020; Livne et al., 2023). These methods are too narrowly focused on just scouting; we use this method as part of our baseline and demonstrate that our method is better than it.

Heterogeneous Team MRTA with Uncertain Task Requirements: Recent works have studied coordinating a heterogeneous team for MRTA with uncertain task requirements. IAOM uses a GNN with RL to coordinate a homogeneous team of robots to solve a variant of MRTA with an unknown number of robots required per task (Long and Liang, 2026). AURA is an auction based method that coordinates a team of search robots and debris robots to complete a heterogeneous set of search and debris tasks (Rossano et al., 2026). Beck et al. (2016) alternates search-route and rescue-schedule optimization using sampled task realizations for search and rescue. None of the previous works consider hybrid agents capable of both scouting and engaging tasks, variations in visibility, and a variable number of capabilities. 

3. Problem Formulation 

Our problem is motivated by the problem of coordinating a team of agents with different capabilities to complete a set of tasks with unknown requirements that are revealed by the scout agents. We formulate the problem of heterogeneous team MRTA with uncertain task requirements . A directed, weighted graph G = ( V, E) represents a map, with node v ∈ V for location, and e ∈ E indicating traversability between two nodes. Let c(v1, v 2), (v1, v 2) ∈ E be the traversal time of an edge. We define the visibility map vis( v) ⊂ V as the set of nodes visible from node v (Lee and Ornik, 2026). Both c(v1, v 2) and vis( v) are known a priori. The traversal time is directional; e.g. traveling uphill from node v1 to v2 takes longer than traveling downhill from v2 to v1.There are multiple agents Ai, 1 ≤ i ≤ Na and multiple tasks Tj , 1 ≤ j ≤ Nt. Tasks are stationary and their locations are known. Agents traverse from node v1 to node v2 along an edge, with their traversal time corresponding to the traversal cost of the edge c(v1, v 2). Two or more agents can occupy the same node simultaneously. We define a set of capabilities Ck ∈ C , 0 ≤ k ≤ Nc. Intuitively, a capability corresponds to the 

trait that an agent needs to have to complete a task. The requirement of a task C(Tj ) ∈ C\{ C0}

is the capability required to complete task Tj . The task requirements are initially unknown with the prior probability of Tj requiring Ck being p(C(Tj ) = Ck). An agent can possess multiple capabilities C(Ai) ⊂ C . Our problem is an ST-SR-TA MRTA problem (Gerkey and Mataric, 2004). C0 represents the scout capability. It is only given to agents, and only agents with C0 can observe an unknown task’s required capability within their current visibility vis( v). Upon reaching a node, a scout instantly reveals the exact capability requirements of all tasks visible from that node at no additional observation cost. We assume that this information is instantly available to all agents. An agent completes a task if it reaches it and possesses the capability required by the task. An agent can only be assigned to complete a task if its requirements are known and possessed by the agent. The objective is to minimize the makespan: the time to complete all the tasks.

Once an action has been assigned, the agent’s path to the action’s location is determined using a shortest path algorithm such as A∗. The agents follow their planned paths until an agent completes its intended action or an unknown task requirement is revealed, at which point the planner replans. This hierarchy reduces the decision space that must be learned and preserves the shortest path optimality for routing. The GNN planner is pretrained on simulated rollouts using RL. 

4.1. CAAT Graph 

The Capability-Agent-Action-Task graph, or CAAT graph, represents the planning context at a given time. It consists of four types of nodes and three types of edges as seen in Figure 1. Its goal is to provide a consistent and compressed representation of the situation and actions. We explain the CAAT graph by following its creation step by step as in Figure 2. First, we extract the agents and tasks from a given timestep of the problem. Every task is con-nected to every agent with a travel time edge. A connection via a travel time edge represents a connection that may be traversed by an agent. The edge feature is the shortest path travel time, which is then normalized using z-score normalization. The agent features are the Boolean indicating whether the agent is alive, remaining transit time, a Boolean indicating whether the agent has the scout capability C0, and the agent’s position (normalized). We then add capabilities as graph nodes. Each capability is a node, and it is connected to agents and tasks via a capability edge. If an agent has a capability, then it is connected via a capability edge (with edge weight 1) to the corresponding capability node. Tasks with known requirements are also connected to their corresponding capability node with a capability edge of weight 1. For a task Tj

with unknown requirements, the capability edge connects all of the possible requirements with the prior p(C(Tj ) = Ck) as the edge weight. Representing capabilities as nodes allows a pretrained GNN policy to accept additional capabilities without changing the model dimensions. The scout capability is an exception; it is encoded as an agent feature. Finally, we add four types of actions to the graph. 1. Wait: remain at the current location. 2. Execute: go to a known task. 3. Observe: reach the closest suitable location to observe a subset of unknown tasks. 4. Stage: position near unknown tasks in anticipation of their requirements being revealed. Each action is connected to its relevant tasks via a task semantics edge. Each action is also connected to all agents and all tasks via a travel time edge. The wait action is the exception: it is not connected to any other node since it is not related to any task and does not have a location on the map. More details are in Appendix 8.1.

4.3. Policy Pretraining 

We pretrain the neural policy πθ using a large number of simulated rollouts. During the rollout, the policy πθ assigns agents to actions, and the agents follow the shortest path to the action’s location. They continue on the path until an unknown task requirement is revealed, a task is completed, or an agent reaches its destination, at which point the planner replans. The rollout ends either when all tasks are complete, or when all agents select wait and the rollout stalls. At the end of each rollout, we calculate the rollout reward by comparing it to the oracle , an optimal planner that knows the ground-truth task requirements. The primary component in the reward is the regret which is the makespan of our policy’s rollout M divided by the makespan of the oracle M ∗. We add a penalty term for the number of incomplete tasks I to discourage stalling, with λI as the hyperparameter.

5. Experiments 

5.1. Experiment Setup Maps. We use three maps in our experiments: WV, WA, and KTH. WV and WA are outdoor maps of locations in the US states of West Virginia and Washington, and are created using the topography data from OpenTopography (U.S. Geological Survey, 2024). The maps are discretized into a 64 × 64 grid graph. The traversal cost is calculated using Tobler’s hiking function with road edges having 40% lower traversal cost (Tobler, 1993). The visibility is modeled using viewshed analysis (Toma et al., 2026). The KTH map is an indoor map of a building at KTH Royal Institute of Technology (Ho et al., 2025). It is also discretized into a grid; its traversal cost is the edge length and the visibility is modeled using line of sight. WV is used as the training map, WA is used as the in-distribution test map, and KTH is used as the out-of-distribution test map with 4.6 times as many nodes compared to WV or WA. 

Capabilities, Agents, and Tasks. We pretrain our policy on the WV map with 3–6 agents and 5–9 randomly placed tasks. There are 3 non-scout capabilities, and they are assigned randomly such that all capabilities are matched with at least one agent. All agents start at the same node. For testing, we use a fixed suite of capabilities, agents, and tasks for each map. All maps have three sets of agent capabilities and three sets of task requirements for each number of agents Na and tasks Nt.For WV and WA, 3 ≤ Na ≤ 6 and 5 ≤ Nt ≤ 9 so there are a total of 12 agent configurations and 15 task configurations. We test every pair of agent and task configurations, resulting in a total of 180 test problems per map for WV and WA. The KTH map is tested with 8 agents and 14 tasks for a total of 9 problems. 

Baselines. We compare the performance of CAAT Graph + HGT with three baselines. The ‘or-acle’ is an optimal planner that knows all of the task requirements, and its makespan is the lowest achievable makespan for a given problem. ‘Scout then engage’ first discovers all of the task require-ments using an optimal WRP planner, then uses the oracle to execute all of the tasks. ‘Engage while scout’ uses the same WRP planner to coordinate scouts and simultaneously assigns non-scouts to execute known tasks using the oracle. Additional details on the baselines are in Appendix 8.5. 

Pretraining. Our policy is pretrained on 12,800 sampled rollouts on the WV map. On a desktop with an NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU, pretraining takes approximately 8 hours. Once pretrained, we freeze all policy weights and do not finetune them during evaluation. Additional experimental settings are provided in Appendix 8.6.

Next, we examine an actual planning decision produced by our method. Figure 4 shows a planning decision on WV with 4 agents and 8 tasks (2 completed, 2 known but incomplete, 4 unknown). In the middle of the map, agents 1 and 3 are close to known task 1 and unknown task 3. Both agents have the capability required to complete task 1, so both of them get a large score for completing task 1. However, only agent 1 has the scout capability, so agent 3’s score for completing task 1 is higher, and our method assigns executing task 1 to agent 3 and assigns agent 1 to observe task 3. This intuitively makes sense: if both agents can complete task 1, but only one can observe task 3, then the scout should go observe. At the bottom, agents 0 and 2 are close to two unknown tasks. Therefore, agent 0 with the scout capability gets large scores for observing task 4 or task 5 and agent 2 gets large scores for staging for those two tasks. These decisions show that our method makes intuitively correct decisions for the given context of the problem.

6. Limitations 

Our planner assumes that the tasks’ locations are known in advance. In a multi-agent planning prob-lem with uncertain task requirements the location of the task may also be uncertain. Additionally, our method is a centralized planner that assumes perfect communication and instant relay of newly acquired information. A decentralized method would be more robust to faulty communication and would scale better with a larger number of agents. 

7. Conclusion 

In this paper, we considered the heterogeneous team multi-robot task allocation problem with uncer-tain task requirements. To solve it, we created a graph representation of the planning context called CAAT (Capability-Agent-Action-Task) graph that captures the key decision-making variables and provides a consistent set of actions on different maps and settings. Our hierarchical planner assigns actions to agents by processing the CAAT graph using a graph neural network, and then gener-ates the path to the action’s location using a shortest-path algorithm. Our method outperformed two baselines which optimize task discovery and task execution independently, showing that it ef-ficiently coordinates task discovery, execution and staging for unknown tasks. Furthermore, our policy trained on an outdoor map transferred without finetuning to a larger indoor map with more agents, tasks, and capabilities than seen during training while still outperforming the baselines.

8.1. Additional Detail on the CAAT Graph Agent node features. Each agent node Ai has an input feature vector x(Ai) ∈ R5. It contains a Boolean indicating whether the agent is alive, remaining transit time, a Boolean indicating whether it possesses the scout capability C0, and its two-dimensional position normalized to [0 , 1] 2. The remaining transit time is needed because replanning may occur while the agent is traversing an edge. In that case, the agent first finishes its committed edge, then follows its newly assigned path. The remaining transit time is normalized in the same way as the travel time edge. The agent’s other capabilities are represented by its connections to capability nodes. 

Task node features. Each task node Tj has an input feature vector x(Tj ) ∈ R4. It contains the task’s two-dimensional position normalized to [0 , 1] 2, a Boolean indicating whether the task has been completed, and a Boolean indicating whether its required capability is known. The required capability or its prior is represented by capability edges. The evaluated architecture retains agent-alive and task-completion indicators inherited from an earlier problem formulation. The alive indicator is constant in the present setting, while the completion indicator distinguishes completed tasks retained in the graph. Both dead agents and completed tasks are kept in the CAAT graph. 

Action node features. Each action node Kn has an input feature vector x(Kn) ∈ R4, whose entries correspond to execute, observe, staging, and wait, in that order. Execute, observe, and wait actions have value 1 in their corresponding entry and 0 elsewhere. The staging entry is 0.5

for a single-task stage and 1 for a two-task stage, with the other entries set to 0. This encoding distinguishes both the action type and the number of tasks associated with a staging action. 

Capability node initialization. Capability nodes represent Ck ∈ C\{ C0} and share a learned 128-dimensional initial embedding. The scout capability C0 is encoded as an agent feature. 

Edge details. Travel-time edges connect agent–task, agent–action, and action–task pairs with fi-nite corresponding travel times, excluding the wait action. At each planning decision, the traversal times are z-score normalized using the mean and standard deviation pooled across all valid travel time edges. This normalization gives maps with different traversal-time scales similarly scaled edge features. A travel-time edge does not imply that the agent can select the action; capability restric-tions are enforced by the decoder. 

Observation sets. For each non-task node of the map, we use the visibility map vis( v) to identify the subset of tasks with unknown requirements that can be observed from that node. Nodes with identical nonempty subsets form an observation set. Each set is represented by one observation action in the CAAT graph. Its task semantic edges connect it to the corresponding unknown tasks. For each agent, the destination is the node in the selected set with the minimum traversal time from that agent’s planning position. 

Staging candidates. The staging action positions an agent near tasks whose required capabilities remain unknown. For each such task, we include up to two non-task nodes with the shortest traversal times to that task. We also construct staging locations for pairs of unknown tasks. Let d(v, T j )

denote the directed shortest traversal time from node v to task Tj . For each pair (Tj , T k), we select a non-task node minimizing max {d(v, T j ), d (v, T k)}. To limit the number of pair actions, we rank

feasible pairs by max {d(Tj , T k), d (Tk, T j )} and retain up to n pairs, where n is the number of remaining unknown tasks.

Scout Then Engage. This policy plans in two phases. First, all scout-capable agents, including hybrids, use the WRP solver to reveal every unknown task requirement, while non-scout agents wait. Once every remaining requirement is known, the policy applies the oracle solver once to all living agents and remaining tasks using the revealed requirements, then follows the resulting execution plan.

Engage While Scout. This policy runs the WRP solver for a scouting group and the oracle solver for an execution group. The scouting group reveals unknown requirements, while the execution group completes known incomplete tasks supported by at least one of its agents. Both groups act simultaneously, and their plans are recomputed at every replanning event. The policy uses only revealed requirements and provides no staging actions. The two solvers optimize their respective subproblems independently. Pure scouts join the scouting group, and non-scout agents join the execution group. A heuristic assigns hybrid agents according to the current known task demand. For each non-scout capability 

Ck, let dk be the number of known incomplete tasks requiring Ck and sk the number of living non-scout agents possessing Ck. The initial capability deficit is 

gk = max {0, d k − sk}. (17) Among the remaining hybrids, we select the agent possessing the largest number of capabilities with a positive deficit, breaking ties uniformly at random. For each capability possessed by that agent, its deficit is reduced by one. Selection repeats until all deficits are zero or no remaining hybrid can reduce a deficit. Selected hybrids join the execution group; the remaining hybrids join the scouting group while unknown requirements remain, and otherwise wait.
