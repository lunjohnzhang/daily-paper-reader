# Dynamic Task Allocation with Dialogue 

Matthew Groholski, Weiwei Gu, and Nakul Gopalan 

School of Computing and Augmented Intelligence Arizona State University Tempe, AZ, USA 

{mgrohols, weiweigu, ngopala6 }@asu.edu 

Abstract —Effective multi-agent collaboration requires agents to adapt task allocation as goals and responsibilities evolve. Prior task-allocation frameworks often assume static goals, full observability, or rely on large language models (LLMs) without explicit, verifiable beliefs about task allocation or intent. These assumptions limit their applicability to real-world collaboration, where agents often lack full information about each other’s actions, progress, or local environment. To support dynamic task allocation under partial observability, we introduce acommunication-augmented Bayesian task-allocation framework. Our agent uses dialogue to reduce uncertainty about the envi-ronment and collaborators, while keeping decisions grounded in explicit probabilistic beliefs. We evaluate our framework in an Overcooked domain extended with dynamically arriving tasks under both full and partial object-space observability. Our communication agent consistently outperforms a vanilla LLM agent in both settings. Additionally, our framework performs comparably to a non-communicating task-allocation baseline under full observability, but substantially outperforms it under partial observability as task complexity increases by an average speed up of 0.585 and completes on average 18 timesteps faster. These results indicate that our agent is more efficient in dynamic task allocation than baselines in partially observable environments, and that communication is important as available information decreases. 

Index Terms —multi-agent collaboration, task allocation, human-robot dialogue, partial observability, Bayesian inference, large language models 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Humans often collaborate in environments where goals, responsibilities, and progress constantly evolve, from cooking in a kitchen to performing symphonies [1]. In these set-tings, collaborators must continually coordinate how duties are distributed and updated over time, a process known as 

dynamic task allocation . In a busy kitchen, for example, cooks redistribute tasks such as preparation, cooking, plating, and serving based on workload and individual progress. Dialog is one of the most common modalities for this coordination, enabling collaborators to communicate progress, negotiate responsibilities, and maintain a shared understanding of the task [2, 3, 4, 5], as shown in Figure 1. Effective human-agent collaboration therefore requires artificial agents that can use dialog to perform dynamic task allocation, reasoning about collaborators’ goals, actions, and progress to adapt responsibilities as an interaction unfolds [6]. Prior work has used reinforcement learning and large lan-guage models (LLMs) to develop collaborative agents [7, 8, 9, 10]. However, many approaches assume static goals, prior knowledge about collaborators, and full observability [11, 12], limiting their applicability to settings where goals and re-sponsibilities constantly change or the environment is only partially observable. Such settings are common in real-world collaboration, where agents must reason under uncertainty about both the task and their collaborators. We address this challenge by developing an agent that maintains belief states over the environment and collaborators, enabling dynamic task allocation under partial observability, evolving tasks, and no prior knowledge of partners. Building on Wu et al. [11], our agent reasons about unobserved objects, updates task allocations from new evidence, and exchanges task-relevant information beyond direct observation with dia-log. Together, these mechanisms support long-horizon collab-orative planning under uncertainty about both the environment and other agents’ behavior. Our contributions are: 1) We introduce a language-lifted agent that maintains beliefs over unobserved state and incorporates natural-language communication as probabilistic evidence for task-allocation inference. 2) We introduce a novel framework for evaluating dynamic multi-agent task allocation with dynamically arriving tasks under both full and partial object-space observabil-ity, implemented within the Overcooked environment. II. R ELATED WORKS 

A. Multi-Agent Planning 

Significant work in multi-agent planning has studied how agents coordinate task allocation with collaborators [13, 14, 15]. Ad hoc teamwork studies the setting where agents need to coordinate with previously unseen teammates without prior agreement on a joint policy [13, 14, 15]. Wu et al. [11] introduce Bayesian Delegation to update task allocations in collaboration, which decomposes tasks into subtasks and uses inverse planning to infer a collaborator’s latent subtask alloca-tion. Ying et al. [12] extend this line to partially observable set-tings by combining recursive Theory of Mind reasoning with communication for human-agent collaboration. However, these prior methods assume fixed goal specifications [11, 12, 13, 14, 15] and do not work when goals are continually evolving. B. Human-Agent Dialogue 

Researchers have long studied how to build collaborative agents that use dialogue [9, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Chai et al. [20] describe dialogue as a form of interaction through which humans communicate task-relevant knowledge to embodied agents. Prior works also develop agents that actively ask for help when the agent is stuck [17] or uncertain about the task [18, 19]. Beyond help-seeking, recent work has incorporated dialogue into collaborative task reasoning, using language models to infer collaborators’ mental states or gen-erate coordinated actions [9, 12, 16]. In our setting, dialogue is used to communicate task-relevant information and provide evidence for deciding future actions. 

C. LLM-based Planning 

LLMs have been increasingly studied for generating plans and coordinating agents [21, 22, 23]. Liu et al. [9] propose an LLM-based hierarchical language agent for real-time human-AI coordination by combining language-based intention rea-soning with reactive execution. Sun et al. [10] show that LLM agents struggle with active collaboration and continuous adaptation by introducing a benchmark for evaluating LLM-based multi-agent systems on collaborative tasks. Furthermore, Valmeekam et al. [24] show that LLMs perform poorly as autonomous long-horizon planners, but can provide useful heuristic guidance for other planners. In our approach, the LLM is used to interpret dialogue as task-relevant evidence for an explicit multi-agent planning procedure. III. M ETHODOLOGY

A. Domain 

We extend the Overcooked environment introduced by Wu et al. [11] with dynamic goal setting, where tasks are re-vealed incrementally during execution rather than fixed at the start. We also introduce partially observable settings in which collaborators’ actions or local environments are not accessible to the agent. This environment allows us to test agents’ reasoning and communication abilities on dynamic goals under different levels of uncertainty. Additional details can be found in Appdx. D. 

B. Baselines 

We compare against baselines suited to each observability setting. Under full observability, we adapt the task-allocation algorithm of Wu et al. [11] to support dynamic task arrivals, modifying it to replan as the task queue changes over an episode. Under partial observability, a setting we introduce for this domain, we use an ablation of our own method with communication removed, isolating the contribution of explicit communication when agents lack full environmental knowledge. In both settings we also compare against a vanilla LLM agent, following the LLM-based agents benchmarked by Sun et al. [10], which receives the same observability-appropriate information but selects actions directly, without the additional mechanisms in our methodology.

D. Experimental Results 

We evaluate full and partial observability separately, varying task queue length and layout within each setting; full experi-mental parameters are reported in Appendix D. 

1) Full Observability: As shown in Fig. 3a, the communi-cation agent consistently outperforms the vanilla LLM agent across both layouts, with the largest speedups at the queue length of one and diminishing gains as queue length increases. As shown in Fig. 3b, our agent is comparable to the non-communicative baseline across most conditions. Because both agents observe the full object space, the non-communication agent already has access to much of the information commu-nication would otherwise convey, limiting its added benefit. 

2) Partial Observability: The communication agent con-sistently outperforms the vanilla LLM agent, with gains again largest at a queue length of one (Fig. 4a). However, communication now yields a clear advantage over the non-communication baseline (Fig. 4b). This advantage generally grows with queue length and is most pronounced in the asymmetric layout at a queue length of three. With less available knowledge, communication is more important to close the information gap between the agents. V. D ISCUSSION 

Our results show that explicit task allocation and commu-nication provide structure that a vanilla LLM agent lacks: the LLM baseline fails to complete any configuration, while our agent succeeds across observability settings. The value of communication depends on observability. Under full observability, our agent performs similarly to the non-communication baseline because agents can infer collab-orators’ tasks from visible actions. Under partial observability, communication becomes increasingly important, especially as queue length grows, because actions alone no longer reveal enough about collaborators’ tasks or progress. VI. L IMITATIONS 

Our work has two main limitations. First, our communi-cation mechanism is deliberately narrow: utterances convey task-allocation intent but not richer beliefs, questions, negoti-ation, or repair, and the entropy-based speaking trigger may not reflect what a collaborator already knows. Second, our experiments are limited in scale, with one environment family, two layouts, short task queues, two baselines, and six runs per configuration, and they assume known task types, a fixed agent set, and reliable cost-free communication. VII. C ONCLUSION 

We introduce a Bayesian task allocation algorithm for dy-namically arriving tasks and partial object-space observability, using language as probabilistic evidence within an explicit inference procedure. Our results show that this framework enables stronger dynamic task allocation under partial observ-ability than the baselines, and that communication becomes more important as available information decreases.
