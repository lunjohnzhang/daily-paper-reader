Abstract
Continuous-time multi-agent path finding on roadmaps (MAPFR) offers a principled way to coordinate
robot fleets with geometric collision avoidance, yet its deployment on industrial automated guided
vehicle (AGV) infrastructures remains challenging, as highly connected and redundant roadmaps
amplify both safe-interval reasoning and multi-agent interaction handling. To bridge this gap, we
formalize AGV-MAPFR-CTC, a configuration-space variant of MAPFR with precomputed continuous-
time conflicts that enables the reuse of MAPFR solvers for AGV fleet coordination with minimal
changes. We then revisit continuous-time priority-based search (CPBS), identifying the boundary
conditions required by its safe interval path planning (SIPP) low-level planner under priority-induced
constraints and enabling earlier solution detection. As a central contribution, we analyze the structure
of the priority search space, clarifying which agents are affected by each priority refinement and when
replanning can be avoided. Building on this analysis, we introduce a novel algorithm, Lazy-CPBS,
which materializes high-level nodes lazily: a child’s plans are computed only when it is expanded, so
siblings that are never expanded cost no low-level search. Experiments on standard MAPF benchmarks
and on industrial AGV roadmaps imported from a proprietary digital-twin simulator show that CPBS-
based solvers remain effective well beyond the agent counts reachable by optimal MAPFR solvers, while
Lazy-CPBS substantially reduces CPBS overhead and offers the most consistent robustness/runtime
trade-off across industrial layouts.

Introduction
1.1
From MAPF to Continuous-Time Roadmap
Planning
Multi-agent path finding (MAPF) has emerged as a central
problem in both AI and robotics, providing algorithmic
foundations for coordinating large fleets of robots under
collision-avoidance constraints. Its techniques have been
successfully applied in domains such as warehouse logis-
tics [1], office service robots [2], airport towing [3], and
video games [4], and have also inspired solutions to related
problems including 3D pipe routing [5], autonomous driv-
ing in urban environments [6], and other complex routing
and scheduling tasks. In the classical MAPF formulation,
the environment is discretized into a grid and agents move
synchronously in discrete time steps, seeking non-colliding
paths that minimize global objectives such as the sum of
arrival times [7, 8]. Over the last decade, this setting has
led to a rich algorithmic landscape, ranging from opti-
mal [9] and bounded-suboptimal search [10] to subopti-
mal methods that scale to hundreds or even thousands of
agents in seconds [11, 12]. However, discrete-time, grid-
based MAPF remains an abstraction: it disregards the
continuous nature of time, the geometry of real roadmaps,
and the kinematic constraints of industrial vehicles, lim-
iting its direct applicability in real-world domains such as
warehouses with automated forklifts and other car-like au-
tomated guided vehicles (AGVs).
To address these limitations, the continuous-time variant
MAPFR has been proposed, in which agents move along
a generic roadmap, collisions are detected geometrically
rather than via grid-cell conflicts, and actions have arbi-
trary real-valued durations [13]. A key component in this
context is safe interval path planning (SIPP) [14], an A∗-
based algorithm that performs continuous-time planning
by encoding dynamic obstacles as time-varying safe inter-
vals on vertices and edges. SIPP serves as the single-agent
planner underlying most MAPFR algorithms, such as
continuous-time conflict-based search (CCBS) [15]. CCBS
and its recent refinements based on wait discretization [16]
or improved branching rules [17] currently represent the
main optimal and solution-complete baselines for MAPFR.
1.2
Industrial AGV Coordination and Roadmap
Complexity
Unlike autonomous mobile robots (AMRs), which navi-
gate primarily through onboard intelligence [18], indus-
trial AGVs are designed to operate on fixed, predefined
roadmaps [19]. Recent advances in MAPFR therefore ap-
pear as a natural algorithmic candidate for AGV fleet co-
ordination, seemingly lowering the barrier to a direct in-
tegration of multi-agent planning into industrial systems.

In practice, however, most deployments still follow a de-
coupled architecture: each vehicle plans as if it were alone,
while a separate traffic-management layer resolves interac-
tions online [20]. Such controllers typically ensure safety
and liveness through priority rules, local negotiation over
shared resources [21], hierarchical control schemes [22], or
segment-reservation policies [23], rather than through a
globally coordinated multi-agent planner. Although well
established, these approaches can degrade in dense, cus-
tomized warehouse layouts, where congestion, deadlocks,
and suboptimal traffic patterns become increasingly fre-
quent as fleet sizes grow [24].
A key reason why MAPFR techniques remain challenging
to deploy at scale lies in the structure of industrial AGV
roadmaps. Such roadmaps are typically highly connected
and redundant, providing strong connectivity, broad cov-
erage of the free space, and multiple alternative routes
between most locations [21, 25]. While this redundancy
is desirable for robustness and for handling local distur-
bances, it amplifies the combinatorial complexity of both
layers in MAPFR solvers. At the single-agent level, many
methods rely on SIPP, which represents temporal con-
straints by maintaining, for each vertex and edge, the
time windows in which it can be safely occupied. Large
and edge-dense industrial roadmaps inflate the number of
vertex/edge safe intervals, raising memory and bookkeep-
ing overhead.
Moreover, redundancy offers many alter-
native outgoing edges that traverse similar regions of the
roadmap, increasing the branching factor that the low-
level planner must consider. At the multi-agent level, the
same structural property induces dense clusters of tem-
porally overlapping interactions, which in CCBS-like ap-
proaches translates into an explosion in the number of con-
flicts to detect and resolve through branching and repeated
replanning. As a consequence, the scalability observed on
standard MAPFR benchmarks often fails to transfer to
real industrial layouts, and practitioners still overwhelm-
ingly rely on faster coordination schemes.
1.3
Priority-Based Algorithms and the Cost of
Replanning
Within this landscape, priority-based planning offers a
natural compromise between the speed of decoupled co-
ordination and the adaptability of conflict-driven MAPF
solvers. In classical prioritized planning, a total order over
the agents is fixed in advance, and each agent plans its
path while treating all higher-priority agents as moving
obstacles [26]. This yields a simple and fast framework,
but it is well known to be incomplete: a fixed priority
ordering may fail to admit any solution even when an al-
ternative ordering would succeed [27].
To mitigate this
limitation, restart-based variants repeatedly modify the
priority order, either by random resampling [28] or by de-
terministic promotion rules for agents that fail to find a
path [29]. A more principled alternative is priority-based
search (PBS), which systematically explores the space of
priority orderings and searches for a priority structure that
is consistent with a conflict-free solution [30].
Continuous-time priority-based search (CPBS) extends
this idea to MAPFR by combining a PBS-style high-level
search over partial priority relations with a SIPP-based
low-level planner operating in continuous time [31].
In
roadmap-based settings, CPBS can exploit precomputed
continuous-time conflict annotations (CTC). These anno-
tations specify, offline, which pairs of roadmap actions or
resources may lead to a collision for given relative exe-
cution times.
At runtime, they allow the trajectory of
a higher-priority agent to be converted into time-varying
reservations for lower-priority agents without performing
geometric collision checks online. Related offline conflict-
detection and annotation strategies are widely used in in-
dustrial AGV systems [23] and have also been explored in
roadmap-based MAPF planning [32, 33].
The resulting framework is attractive for industrial AGV
coordination, but its performance is strongly affected by
the cost of repeated replanning.
Each new priority re-
finement may require recomputing the path of the lower-
priority agent and, through transitivity, the paths of
all agents whose set of higher-priority predecessors has
changed. We refer to this computation of the multi-agent
plan of a high-level node as its materialization. On large
or highly connected industrial roadmaps, this operation
can be expensive because each replanning step requires re-
building priority-induced safe intervals and running SIPP
over a roadmap with many alternative outgoing motions.
At the same time, the benefit of this effort depends on
the interaction density of the instance. In dense or highly
constrained layouts, where bottlenecks generate many con-
flicts, revising priorities is essential because fixed-order pri-
oritized planning is brittle. In sparse layouts, instead, a
simple priority ordering may already be sufficient, and ea-
ger materialization of high-level nodes can introduce over-
head with limited conflict-resolution benefit.
These observations motivate a lazy variant of CPBS
that avoids materializing nodes that are never expanded.
Rather than replanning immediately for every generated
child node, the proposed Lazy-CPBS delays materializa-
tion until node expansion, so that siblings that are never
expanded cost no low-level search.
The goal is to pre-
serve the robustness of systematic priority revision in con-
gested regimes while approaching the efficiency of priori-
tized planning when interactions are sparse.

Problem Statement
The scope of this work spans both the MAPFR litera-
ture and the specific requirements of industrial AGV co-
ordination. We therefore introduce the problem setting in
three steps. We first recall the classical continuous-time
MAPF problem on generic roadmaps (MAPFR). We then
introduce MAPFR-CTC, where continuous-time conflict
annotations are precomputed and provided as part of the
problem input. Finally, we formalize AGV-MAPFR-CTC,
a configuration-space variant tailored to AGV fleets op-
erating on fixed industrial infrastructures. This modeling
structure is illustrated in Fig. 1.
This separation between the general MAPFR model and
its industrial variant clarifies both the algorithmic con-
text of our approach and the operational constraints under
which our methods will be evaluated.

2.2
MAPFR-CTC
The classical MAPFR formulation defined in Section 2.1
assumes that planners have no prior knowledge of which
pairs of actions may lead to collisions: any potential inter-
action between agents must be detected online through ge-
ometric collision checks. In this sense, MAPFR solvers are
conflict-blind:
all continuous-time interference between
agents must be inferred dynamically during planning.
In many roadmap-based domains, however—including au-
tomated warehouses, factory logistics, and other infras-
tructures where the roadmap is fixed—collision relation-
ships between roadmap elements can be computed offline,
before planning begins. Providing this information as part
of the problem input yields a richer model, which we refer
to as MAPFR-CTC.
A defining feature of the MAPFR-CTC formulation is that
the roadmap is annotated offline with continuous-time
conflicts (CTC). For every vertex–edge and edge–edge
pair, preprocessing computes a maximal, time-translation-
invariant unsafe interval of relative start times:
if two
actions begin within this interval, they are guaranteed
to collide, whereas any larger delay is guaranteed to be
safe [33].
All non-empty intervals are stored as tuples
(v, e, Imax(v, e)) and (e1, e2, Imax(e1, e2)) in the vertex–
edge and edge–edge conflict sets CVEC and CEEC, which
are treated as part of the problem input.
A tuple (e1, e2, Imax(e1, e2))
∈
CEEC means that if
one agent performs move(e1, t) and another executes

checks rather than online geometric tests. See [33] for the
full construction of CVEC and CEEC and a representative
roadmap-annotation example with circular agents.

Continuous-Time Priority-Based
Search
Continuous-time priority-based search (CPBS) [31] is
an incomplete, suboptimal MAPFR-CTC (and AGV-
MAPFR-CTC) solver that extends the classical priority-
based search (PBS) framework [30] to continuous-time set-
tings. It follows a two-level architecture: the high level
explores partial priority orderings among agents, while
the low level performs single-agent replanning using a
SIPP-based planner that treats higher-priority agents as
dynamic obstacles through time-varying reservations on
roadmap resources.
A key difference between CPBS and CCBS lies in how
conflicts are handled. When CPBS imposes a precedence
constraint i ≺j, i, j ∈A, the meaning is global: agent
j ∈A must find a trajectory that avoids every possible
continuous-time interaction with the current trajectory of
agent i ∈A. To enforce this, CPBS must treat the full
timed path of i as a dynamic obstacle for j, inserting time-
stamped reservations not only on the vertices and edges
directly occupied by i, but also on all roadmap elements
that are geometrically blocked by those resources. These
reservations can be constructed online by checking candi-
date roadmap actions against the complete timed trajec-
tories of higher-priority agents. Without annotations, re-
peated low-level searches may require recomputing forbid-
den intervals for many resources, which can be expensive
on large, highly connected roadmaps. CTC annotations
avoid repeating the geometric computation by translating
precomputed conflict relations into time-stamped reserva-
tions.
CCBS instead introduces agent-specific action-time con-
straints from selected conflicts between current plans. Its
low-level planner enforces these constraints without treat-
ing other agents’ complete trajectories as fixed obsta-
cles.
CCBS can use online geometric conflict detection
or precomputed annotations. Priority-based solvers like-
wise remain applicable to MAPFR without CTC annota-
tions. We adopt MAPFR-CTC to reuse precomputed col-
lision geometry across replanning calls, priority branches,

3.1
CPBS High-Level Search
Unlike CCBS-style methods, which maintain and expand
a constraint tree, CPBS relies exclusively on priority re-
lations to solve conflicts, incrementally refining a prior-
ity directed acyclic graph (pDAG) that encodes the local
precedence constraints required to eliminate inter-agent
collisions.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, CPBS performs a depth-first
search [38] over the priority tree (PT), whose nodes en-
code partial priority structures and their associated multi-
agent plans.
A PT node N is defined by a tuple N =
(N.pDAG, N.Π, N.cost, N.Conflicts), where N.pDAG
is a priority directed acyclic graph, N.Π = {π1, . . . , πn} is
a set of continuous-time paths that comply with N.pDAG,
N.cost is the SoC of N.Π, and N.Conflicts is the set of
unresolved pairwise conflicts in the current solution N.Π.

a
priority

agents and ≺⊆A × A specifies precedence constraints.

higher priority than agent j, denoted i ≺j.

≺defines a valid partial order, and therefore admits at

If a

The set of single-agent paths N.Π is said to respect

that collisions between higher- and lower-priority agents

Algorithm 1 illustrates the high-level search procedure of
CPBS. The algorithm initializes the root PT node with an
edge-empty Root.pDAG = (A, ∅) and an initially empty
multi-agent plan Root.Π (line 1). It then constructs the
root plan by calling UpdatePlan once per agent i ∈A,
ensuring that the initial multi-agent plan is computed in
a temporally unconstrained environment, with constraints
introduced only through explicit precedence relations in
the pDAG. Since no precedence relations are enforced yet,
these initial paths treat all other agents as absent; if any
SIPP call fails, the instance is deemed infeasible and the
algorithm terminates immediately (lines 2–6).
Once the initial multi-agent plan Root.Π has been com-
puted, the algorithm tests it for conflicts (line 7). If no
collisions are present, the root node already represents a
valid solution and the search halts. Otherwise, CPBS ini-
tiates a depth-first search over the priority tree: a stack
is created and the root node is pushed onto it (line 10),
after which nodes are extracted and expanded in LIFO or-
der (lines 11–13).
For each extracted PT node N, the
algorithm selects one conflict according to a determin-
istic conflict selection heuristic (in our implementation,
earliest-conflict-time policy, line 14). Let the selected con-
flict involve agents i and j. To solve it, CPBS branches on
the two possible local precedence assignments i ≺j and
j ≺i, thereby generating two child nodes (lines 15–18).
Each child inherits the parent’s pDAG and adds exactly
one new edge: the child priority graph is obtained by ex-
tending the parent pDAG with the corresponding directed
edge choice x ≺y.
Since enforcing x ≺y makes agent y ∈A responsible for
avoiding x ∈A, CPBS invokes UpdatePlan on y, which
recomputes its trajectory and, if necessary, those of all
agents that depend on it through transitivity. This replan-
ning step materializes the child node: the full multi-agent
plan associated with the updated pDAG is generated im-
mediately, and its conflicts can therefore be evaluated on
the spot. Unlike [31, 30], where the goal test is performed
at expansion, this anticipates it to the moment a child is
materialized. As a consequence, if a child node already
yields a conflict-free plan, the solver can recognize it as a
valid solution without exploring the sibling branch. We

3.2
SIPP
CPBS relies on a modified version of SIPP [14] as its single-
agent planner. SIPP extends A∗search [40] by associating
each vertex v ∈V and edge e ∈E with a set of safe inter-
vals (Iv ∈I and Ie ∈I, respectively), i.e., maximal time
windows during which the resource can be used without
colliding with any higher-priority agent. A state is a pair
(v, I), where v ∈V is a vertex and I = [Is, Ie] ∈Iv
is one such interval; its cost g(v, I) is the earliest fea-
sible arrival time at v within I, and its priority key is
f(v, I) = g(v, I)+h(v, ga), where h(v, ga) ∈H is a precom-
puted admissible heuristic. In our implementations we use
the exact shortest-path travel time on the static roadmap
with unbounded resource availability, precomputed once
by running backward Dijkstra search from each goal ver-
tex over the entire roadmap, which is admissible by con-
struction. The OPEN list is ordered by increasing f, and
dominated states are pruned: if a state (v, I) is reached
with an arrival time no better than a previously observed
one, it is discarded [41]. Fig. 3 illustrates how priority-
induced reservations are translated into safe intervals for
the low-level planner.
To ensure soundness under priority-based reasoning,
CPBS requires two boundary conditions on the SIPP low-
level planner that are not necessarily enforced in CCBS-
style implementations.
First, the planner must start
from the earliest safe interval of sa, which must inter-
sect t = 0; allowing SIPP to start from a later interval
(e.g., [45, +∞) when [0, 33] exists) would introduce an ini-
tial wait that may already violate reservations imposed by
higher-priority agents. Second, a plan is accepted as a goal
only if it terminates in the unbounded safe interval of ga:
reaching ga inside a finite interval, such as [33, 190], would
imply that the agent remains at its goal beyond the end
of that interval, thereby postponing an unavoidable future
conflict that CPBS cannot repair within the same priority
assignment.
These conditions are specific to the semantics of priority-
induced reservations. In CCBS-style solvers, SIPP is typi-
cally used inside an incremental repair loop: if a later con-
flict is detected, the high level can introduce an additional
constraint and replan. In CPBS, instead, a precedence re-
lation is interpreted as eliminating all interactions between
the replanned agent and its higher-priority predecessors in
a single low-level call. Therefore, starting from a late safe
interval or accepting a finite goal interval may produce a
plan that is valid for a CCBS repair step but invalid under
CPBS semantics. Enforcing the two boundary conditions
above ensures that SIPP preserves the intended meaning
of the pDAG.
Given a state (v, I), successors are generated by consid-
ering each outgoing edge e = (v, u) and computing the
earliest departure time compatible with the current inter-
val I, the safe intervals of e, and those of u. A successor
(u, J) is added only if the traversal lies entirely within the
corresponding edge and vertex intervals; for each interval
J, only the earliest feasible arrival is retained, since later
departures cannot improve the solution under a monotone
time metric [41]. This SIPP variant is thus both sound for
CPBS and efficient, matching the structure of standard
SIPP while enforcing the additional temporal constraints
required by priority-based continuous-time planning.

Lazy-CPBS
In this section we introduce Lazy-CPBS, a novel algorithm
that explores the PT while postponing replanning until
nodes are expanded.
The CPBS solver described in Section 3.1 already ex-
ploits a depth-first exploration of the PT. At each high-
level step, CPBS incrementally refines the current pDAG
by adding precedence edges between conflicting agents,
thereby constructing a search tree over feasible priority re-
lations. However, whenever a conflict between two agents
⟨i, j⟩is selected, CPBS immediately materializes both chil-
dren. For each child, it augments the pDAG with either
i ≺j or j ≺i and then triggers a full low-level replanning

Priority-Based Search
9
cycle via UpdatePlan. This procedure first resets all safe
intervals on the roadmap to their default “fully safe” state
and then scans the agents in a topological order of the up-
dated pDAG, repeatedly shrinking safe intervals and call-
ing SIPP to replan the lower-priority agent and any agent
that becomes reachable from it in pDAG (4). As a result,
a single high-level branching may induce multiple SIPP
calls and global passes over the safe-interval structure.
This eager strategy becomes particularly costly on sparse
instances. Instance density plays a crucial role in the be-
havior of priority-based methods: as the interaction den-
sity grows, CPBS remains robust by systematically re-
vising priority decisions through precedence constraints.
However, on large layouts the same number of agents in-
duces much sparser interactions. In such regimes, even a
fixed priority ordering is frequently sufficient to solve the
instance, while CPBS may still pay the full cost of re-
peatedly descending the priority tree. As a result, CPBS
can spend a significant portion of its runtime materializing
children that are never expanded, even on instances that
a simple fixed ordering would already solve.
These observations motivate the introduction of Lazy-
CPBS, a lazy variant of CPBS that preserves the same
search space over priority orderings but avoids eagerly
recomputing safe intervals and single-agent plans at ev-
ery high-level decision. Algorithm 2 shows the high-level
search of Lazy-CPBS. The PT has the same meaning as
in CPBS, but each node now stores different information:
besides the current pDAG, a node N keeps a multi-agent
plan N.Π and a replan list N.ReplanList containing ex-
actly the agents to be replanned (Property 3) when N is
expanded.
The search starts by creating the root node Root.
Its
pDAG contains all agents but no precedence edges, rep-
resenting a fully unconstrained priority ordering. No low-
level paths are computed at this point: the root carries
an unmaterialized plan, and the node is simply pushed
onto the STACK (lines 1–3). When a node N is popped
from STACK for expansion (lines 5–6), it is first mate-
rialized.
The current multi-agent plan N.Π (empty at
the root) is updated by replanning exactly the agents in
N.ReplanList (line 7). If materialization fails, the node
is discarded without branching and the high-level search
backtracks; otherwise, N.Π is updated and conflicts are
then checked on the resulting plan (lines 7-9). If the cur-
rent multi-agent plan N.Π is collision-free, it is returned
as a valid solution. Otherwise, a conflict ⟨i, j⟩is selected
for expansion (lines 10–12). As in CPBS, our implementa-
tion adopts the earliest-conflict-time policy. The selected
conflict ⟨i, j⟩is then resolved by branching on the two
possible local precedence relations.
Lazy-CPBS creates
two children, N ′ and N ′′, whose pDAGs extend N.pDAG
with the corresponding precedence edge (lines 13–15). For
each child, the new constraint is injected into the pDAG,
the set of agents to be replanned is extracted in topo-
logical order from the parent (Property 5) and stored in
N ′.ReplanList and N′′.ReplanList (lines 16–17). At this
point, both children inherit a copy of N.Π as a provisional
multi-agent plan, which will only be updated when the
node is expanded and materialized. Finally, the two child
nodes are pushed onto STACK in an order that favors the
node with the smaller replan list as illustrated in Fig. 5
(line 19). In this way, Lazy-CPBS not only performs low-
level materialization for one node at a time, but also biases
the depth-first search towards nodes that require less re-
planning effort, in terms of both safe-interval updates and
SIPP calls, thereby accelerating the descent along the PT.
The MaterializeNode procedure plays the same role as
UpdatePlan in CPBS and replans the same set of agents
(Property 3), the only difference is that this set, together
with its topological order, has already been computed on
the parent (Property 5) and stored in N.ReplanList. For
each agent in this list, the procedure resets the safe inter-
vals, inserts the reservations of its higher-priority agents,
and calls SIPP to compute a new collision-free path (lines
24–27). If all SIPP calls succeed, the multi-agent plan N.Π
is updated accordingly (line 30) and the node N is con-
sidered fully materialized; otherwise, MaterializeNode
returns false and the node is discarded (lines 28 and 7).
Remark 3. Lazy-CPBS inherits the structural Proper-
ties 1–5: it changes only when replanning is performed
and which child is explored first, not the PT itself, and is
therefore amenable to the same parallel high-level explo-
ration strategy discussed in Section 3.3.

5.3
Overall Findings
Across all environments, the observed behaviors are largely
density-driven, where density refers to the effective conges-
tion induced by agents competing for the same free space
and, especially, the same bottlenecks. When instances be-
come dense quickly (e.g., narrow corridors and tight pas-
sages), PP’s fixed-order policy is brittle and often collapses
early due to infeasibility, whereas priority-revising meth-
ods (CPBS and Lazy-CPBS) remain effective over a wider
range of agent counts. Conversely, in environments that
remain relatively sparse as n grows (e.g., open layouts such
as empty256), all suboptimal solvers retain high success
for longer ranges and PP benefits the most from its low
computational overhead, typically dominating runtime by
a wide margin; CPBS/Lazy-CPBS become mainly bene-
ficial near the highest densities, where revising priorities
mitigates the fragility of fixed ordering.
Finally, it is important to note that these instances origi-
nate from discrete-time MAPF benchmarks, where scaling
to hundreds or even thousands of agents is often feasi-
ble. In the continuous-time roadmap setting, feasibility
and runtime are shaped by interaction handling: poten-
tially conflicting motions require continuous-time collision
checking and safe-interval/time-constraint updates, and
planning occurs on geometric roadmaps rather than grids.
As a result, once congestion emerges, computational over-
head increases sharply and the practical “hardness thresh-
old” is reached at substantially smaller agent counts than
in the discrete domain.

6.2
Results and Discussion
The results of the simulations on the five layouts are re-
ported in Fig. 11. For each layout and algorithm, we ana-
lyze runtime, solution SoC, and success rate as the number
of agents increases, with particular attention to the oper-
ating fleet sizes reported in Table 2.
The first observation concerns CCBS. Consistently with
the benchmark-domain experiments, CCBS provides the
lowest SoC when it succeeds, but its scalability remains
limited in the industrial AGV-MAPFR-CTC setting. Its
success rate degrades after only a few agents and vanishes
by about ten agents on every layout. As a result, CCBS
does not reliably reach the operating fleet size on any lay-
out: it solves no instance at the operating points of lay-
outs A and B, and only a fraction of them on layouts C,
D, and E. This behavior is expected for CBS-style opti-
mal solvers: resolving conflicts through a constraint tree
becomes increasingly expensive on redundant roadmaps,
where many alternative low-level paths traverse overlap-
ping regions and generate additional interactions. Thus,
although CCBS remains the strongest method in terms of
solution quality, it is not a viable option at the operating
points of any of the considered layouts.
The second observation concerns prioritized planning. PP
is consistently the fastest method when it succeeds, but it

The restart-based variants PP+RR and PP+DR substan-
tially improve over vanilla PP. By testing multiple priority
orderings, they recover feasible solutions in cases where a
single fixed ordering fails, and around the operating points
they match CPBS-based methods on layouts C and D,
miss at most two instances on layouts A and E, and fall
well behind on layout B. Neither variant dominates the
other: PP+RR is more reliable at intermediate densities
on layout A, whereas PP+DR degrades more gracefully
as density grows and scales further on layouts A and B.
However, both methods remain weakly informed: they ex-
plore complete priority orderings without maintaining a
structured representation of partial priority decisions or
of previously failed priority prefixes. As a consequence,
their performance is more variable across layouts and den-
sity regimes. In dense instances, where only a small frac-
tion of priority orderings may be feasible, restart strate-
gies can spend significant runtime testing orderings that do
not lead to valid plans, whereas CPBS-based methods ex-
ploit partial priority constraints to guide the search more
systematically. Thus, although PP+RR and PP+DR are
effective practical improvements over PP, their lower pre-
dictability near the operating points limits their suitability
as standalone strategies for industrial AGV fleet coordina-
tion.
The strongest overall results are obtained by the CPBS-
based methods.
Both CPBS and Lazy-CPBS maintain
partial priority relations and revise priorities only when
conflicts arise, which makes them substantially more ro-
bust than PP and more consistent than its restart variants
across the congested regimes typical of industrial AGV
roadmaps. Lazy-CPBS further avoids materializing chil-
dren that are never expanded. On layouts A, B, and E,
this translates into markedly lower runtimes than CPBS,
and on layouts B and C it allows Lazy-CPBS to solve in-
stances with more agents than any other method.
The comparison across layouts is, however, more nuanced
than a uniform ranking. On layout A, PP+RR is the most
reliable method up to about 28 agents, CPBS from about
30 to 46 agents, and PP+DR at the highest densities;
Lazy-CPBS stays close to CPBS up to 28 agents and is less
robust beyond, but it is the fastest of the priority-revising
methods. On layout B, Lazy-CPBS has the best success
profile over most of the range and is the only method solv-
ing instances beyond 31 agents, with CPBS and PP+DR
marginally ahead on some instances. On layout C, Lazy-
CPBS remains fully reliable up to 8 agents, when all other
methods have started to fail, and scales the furthest; at in-
termediate densities, the priority-based methods perform
similarly. On layout D, CPBS, Lazy-CPBS, and PP+RR
are nearly indistinguishable in success rate, with Lazy-
CPBS slightly ahead at 5 agents, whereas PP+DR de-
grades earlier; however, PP+RR pays for its reliability
with a sharp runtime increase, up to two orders of mag-
nitude above the other priority-based methods, which all
run in a fraction of a second. On layout E, Lazy-CPBS,
CPBS, and PP+DR largely overlap, with Lazy-CPBS re-
maining fully reliable up to 7 agents and CPBS incurring
higher runtimes at high densities.
Thus, Lazy-CPBS is
not the best method on every layout and density, but it is
the most consistent: every competitor falls clearly behind
on at least one layout, namely CPBS in runtime, PP+RR
through its collapse at higher densities on layouts A and B,
and PP+DR through its lower reliability near the operat-
ing points of layouts A and B.

the operating fleet sizes. In this regime, Lazy-CPBS solves

Finally, the SoC results follow the expected trade-off be-

solve the same instance, CCBS returns the lowest-cost so-
lution, typically followed by CPBS, Lazy-CPBS, and the
prioritized-planning baselines. The restart-based variants
produce solutions of comparable quality to Lazy-CPBS,
but with less predictable success.
6.3
Overall Findings
The industrial evaluation identifies CPBS-based planners
as the most promising methods among the evaluated alter-
natives for realistic AGV roadmap coordination. In par-
ticular, Lazy-CPBS provides the strongest overall robust-
ness/runtime compromise: it remains reliable under con-
gestion and bottlenecks, where fixed or weakly informed
priority strategies are less consistent across layouts, while
substantially reducing the replanning overhead of CPBS
and avoiding the scalability limitations of CCBS.
At the same time, the current implementation does not yet
meet the strictest industrial requirements for real-time co-
ordination of very large AGV fleets. However, CPBS and
Lazy-CPBS provide concrete directions for further accel-
eration because they are grounded in a principled, sys-
tematic search structure.
In particular, the structural
properties discussed in Section 3.3 indicate that differ-
ent regions of the priority-search space can be explored
independently, suggesting that parallel high-level imple-
mentations could reach feasible priority structures earlier.
This stands in contrast to heuristic prioritized-planning
enhancements such as PP+RR and PP+DR, which may
occasionally yield strong performance but tend to be less
predictable and less consistent across layouts and traffic
regimes.

Experimental Design
We introduce an intermediate CPBS-based variant, Eager-
RL, which materializes both children at every expansion
as CPBS does, but orders them by |ReplanList| as Lazy-
CPBS does. Comparing Eager-RL with Lazy-CPBS iso-
lates the effect of lazy materialization. To make this com-
parison exact, Eager-RL is run here without the early goal
test (i.e., plans are checked for conflicts at node expansion
rather than at materialization): the two algorithms then
expand the same sequence of nodes, so their runtime dif-
ference is due to lazy materialization alone. Comparing
CPBS with Eager-RL, both with the early goal test, iso-
lates instead the effect of the child ordering.
Table 3 reports means over the instances solved by all
variants. For the first comparison, d is the depth of the
solution node, ∆m the number of materializations saved by
Lazy-CPBS, ¯tm the mean time of one eager materializa-
tion, and ρ the runtime of Lazy-CPBS relative to Eager-
RL. The predicted saving ∆tpred = ∆m ¯tm is compared
with the measured one, ∆tmeas. For the second compari-
son, the table reports the number of expanded nodes and
of SIPP calls under SoC (S) and ReplanList (R) ordering.
7.2
Results and Discussion
When a node is expanded, Lazy-CPBS materializes the
child it pops next and leaves its sibling unmaterialized
on the stack, whereas eager search materializes both. A
sibling that is popped later, upon backtracking, is mate-
rialized by both algorithms. The savings therefore come
from the siblings that are never popped, of which there is
at most one per level of the branch from the root to the
solution node, so ∆m ≤d.
On the benchmark roadmaps, backtracking is rare and ∆m
almost coincides with d. On the industrial layouts, which
are denser, backtracking is more frequent and ∆m falls
below d. In all settings but one (layout C), ∆tpred un-
derestimates the measured saving. The reason is that the
skipped sibling is the child with the longer replan list, so its
materialization costs more than the average ¯tm. Overall,
lazy materialization reduces the runtime by a factor of 2–4
on the benchmark roadmaps and by 22–59% on the indus-

trial layouts. The gain is smaller on the industrial layouts
because backtracking and failed materializations reduce
the share of materializations that can be saved: ∆m is
about half of the eager materializations on the benchmark
roadmaps, but only 25–47% on the industrial layouts.
With eager materialization, SoC ordering expands fewer
nodes than ReplanList ordering in most settings, up to
about 20% fewer, i.e., it selects slightly better children.
However, the child with the lower SoC often requires re-
planning a larger part of the pDAG. On the benchmark
roadmaps, SoC ordering therefore performs up to 41%
more SIPP calls, each more expensive on average, and
CPBS is 1.5–2.6× slower than Eager-RL. The only excep-
tion is empty16, where the two orderings are on par. On
the industrial layouts, the two orderings differ by approx-
imately 10% in SIPP calls and 11% in runtime.
Hence, on the benchmark roadmaps the weaker guid-
ance of ReplanList ordering is more than compensated
by cheaper materializations, and it is the only one of the
two orderings that is compatible with lazy materializa-
tion. There, the speed-up of Lazy-CPBS over CPBS com-
bines lazy materialization, the larger contribution, and the
cheaper child ordering. On the industrial layouts, it stems
from lazy materialization alone: its absolute size is gov-
erned by ∆m ¯tm, and its relative size by the share of ma-
terializations saved.
8
Conclusion
In this paper, we studied priority-based continuous-time
MAPFR on industrial AGV roadmaps. We first revisited
the algorithmic foundations of this setting by analyzing
the interaction between priority-induced reservations and
the SIPP low-level planner. This clarified the SIPP bound-
ary conditions required by priority-induced reservations
and enabled earlier solution detection in CPBS.
As a key contribution, we analyzed the priority-based
search space induced by partial precedence relations
among agents.
The resulting structural properties clar-
ify how priority decisions propagate through the search,
which agents are affected by a refinement, and why dif-
ferent branches of the priority space can be treated inde-
pendently. Beyond their theoretical interest, these prop-
erties provide concrete guidance for engineering more ef-
ficient priority-based algorithms, including parallel high-
level exploration. Building on this analysis, we introduced
Lazy-CPBS, a novel algorithm that delays node material-
ization until expansion. By avoiding redundant low-level
replanning, Lazy-CPBS improves the practical scalability
of priority-based continuous-time planning while preserv-
ing the robustness benefits of systematic priority revision
under congestion. An ablation study showed that these
savings are bounded by the depth of the solution in the
priority tree, and that the replan-list ordering required by
laziness is itself cheaper than SoC ordering on the bench-
mark roadmaps.
We grounded the proposed approach in industrial AGV
coordination by formalizing AGV-MAPFR-CTC and eval-
uating the methods on both standard MAPF bench-
marks and industrial AGV roadmaps imported from a
proprietary digital-twin simulator. The results show that
priority-based MAPFR solvers provide a strong robust-
ness/runtime compromise and that Lazy-CPBS is a prac-
tical step toward centralized AGV fleet coordination on
realistic warehouse roadmaps.
Future research will focus on further accelerating CPBS
and Lazy-CPBS through structural speed-up techniques,
including the design of zone-distributed variants to bet-
ter exploit large and highly redundant roadmaps.
We
also plan to study theoretical guarantees of completeness
and to develop a structurally efficient multi-threaded im-
plementation. In addition, we aim to extend CPBS and
Lazy-CPBS to a lifelong planning setting and integrate
them into a novel AGV traffic manager capable of han-
dling continuous streams of tasks under real-time indus-
trial constraints. Taken together, these directions aim to
further narrow the gap between theory and practice, ad-
vancing both the academic foundations and the industrial
applicability of continuous-time MAPF.
