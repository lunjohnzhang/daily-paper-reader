Scalable Iterative Multi-Agent Path Finding under Turn Constraints
Zheyu Taoa, Chunlong Yua,∗, Yicheng Hua
aSchool of Mechanical Engineering, Tongji University, Caoan Road 4800, 201804, Shanghai, China
Abstract
Large-scale multi-mobile-robot systems require fast and reliable path planning under realistic motion constraints.
Multi-Agent Path Finding with turn actions (MAPFT) explicitly models orientation changes and turn-induced delays,
but these constraints increase the computational burden of search-based methods. Priority-inheritance-based planning
offers a highly scalable alternative, yet directly extending it to MAPFT is nontrivial because turn actions break the
immediate node-vacation assumption underlying conventional priority inheritance. To address this challenge, this
paper proposes PIBT*
T, an efficient iterative solver for MAPFT. PIBT*
T advances scalable multi-agent coordination
by preserving the lightweight single-timestep planning mechanism of Priority Inheritance with Backtracking (PIBT),
while introducing dedicated mechanisms to handle turn-induced dependencies, local deadlocks, livelock, and ineffi-
cient repeated pushing. Experiments on standard MAPF benchmarks show that PIBT*
T achieves high success rates and
strong scalability, solving instances with up to 1,000 agents within seconds. For realistic engineering environments,
Action Dependency Graph (ADG)-based execution simulation and a flexible manufacturing system case study show
that turn-aware planning better reflects robot execution and reduces actual makespan compared with classical MAPF
planning. Experiments with TurtleBot4 robots further validate the practical feasibility of the proposed method.
Keywords: Multi-mobile-robot systems, multi-agent path finding, collision avoidance, motion and path planning,
turn actions
1. Introduction
Multi-mobile-robot systems (MMRSs) are increasingly deployed in automated warehouses, flexible manufactur-
ing systems, intelligent transportation, and other large-scale autonomous systems (Liu et al., 2023). Their effectiveness
depends critically on the ability to coordinate large robot fleets safely and efficiently, particularly in confined environ-
ments where robot interactions are frequent and replanning must be performed online. This creates a practical demand
for path-planning methods that are not only computationally scalable, but also sufficiently consistent with the motion
capabilities of physical robots.
Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) provides a widely used discrete framework for coordinating multiple robots
(Stern et al., 2019). Recent developments, including bounded-suboptimal conflict-based search (Li et al., 2021c),
large-neighborhood-search methods (Li et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025), and LaCAM-style search (Okumura, 2023b;
Idgar et al., 2026), have enabled increasingly large MAPF instances to be solved efficiently. However, classical
MAPF typically assumes that an agent can move to any neighboring vertex within one timestep, regardless of its

robot must first rotate toward its desired direction before moving forward (Yan and Li, 2025). Consequently, a path

This discrepancy is illustrated in Fig. 1. A classical MAPF planner may favor staircase-like paths because move-
ments in different directions are assigned identical unit costs. When executed by a mobile robot, however, each
direction change introduces additional rotational, acceleration/deceleration, and synchronization delays. Explicitly
modeling turn actions at the planning level can therefore produce paths that better reflect physical execution. This
motivates Multi-Agent Path Finding with turn actions (MAPFT), in which orientation is included in the agent state
and in-place rotations are represented explicitly. MAPFT improves motion fidelity, but the enlarged state space and
additional temporal dependencies make large-scale coordination substantially more difficult.
Existing high-level MAPFT methods are predominantly search-based. Orientation-augmented ECBS formulations
incorporate turning costs into bounded-suboptimal planning (Varambally et al., 2022; Hönig et al., 2019), while CBST
explicitly models turn actions within conflict-based search and provides optimal solutions (Zhang et al., 2023). These
methods demonstrate the benefit of incorporating realistic actions during high-level planning, but their computational
burden increases as the number of agents and conflicts grows. This limitation is important in logistics and manufac-
turing applications, where hundreds or even thousands of robots may need to be coordinated within highly structured
environments.
Priority Inheritance with Backtracking (PIBT) (Okumura et al., 2022) provides an attractive alternative because
it generates only the next joint configuration through lightweight priority inheritance and recursive backtracking,
enabling real-time coordination of very large agent populations. Extending this mechanism to MAPFT, however, is
nontrivial. Conventional PIBT implicitly couples an accepted request to a neighboring vertex with the release of
the agent’s current vertex. Under turn constraints, these two events become temporally decoupled: an agent may
successfully request a neighboring vertex but first rotate in place, leaving its current vertex occupied at the next
2


timestep. This delayed vertex release disrupts the node-based dependency reasoning underlying priority inheritance
and may create cyclic or persistent local interactions that cannot be handled effectively by conventional PIBT (Jiang
et al., 2024).
The preliminary conference work, PIBTT, was the first, to the best of our knowledge, to extend priority-inheritance-
based iterative planning to MAPFT, demonstrating that explicit turn actions can be incorporated while retaining
lightweight single-timestep planning (Tao and Yu, 2025). Nevertheless, the resulting local coordination remains vul-
nerable to several failure modes. Mutual blocking can prevent agents from making progress, repeated displacement
of goal-reaching agents can delay or prevent simultaneous termination, and repeated pushing can introduce substan-
tial detours and degrade solution quality. These limitations become increasingly important in dense interactions and
restrictive map structures.
Building on this foundation, this paper develops PIBT∗
T, a more robust priority-inheritance-based framework for
large-scale MAPFT. The proposed method preserves the computationally efficient single-timestep planning paradigm
of PIBT, while strengthening its local coordination mechanisms to address deadlock, livelock, and inefficient repeated
pushing. The resulting framework is evaluated not only in terms of planning performance and scalability, but also
with respect to realistic robot execution through ADG-based simulation, a flexible manufacturing system case study,
and physical TurtleBot4 experiments.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
1. We develop PIBT∗
T, a robust and scalable priority-inheritance-based solver for MAPFT. The proposed frame-
work preserves lightweight single-timestep planning while improving coordination under turn-induced temporal
dependencies.
2. We introduce two complementary coordination mechanisms to address characteristic failure modes in turn-
aware priority-inheritance planning. The swap pattern detector (SPD) identifies local blocking configurations
and enables cooperative yielding to resolve deadlock, while the k-Push Escape Trigger (PET) exploits inter-
action history to mitigate livelock and inefficient repeated pushing. Their individual and combined effects are
systematically examined through ablation experiments.
3. We provide a comprehensive evaluation of both algorithmic and execution-level performance. Standard MAPF
benchmarks and large-scale stress tests demonstrate high success rates and scalability for up to 1,000 agents
with limited computational overhead. ADG-based execution simulation and a flexible manufacturing system
case study quantify the gap between discrete planning cost and realistic execution cost, while experiments with
TurtleBot4 robots further validate the practical applicability of the proposed approach.

2. Related work
Existing studies related to this work can be broadly organized along two research directions. The first incorpo-
rates realistic motion constraints, particularly orientation and turn actions, into MAPF planning and execution. The
second focuses on scalable MAPF algorithms, especially priority-inheritance-based methods that enable lightweight
coordination for large agent populations. We review these two directions and then position the present work at their
intersection.

2.1. MAPF with realistic motion constraints
Classical multi-agent path finding (MAPF) models agents as moving between adjacent graph vertices in discrete
timesteps, typically abstracting away orientation, turning time, and other motion dynamics (Stern et al., 2019). This
abstraction facilitates efficient high-level coordination, but may underestimate the execution cost of paths containing
frequent direction changes. For mobile robots that move forward and rotate in place, explicitly accounting for turn
actions is therefore important for obtaining plans that are temporally consistent with physical execution.
One line of research addresses realistic motion primarily at the execution level. MAPF-POST converts discrete
MAPF paths into executable schedules subject to kinematic constraints (Hönig et al., 2016), while the action depen-
dency graph (ADG) represents precedence relationships between robot actions to coordinate robust execution (Hönig
et al., 2019). Subsequent studies improve execution flexibility by relaxing unnecessary dependencies, changing pass-
ing orders during execution, or reducing redundant waiting (Berndt et al., 2023; Su et al., 2024; Dunkel, 2024). The
SMART testbed and subsequent analyses further show that the performance of a multi-robot system depends jointly
on the high-level planner and the execution framework (Yan et al., 2026a,b). These approaches demonstrate that a
discrete MAPF solution can be converted into a physically executable motion schedule. However, execution-level
adjustment does not ensure that the high-level routes themselves account for the temporal cost of turning and may
lead to worse solution quality (Yan et al., 2026b). This distinction motivates MAPF formulations that incorporate
realistic actions directly into the planning process.
A second line of research explicitly models orientation and turning during high-level planning. Orientation-
augmented ECBS formulations provide bounded-suboptimal planning with turn actions, and computational studies
demonstrate the benefit of accounting for rotations in warehouse environments (Varambally et al., 2022). Their action
abstraction, however, adopts a simplified representation of rotational motion and does not fully distinguish the execu-
tion costs associated with turns of different angular magnitudes. CBST further develops an optimal solver for MAPFT
by incorporating turn actions into conflict-based search together with symmetry reasoning and search acceleration
(Zhang et al., 2023). Other studies consider richer motion models using different planning paradigms. TP-SIPPwRT
combines token passing with safe-interval planning for kinematically constrained pickup-and-delivery tasks and pro-
vides completeness for well-formed instances (Ma et al., 2019). A three-level stationary-state search called MASS
models orientation, velocity, and acceleration for differential-drive robots (Yan and Li, 2025). Collectively, these stud-
ies establish the value of explicitly modeling robot motion, although their formulations, guarantees, and demonstrated
scales differ substantially.
MAPFT introduces difficulties beyond the increase in state-space size. In particular, a rotation may consume time
while the agent continues to occupy its current vertex. Consequently, an intended transition to a neighboring vertex
does not necessarily coincide with the release of the currently occupied vertex. Such delayed vertex release introduces
additional temporal dependencies into multi-agent coordination and complicates conflict resolution. In global search
frameworks such as CBST, dense interactions may further require extensive exploration of the constraint tree. A key
challenge is therefore to retain explicit turn modeling while coordinating large agent populations without incurring
excessive global-search overhead.
2.2. Scalable and priority-inheritance-based MAPF
MAPF scalability has been pursued through several complementary strategies. EECBS accelerates conflict-based
search while retaining a prescribed suboptimality bound (Li et al., 2021c). Large-neighborhood-search methods im-
prove feasible plans by repeatedly replanning selected subsets of agents, while MAPF-LNS2 focuses on conflict
4


repair and LNS2+RL incorporates learned coordination into the repair process (Li et al., 2021a, 2022; Wang et al.,
2025). For lifelong settings, RHCR reduces planning complexity through temporal decomposition, repeatedly solv-
ing MAPF over a limited planning horizon (Li et al., 2021d). These approaches improve scalability by restricting
the search space, replanning scope, or temporal horizon while retaining a search- or replanning-based coordination
structure.
Priority-inheritance-based planning provides a different approach to large-scale coordination. PIBT constructs
the next joint configuration using dynamic priorities, priority inheritance, and recursive backtracking, enabling highly
efficient local coordination without explicitly constructing a large global search tree (Okumura et al., 2022). The same
mechanism also serves as an efficient successor generator within higher-level search frameworks. LaCAM performs
search over joint configurations while using constrained PIBT to generate successor configurations (Okumura, 2023b).
LaCAM* extends this framework toward eventual optimality for makespan and sum of loss, and subsequent variants
further improve makespan-oriented search (Okumura, 2023a, 2024; Idgar et al., 2026). Real-Time LaCAM maintains
such search incrementally across execution steps while preserving completeness (Liang et al., 2025). These develop-
ments show that lightweight PIBT-based configuration generation can support both direct iterative coordination and
more systematic global search.
For PIBT used directly as an iterative planner, however, its behavior depends strongly on the structural assumptions
underlying local configuration generation. Existing extensions address different limitations of the original framework.
WinPIBT enlarges the planning window (Okumura et al., 2019b), Causal-PIBT introduces asynchronous action depen-
dencies (Okumura et al., 2021), and temporary-priority mechanisms mitigate deadlocks in maps containing tree-like
regions (Fujitani et al., 2022). Improved preference construction further enhances local action selection (Okumura and
Nagai, 2025), while Anytime PIBT shows that improving individual single-step decisions does not necessarily lead to
comparable improvements in complete solution paths (Gandotra et al., 2025). These extensions retain the efficiency
of local priority inheritance while addressing specific coordination limitations.
Introducing turn actions poses a more fundamental difficulty for conventional PIBT reasoning. In the classical
action model, accepting a request to move to a neighboring vertex is closely associated with vacating the current
vertex. With explicit rotations, an agent may instead accept such a request, rotate first, and continue occupying its
current vertex for one or more action steps. The intended movement and the actual release of the occupied vertex are
therefore temporally decoupled. This mismatch complicates the node-based dependency reasoning on which priority
inheritance and recursive backtracking rely (Jiang et al., 2024).
2.3. Positioning of the present work
Taken together, the above studies reveal a gap between realistic turn-aware planning and scalable local coordina-
tion. Existing search-based MAPFT methods explicitly model turn actions, but their computational burden can grow
substantially with the number of agents and conflicts. In contrast, PIBT provides highly efficient priority-inheritance-
based coordination, yet its node-based reasoning becomes nontrivial under turn-induced delayed vertex release.
To the best of our knowledge, the preliminary conference work, PIBTT, was the first to extend priority-inheritance-
based iterative planning to MAPFT, demonstrating that lightweight single-timestep planning can be retained under
explicit turn constraints (Tao and Yu, 2025). However, the resulting coordination mechanism remains vulnerable
to local blocking, repeated displacement of goal-reaching agents, and inefficient pushing behaviors, particularly in
dense interactions and restrictive map structures. Building on this foundation, the present study develops PIBT∗
T, a
more robust turn-aware priority-inheritance framework for large-scale MAPFT. The proposed method strengthens
5


local coordination by explicitly addressing the failure modes that remain in PIBTT, while preserving its lightweight
single-timestep planning paradigm.
3. Problem description
1) Input: MAPFT considers a set of agents A = {a1, a2, . . . , ak} operating on an undirected grid graph G = (V, E).
The initial configuration of each agent is defined by the tuple si[0] = (πi[0], θi[0]), which consists of its starting node
πi[0] ∈V and initial orientation θi[0] ∈{North, East, S outh, West}. Each agent is further assigned a specific goal
node gi ∈V, and no terminal orientation is required.
2) Action model: At each timestep, an agent can execute one of three actions: move forward to the adjacent node
in its current orientation, rotate by 90 degrees clockwise or counterclockwise while remaining in place, or wait in
place without changing its orientation.
3) Conflict: At any timestep, a conflict occurs if two or more agents attempt either of the following: occupy the
same node, which constitutes a vertex conflict; or traverse the same edge in opposite directions, which constitutes an
edge conflict.
4) Solution:
A solution to MAPFT is a set of action sequences, one for each agent, that move the agents from
their initial states to their respective goal nodes while ensuring that the entire process is free of conflicts.
5) Performance metrics:
The quality of an MAPFT solution can be evaluated using the following metrics: The
Sum of costs (SoC) is defined as the total number of timesteps taken by all agents from their initial states until they
reach and remain at their respective goal nodes. The makespan is the elapsed time until all agents have reached their
goal nodes, which is equivalently the timestep at which the last agent arrives at its goal.
4. Methodology
This section presents the proposed PIBT*
T method for MAPFT. The method aims to preserve the lightweight
single-timestep planning structure of PIBT while extending it to an orientation-aware action model. We first review
the original PIBT framework and explain why its immediate node-vacation assumption becomes invalid under turn
constraints. We then introduce the operational mechanism of PIBT*
T, including the recursive priority inheritance pro-
cedure and the turn-aware action update function. Next, we present several dedicated mechanisms for addressing
the algorithmic difficulties that arise in MAPFT, including cycle handling for circular request chains, a local greedy
strategy for orientation-aware node selection, a swap pattern detector for local deadlock resolution, and a k-push es-
cape trigger for mitigating livelock and inefficient repeated pushing behaviors. Finally, we analyze the computational
complexity of the proposed algorithm.
4.1. PIBT
PIBT is built around a simple priority-based planning mechanism. At each timestep, agents are ordered accord-
ing to their priorities and plan their next configurations sequentially from the highest priority to the lowest priority.
Each agent selects a preferred node from its candidate set, typically consisting of its current node and neighboring
nodes, and attempts to reserve that node for the next timestep. Through this sequential reservation process, PIBT
incrementally constructs a conflict-free joint configuration for the next timestep.
The core mechanism of PIBT is to resolve conflicts through priority inheritance. When an agent ai requests a node
that is currently occupied by a lower-priority agent a j, ai temporarily transfers its priority to a j and forces aj to move
6


away from the requested node. If a j is itself blocked by another lower-priority agent, the same process is applied
recursively, forming a chain of priority inheritance until all blocking agents attempt to relocate.
This simple configuration-generation mechanism gives PIBT very low computational overhead, enabling it to
solve large-scale MAPF instances efficiently. However, directly applying PIBT to MAPFT is nontrivial. In MAPFT,
an agent may need to rotate in place before moving into a requested neighboring node. Consequently, a successful
node request does not necessarily imply that the agent’s current node will be vacated at the next timestep. This violates
the immediate node-vacation assumption underlying the recursive conflict-resolution logic of PIBT. To address this
limitation, we propose PIBT*
T.
4.2. Operational mechanism of PIBT*
T
Before presenting the detailed procedure, we highlight how PIBT*
T modifies the original PIBT mechanism. In
PIBT, node reservation and next-step occupation are tightly coupled: once a node request is accepted, the correspond-
ing agent is assumed to move into the requested node at the next timestep. PIBT*
T decouples these two operations.
The recursive priority-inheritance procedure is still used to determine requested nodes, but each accepted request is
converted into an executable next configuration by a turn-aware action update function. This design allows an agent
to rotate, move forward, or wait according to its current orientation, while additional checks are introduced to handle
circular request chains caused by turn-induced delays.
Algorithm 1 describes one planning step of PIBT*
T at timestep t. Before the iterative planning process starts, each
agent is assigned a unique initial priority value in (0, 1), which is used as a tie-breaker. At the beginning of each
timestep, priorities are updated according to the same rule as in PIBT: the priority of an agent that has not reached
its goal is increased, whereas the priority of an agent already at its goal is reset to its initial value. Agents are then
processed in descending order of priority. If the next configuration of agent ai has not yet been determined, the
recursive procedure PIBT*
T(ai, NULL) is invoked.
The recursive procedure PIBT*
T(ai, aj) determines a feasible next configuration for agent ai, where a j denotes the
parent agent that has requested ai to move, if such an agent exists. The procedure first constructs the candidate set
Pi, which consists of the current node of ai and its neighboring nodes. These candidates are ordered by the node
selection strategy introduced in Section 4.6. The algorithm then examines the candidates sequentially according to
this preference order.
For a candidate node u, the algorithm first checks whether u has already been reserved by another agent at timestep
t +1, or whether u is the current node of the parent agent aj. The former condition prevents two agents from reserving
the same next-step node, while the latter avoids an immediate swap with the parent agent. If either condition holds,
candidate u is skipped.
If u is currently occupied by another agent ak whose next configuration has not yet been determined, priority
inheritance is triggered by recursively calling PIBT*
T(ak, ai). In this way, ak temporarily inherits the priority of ai and
attempts to determine its own next configuration first. If the recursive call fails, ai continues to test the next candidate
node in Pi.
In addition to the standard recursive inheritance process, PIBT*
T checks whether the current node request closes a
circular request chain. If such a cycle is detected, the cycle-handling mechanism is invoked to determine consistent
next-step configurations for all agents involved in the cycle. The details of this mechanism are given in Section 4.5.

4.3. Action update function
Given the current state (πi[t], θi[t]) of agent ai and a requested node u, the Compute_action function (line 27,
Algorithm 1) determines the first executable action toward u as follows:
1. If the requested node lies in the agent’s current facing direction, the agent moves forward.
2. If the requested direction is 90° to the left or right of the current orientation, the agent performs a single 90°
rotation toward that direction.
3. If the requested direction is opposite (180° difference), the agent performs a 90° counterclockwise rotation.
4. If the requested node is the agent’s current node, the agent remains in place and executes no action.
This function is the key component that decouples node requests from actual motion execution. A requested
neighboring node represents the intended movement direction, whereas the executable next configuration may be a
forward movement, an in-place rotation, or a wait action. The following example illustrates how node requests and
action updates interact in PIBT*
T.

4.5. Cycle handling approach
When turn actions are incorporated, the node request mechanism inherited from PIBT may fail in the presence of
circular request chains. Figure 3(a) illustrates such a case, where agents 1, 2, 3, and 4 have goal nodes A, B, C, and D,
respectively. Following the node request mechanism described in Section 4.2, all agents successfully request their
goal nodes. However, agent 3 must first rotate in place to align with its requested direction. This turn-induced delay
prevents agents 1 and 2 from moving as expected and ultimately leads to a vertex conflict between agents 4 and 1.
This example shows that, in MAPFT, a circular request chain may invalidate the conflict-resolution logic based on
recursive backtracking. We refer to such a request structure as a cycle and formalize it as follows.
Definition 1. Consider a sequence of agents C = ⟨a1, a2, . . . , an⟩(n > 2) involved in a recursive priority inheritance
chain at timestep t. Let πi[t] denote the current node occupied by agent ai, and let vi[t + 1] denote the target node
requested by ai. We define C as a cycle if and only if:
1. For all 1 ≤i < n, agent ai requests the node currently occupied by its successor ai+1, i.e., vi[t + 1] = πi+1[t];
2. The last agent an requests the node occupied by the first agent a1, i.e., vn[t + 1] = π1[t].

Proposition 1. Let C = ⟨a1, a2, . . . , an⟩be a cycle formed during recursive priority inheritance at timestep t. The
only potential collision induced by this cycle can occur between the terminator agent an and the initiator agent a1. In

Proof. In the recursive planning framework, the recursion unfolds along the request chain until it reaches the termi-
nator agent an, which determines its next-step action first and passes the resulting occupation status back up the chain.
Consider any intermediate pair (ai, ai+1) with 1 ≤i < n. Since agent ai+1 determines its action before ai, agent ai can
evaluate whether πi+1[t] will be vacated at timestep t + 1. Under the MAPFT action model, an agent may either move
forward, rotate 90◦in place, or wait in place. If ai+1 is not aligned with its requested node, then its next-step action
is an in-place rotation, and therefore πi+1[t] remains occupied at timestep t + 1. This information is available to ai
through recursive backtracking. Hence, even if ai is aligned with its requested node, it updates its action to wait in
place once it detects that the node will remain occupied at timestep t + 1. Therefore, no collision can occur between
any intermediate pair (ai, ai+1). The above safeguard does not necessarily apply to the pair (an, a1). When an deter-
mines its action, agent a1 is still in the recursion stack and its next-step state has not yet been finalized. Consequently,
an cannot know whether π1[t] will actually be vacated at timestep t+1. If an is aligned with π1[t] and chooses to move
forward, while a1 is subsequently forced to rotate or wait in place, then a collision may occur. Therefore, among the
conflicts induced by the cycle, the only unresolved one can occur between an and a1.
To eliminate the vulnerability identified in Proposition 1, a naive conservative rule would simply block the termi-
nator agent whenever the initiator’s next position is uncertain. While safe, such a rule may unnecessarily freeze the
whole cycle and delay progress. Instead, we propose the cycle handling approach shown in Figure 4, which preserves
safety while allowing the cycle to make progress whenever possible. Once a cycle C is detected, the algorithm first
checks whether all agents in C are already aligned with their requested directions. If so, all of them move simultane-
ously to their requested nodes. Otherwise, in the next timestep, agents in C that are not aligned perform turn actions,
while the remaining agents in C wait in place, as illustrated in Figure 3(b).
Proposition 2. For any detected cycle C, the proposed cycle handling approach guarantees a conflict-free next-step
update for all agents in C.
11


Proof. We consider the two cases of the cycle handling approach:
1. All agents in C are aligned. In this case, every agent in C moves forward to its requested node. Since C is
a cycle, each requested node is exactly the current node of another agent in C, and each such node is vacated
simultaneously by that agent. Therefore, no two agents move into the same node, and no agent moves into a
node that remains occupied. Moreover, the motions follow the directed cycle consistently, so no edge conflict
occurs. Hence, the simultaneous update is conflict-free.
2. At least one agent in C is not aligned. In this case, every misaligned agent performs an in-place turn, while
every other agent in C waits in place. Thus, no agent in C attempts to enter a different node at timestep t + 1.
Since the configuration at timestep t is collision-free, keeping all agents in C on their current nodes preserves
collision-freeness at timestep t + 1. Therefore, the next-step update is also conflict-free.
Since both cases yield a conflict-free next-step update for all agents in C, the proposed cycle handling approach is
correct.
4.6. Node selection strategy
In PIBT-based algorithms, the node selection strategy (line 10, Algorithm 1) determines the next preferred position
for each agent. The node selection strategy plays a key role in the overall performance of the algorithm. An effective
strategy can resolve deadlocks among agents, guide them to their goals efficiently, and consequently enhance the
operational efficiency of the entire system.
Local greedy strategy. In the original PIBT algorithm, candidate nodes are ordered by their distances to the
agent’s goal, where distances are computed on the static map (ignoring other agents) and can thus be preprocessed
using A∗. However, under MAPFT, turn actions introduce additional costs that invalidate such heuristics: the cost of
reaching the goal through an intermediate node depends not only on spatial distance but also on orientation transitions.
This makes classical distance heuristics inaccurate for ranking neighboring nodes.
To address this challenge, we introduce a Local Greedy Strategy (LGS) that sorts candidate nodes according to
the following cost function:
TC(s, vi, g) = CC(s, vi) + RC(s′, g),
(1)
where TC(s, vi, g) represents the total cost of reaching the goal node g from the current state s via a neighboring
node vi. The term CC(s, vi) denotes the cost from s to the first state s′ upon arriving at vi, while RC(s′, g) denotes
the remaining cost from s′ to g. We next show that, in the single-agent setting, this ranking rule is consistent with
shortest-path optimality.

Swap pattern detector. The swap pattern detector is designed to resolve deadlock and livelock situations that may
arise in PIBT-based planning. In the original PIBT, all agents are guaranteed to reach their goal nodes at least once
within finite time under the condition that every pair of adjacent nodes belongs to a simple cycle. When this condition
is violated, agents may stop moving after a certain timestep, resulting in deadlock. As illustrated in Figure 5(a), a
higher-priority agent a1 requests node C, currently occupied by a2. The recursive PIBT*
T procedure for a2 fails because
moving to B or staying at C would conflict with a1. Consequently, a1 is forced to remain at B according to the LGS,
leading to a state of mutual obstruction. Even when the condition is satisfied, agents may alternately reach their goals
due to priority resets without making simultaneous progress, leading to livelock.
Most deadlock and livelock cases stem from local mutual blocking between two agents and can be resolved
through a simple cooperative swap, rather than strictly following the LGS. Motivated by this observation, we introduce
a swap pattern detector (SPD) inspired by Okumura (2023a), which identifies situations where a higher-priority agent
should temporarily yield to enable such cooperation (Algorithm 2).
After obtaining the preference list Pi for agent ai, the detector first checks whether the most preferred node Pi[0] is

occupied by another agent aj. If so, a two-stage simulation is performed to determine whether a swap is both required
and possible. The first stage evaluates swap necessity by simulating ai pushing toward a j while a j is displaced to
adjacent nodes. A swap is deemed required if (i) a j is forced into a dead-end corridor and the relative goal distances
indicate a “crossing” situation, i.e., ai is closer to gj than a j is, and a j is closer to gi than ai is. (ii) ai reaches its goal
but blocks a j’s only path forward. In both cases, strictly following the LGS would trap aj, whereas a swap allows both
agents to progress. If a j instead reaches a node with sufficient maneuvering space (degree more than 2), the swap is
considered unnecessary.

adjacent nodes. If ai reaches a dead end (degree 1), the swap is infeasible. If ai reaches a node of degree more than 2,
the swap is possible. When a swap is confirmed, aj is marked as the swap agent and the preference list Pi is reversed
(line 15), allowing ai to concede temporarily.

nodes should enter a dead-end corridor (lines 7–13), extending swap detection to a broader set of local configurations.

To address this problem, we introduce a simple escape mechanism that detects repeated priority inheritance be-

specific agent. All entries in PT are initialized to zero. Whenever agent a j is recursively invoked by a higher-priority

reaches a predefined threshold k, the next time aj is pushed by ai, its node preference list P j is randomly shuffled to
encourage exploration of alternative routes and break the repetitive pushing pattern.
The choice of k plays an important role in performance. A small k may cause agents to frequently deviate from
their individually optimal paths in high-density scenarios, degrading solution quality. Conversely, a large k allows
repetitive pushing to persist for many timesteps, limiting the improvement. In the next section, a suitable value of k is
determined through numerical experiments.
4.7. Complexity analysis
As shown in Algorithm 1, within a single timestep of planning, sorting the agents based on updated priorities
(line 2) requires O(|A|lg|A|). During the planning process for each agent, iterating over the candidate nodes requires
O(∆(G) + 1), where O(∆(G)) is the maximum degree of the graph. Both detecting cycles and handling cycles requires
O(|C|), where |C| is the number of agents in the cycle with |C| ≤|A|, resulting in an overall complexity of O(|A|).
Assuming other operations are completed in constant time O(K), the proposed algorithm achieves a time complexity
of O(|A| · (lg|A| + |A|∆(G) + K)). It is evident that the proposed method requires only polynomial time complexity
for planning within a single timestep, offering a significant computational advantage over algorithm with exponential
time complexity (Zhang et al., 2023).
The time complexity for creating the cost table during initialization is O(4|A| · (4|V| · 3)) = O(|A| · |V|). In large-
scale instances involving extensive maps or a high number of agents, the preprocessing time will increase accordingly.
However, experiments on benchmark maps have demonstrated acceptable preprocessing speeds, which will be further
explained in the numerical experiments section.

5.1. Experimental setup
We evaluate the proposed algorithm through six sets of experiments, covering parameter calibration, mechanism
analysis, benchmark comparison, scalability testing, and realistic execution validation. The first set investigates the
impact of the parameter k in the k-Push Escape Trigger (PET) on solution quality. Based on the calibrated value of
k, the second set conducts an ablation study on PET and SPD, evaluating their effects on success rate and solution
quality. The third set compares the proposed PIBT*

computational efficiency, and solution quality.
The fourth set performs a large-scale stress test to assess the scalability of PIBT*

Benchmarks and Instances. The benchmark experiments are conducted on five standard maps from the Movin-

5.6. Gap between theoretical planning cost and realistic execution cost
This subsection examines whether turn actions should be considered during high-level planning for realistic robot
execution. As discussed in Section 1, solutions generated by classical MAPF algorithms ignore turning actions, but
they can still be converted into conflict-free executable plans through low-level mechanisms such as ADG (Hönig
et al., 2019). This motivates a direct comparison between such a hierarchical approach and the proposed turn-aware
high-level planning approach. Specifically, we compare the solutions generated by PIBT*
T and the classical MAPF
solver PIBT. Both solutions are fed into an ADG-based simulator that incorporates turning actions, acceleration,
and deceleration. The simulation results are used to quantify the gap between theoretical planning cost and actual
execution cost, and to compare the actual execution performance of the two methods.
Based on Hönig et al. (2019), we implement an ADG-based simulator that converts each discrete plan into a se-
quence of executable rotation, translation, and waiting actions. For each robot, Type-1 dependencies are generated to
preserve the intra-robot action order along its planned path, while Type-2 dependencies are created according to the
visiting order of shared grid cells among different robots. Since the converted ADG may contain cyclic dependency
structures when multiple robots are mutually dependent within the same discrete planning step, we handle such cases
through strict synchronization: mutually dependent actions are released simultaneously once all external predecessors
have been completed. After the ADG is constructed, the simulator assigns continuous execution durations to transla-
tions and 90◦rotations according to the robots’ maximum velocity, acceleration, and angular velocity. In this way, the
simulator captures the additional execution cost caused by turning, acceleration and deceleration, while preserving
the dependency structure required for collision-free execution.
In the ADG-based simulation, each grid edge represents a physical distance of 1 m. All robots are assumed to have
identical kinematic parameters. The maximum translational velocity is set to 1 m/s, the translational acceleration and
deceleration are both set to 1 m/s2, and the maximum angular velocity is set to 1.5708 rad/s. Consecutive forward

MMRSs are increasingly deployed in industrial environments. To evaluate the practical relevance of the proposed
approach, this subsection presents a case study on pallet transportation in a Flexible Manufacturing System (FMS),
adapted from Verma et al. (2024). As illustrated in Figure 12, the layout contains two types of functional areas: parking

6. Demonstration with real robots
To further validate the applicability of the proposed algorithm on real robots, we conduct a physical experiment
using three TurtleBot4 robots on a 6×6 grid map with obstacles. The robot team consists of two TurtleBot4 Lite robots
and one TurtleBot4 Standard robot, denoted as R1, R2, and R3, respectively. The start and goal locations of the three
robots are shown in Figure 15(a). In the experiment, the high-level planner first computes a discrete path plan for each
robot. The resulting plans are then processed by an ADG-based controller, which constructs the action dependency
relationships among robots and converts the discrete plans into adjusted continuous action sequences. These action
sequences are subsequently distributed to the corresponding robots and executed on the physical platform.
We compare two execution pipelines on the same task: PIBT combined with ADG-based execution, and the
proposed PIBT*
T combined with the same ADG-based execution framework. In the first pipeline, the paths generated
by PIBT are directly passed to the ADG-based controller. Although the controller preserves collision-free execution,
the resulting execution can still suffer from poor coordination quality. As shown in Figure 15(b), R2 and R3 enter a
deadlock approximately 5 seconds after the start of execution and remain stationary thereafter. This deadlock occurs
because R2 repeatedly requests the position occupied by R3, while R3 has no available space to yield. Consequently,
the two robots mutually block each other and fail to complete their tasks. In the same experiment, R1 eventually
reaches its goal after 41 seconds, as shown in Figure 15(c). However, its action sequence contains three turning
actions, two of which are unnecessary, thereby increasing the execution time.
In contrast, when PIBT*
T is used as the high-level planner, all three robots successfully reach their goal locations.
As shown in Figure 15(d), R1 reaches its goal in 32 seconds, which is 9 seconds faster than in the PIBT+ADG
setting. This improvement is mainly because PIBT*
T explicitly accounts for turn actions during planning and avoids
unnecessary orientation changes. Meanwhile, R2 successfully yields to R3, allowing both robots to pass through the
constrained area and reach their respective goals approximately 36 seconds after the start of execution. These results
show that the proposed method not only improves execution efficiency, but also avoids deadlock patterns that may
arise when turn actions and their induced delays are ignored at the planning level. The complete experimental video
is provided in the supplementary material.
7. Conclusion
This paper presents PIBT*
T, an efficient priority-inheritance-based path planning method for large-scale multi-
mobile-robot systems under turn constraints. By extending the original PIBT framework to an orientation-aware
setting, the proposed method preserves the computational efficiency of single-timestep priority-inheritance planning
while addressing the key difficulties introduced by turn actions. To this end, we develop several dedicated mechanisms,

including turn-aware action updates, cycle handling for circular request chains, a swap pattern detector (SPD) for
resolving local deadlocks, and a k-Push Escape Trigger (PET) for mitigating livelock and inefficient repeated pushing
behaviors.
Extensive computational experiments are conducted to evaluate the proposed method. First, the impact of the PET
threshold parameter k and the stability of the algorithm outputs are systematically analyzed. Second, the effects of the
proposed SPD and PET modules are investigated through an ablation study. Third, the proposed method is compared
with representative MAPFT solvers on standard benchmarks. The results show that PIBT*
T achieves high success rates
and strong scalability. Furthermore, a large-scale stress test shows that the proposed method can solve instances with
up to 1,000 agents within seconds while incurring only limited computational overhead.
To investigate the execution performance of the proposed method, we conduct an ADG-based execution simula-
tion, in which the solutions generated by different planners are converted into executable action sequences subject
to kinematic constraints. Compared with classical MAPF approaches that ignore turn actions, PIBT*
T produces plans
whose estimated costs are more consistent with realistic execution and achieves lower actual execution makespan. We
further validate the practical performance of the proposed method through a case study on a flexible manufacturing
system, where AGVs are used to perform logistics tasks. Finally, a real-robot demonstration with TurtleBot4 robots
further confirms the feasibility of deploying the proposed method on physical multi-robot platforms.
Future work will first focus on strengthening the theoretical foundations of the proposed method. Although PIBT*
T
26


achieves strong empirical performance and scalability, it remains a heuristic method and does not yet provide formal
completeness or optimality guarantees. Establishing stronger guarantees on reachability, termination, and solution
quality under turn constraints is therefore an important direction for future research. Another promising direction
is to incorporate learning-based mechanisms into priority-inheritance-based planning, for example to improve node
selection, escape decisions, and conflict-resolution strategies while preserving the scalability and interpretability of
the proposed framework. Finally, we plan to extend the proposed method to more realistic multi-robot coordination
systems with dynamic tasks, uncertain execution times, and online disturbances, such as integrated task assignment
and path planning, execution-aware replanning under real-time feedback, and lifelong coordination of robot fleets in
logistics and manufacturing environments.
