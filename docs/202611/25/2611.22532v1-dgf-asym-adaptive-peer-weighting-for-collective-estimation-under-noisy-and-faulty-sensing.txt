# DGF-Asym: Adaptive Peer Weighting for Collective Estimation under Noisy and Faulty Sensing 

Mohammad Nomaan Husain 1 and Tanja Katharina Kaiser 1

Abstract — Collective estimation under unreliable sensing requires robots to adaptively balance personal observations and social information from peers. We propose DGF-Asym, a decentralized collective estimation method extending the DeGroot-Friedkin model with asymmetric peer weighting based on retrospective predictive performance. The method supports consensus formation without prior sensor-noise statistics or explicit fault models while keeping inter-robot communication asynchronous. We evaluate DGF-Asym in simulated collective navigation under noisy observations and time-varying sensor faults. Compared with three baselines, it produces more stable estimates and more reliably sustains the decision criterion, at the cost of longer decision times. However, overall performance depends on fault ratio, group size, and influence-threshold selection. Our results demonstrate the potential of adaptive peer weighting for stable model-free collective estimation under unreliable sensing. 

I. INTRODUCTION Multi-robot systems can accomplish tasks that are difficult or impossible for a single robot by collectively making decisions, acting, and exchanging information about their environment. Their effectiveness, however, depends critically on the reliability of the information shared among team mem-bers. In open or adversarial settings, robots may receive false or misleading information from external intruders or from compromised or defecting members of the team. Previous work has shown that even small numbers of disruptive robots can substantially impair the performance of an entire multi-robot system [1]. This raises a fundamental challenge for robust collec-tive estimation: how should robots combine information received from others with evidence obtained through their own sensors? While communication can increase the amount of available information and thus compensate for limited individual observations, blindly trusting communicated infor-mation makes the system vulnerable to misinformation [1]. Conversely, relying on local observations discards potentially valuable knowledge held by other robots. Effective infor-mation fusion, therefore, requires mechanisms that balance these two sources of information while accounting for their uncertainty and reliability. In this work, we study infor-mation fusion in multi-robot systems in the presence of potentially unreliable information, with the goal of enabling individual robots to form accurate beliefs while remain-ing robust to faulty observations communicated by other agents. We propose a fully decentralized collective estimation

DeGroot-Friedkin’s self-appraisal mechanism enables robots to continuously up-date the weight they put on their personal information based on the perceived influence on the collective estimate. Our dy-namic peer-specific social-information-weighting mechanism further enables robots to assign different importance levels to information received from individual neighbors, rather than treating all neighbors with the same importance as in the original formulation. RELATED WORK 

A. Bayesian Fusion 

Bayesian fusion combines information from multiple un-certain sources through probabilistic models of states and ob-servations [3]. Kalman and extended Kalman filtering (EKF) instantiate this principle by weighting model predictions against measurements according to their estimated uncer-tainties [4]. Standard formulations typically assume that the relevant process and measurement noise statistics are known or specified. Adaptive methods reduce this dependence by innovation-based covariance estimation [5] or variational Bayesian adaptation [6]. Liu et al. [7] extend noise-adaptive estimation to distributed sensor networks by jointly updat-ing local states and measurement-noise precision matrices. Although these methods can infer previously unknown noise parameters, they still rely on an explicit probabilistic state and observation model and assumptions about the form of the underlying noise distributions. 

B. Collective Perception and Adaptive Information Weight-ing 

Collective perception enables groups of robots to infer environmental properties from distributed observations [8]. While collective decision making selects among discrete alternatives, collective estimation seeks to infer an underlying environmental state or quantity [9]. Bayesian approaches such as Bayes Bots [10] and distributed Bayesian belief sharing [9] combine uncertain local and social information, while change-detection methods enable adaptation to dy-namic environments [11]. A related line of work addresses uncertainty in source reli-ability. Minimalistic Collective Perception [12] incorporates sensor accuracy in collective estimation, while the Adaptive Sensor Degradation Framework [13] and BayesCPF [14] adapt sensor-accuracy estimates online using social informa-tion. In contrast, our approach adapts reliance on personal and social information without explicitly estimating sensor accuracy or assuming a sensor-degradation model. Reliability has also been represented through trust and adaptive fusion weights. Pierson and Schwager [15] infer inter-robot trust from relative sensing performance. However, they use it to allocate spatial responsibility rather than contributions to a shared estimate. Hallyburton and Pajic [16] maintain probabilistic trust states for sensor fusion using physical-model consistency and peer information, whereas our approach does not require an explicit trust or consistency model. Gupta et al.’s Autonomous Online Learning (AOL) framework [17] is the closest to our setting, directly adapting local and social fusion weights under sensor failures using online learning signals. Unlike AOL, our weights are derived from interaction dynamics: personal reliance is adapted from perceived social influence, and peer reliance from retrospec-tive predictive performance. 

C. Opinion Dynamics 

Opinion dynamics provide a complementary perspec-tive on information fusion by modeling how agents revise their beliefs through repeated social interaction. DeGroot’s model [18] formalizes weighted opinion averaging, while the DeGroot-Friedkin model [2] extends this framework with self-confidence adaptation through reflected appraisal of social influence. Subsequent extensions introduce finite-time, neighbor-based self-appraisal [19] and issue-dependent interaction topology [20]. These models provide a basis for adapting information weights from interaction dynamics rather than from explicit estimates of sensing uncertainties. Building on this perspective, our approach applies influence-based self-appraisal and retrospective peer weight-ing to collective estimation. Unlike probabilistic fusion and sensor-calibration approaches, the resulting weights represent interaction-derived reliance rather than calibrated measures of sensor accuracy or uncertainty. III. 2) Asymmetric Peer Weighting: We propose DGF-Asym, an extension of the DeGroot-Friedkin model with asymmet-ric peer weighting; that is, we change the way in which social information si is constructed. In the standard DGF model, the relative interpersonal weights of peers remain fixed. Here, we replace this fixed weighting with a peer-specific weighted averaging, allowing robot i to assign different levels of influ-ence to different neighbors when forming its social estimate. In this way, peers with potentially more accurate estimates gain influence, while those with less reliable estimates, for example, due to noisy or faulty sensors, have less influence on the overall estimate. C. Baselines 

We compare our approach with AOL-C and AOL-1p, two variants of the Autonomous Online Learning framework proposed by Gupta et al. [17]. These decentralized methods learn fusion weights online without ground-truth feedback. Developed for cooperative target monitoring under noise, sensor failures, and intermittent visibility, they provide rele-vant baselines for adaptive fusion under unreliable sensing. Both methods combine local and social estimation through exchange of intermediate estimates and their weights. AOL-C compares the current observation and previous estimate with the locally available intermediate estimate having the highest weight. AOL-1P uses a perturbation-greedy reward to explore alternative weightings and reinforce changes that improve target-detection confidence. We adapt both methods to our sensing model: loss of visibility produces a maximum-ignorance observation rather than retention of the previous estimate. We also adjust AOL parameters to accommodate sensing without a finite range limit, where goal visibility depends on occlusion and the robot’s field of view. 

D. Results 

We first compare the effect of in-threshold durations on the estimation process, testing τ = 1 s and τ = 10 s with 

N = 5 robots, ρ = 0 .2, and Ithresh = 1 . For τ = 1 s, AOL-C and AOL-1P both achieve 100 % success, compared with 85 % for DGF-Asym, and 42 .5 % for DGF, see Tab. I. Increasing the required duration to 10 s reduces both AOL methods’ success rates to 0 % , while DGF-Asym and DGF retain 82 .5 % and 37 .5 % , respectively. These results indicate that, although the AOL methods more frequently satisfy the short-duration criterion, DGF-Asym more reliably maintains collective opinions within the threshold over time. The stability metrics support this distinction. DGF-Asym reduces collective opinion error jitter Jc by 91 .8 % , 99 .06 % ,and 99 .07 % relative to DGF, AOL-C, and AOL-1P, respec-tively. The corresponding reductions in median collective opinion error Ec are 73 .5% , 76 .4% , and 77 .9% . The greater temporal variance of the AOL methods may partly reflect their synchronous communication, which allows noisy ob-servations to propagate across communicating robots within a single update step. DGF-Asym reduces the weight of personal information as consensus emerges and assigns less influence to unreliable peers, limiting sensitivity to noisy inputs and supporting a more stable consensus. V. CONCLUSIONS We presented DGF-Asym, a decentralized method for collective estimation under noisy and faulty sensing. The method balances personal and social information by using influence-based self-confidence and weights peers using a retrospective measure of predictive performance. It sup-ports asynchronous communication without requiring pre-characterized sensor-noise statistics or explicit degradation models. Simulation results demonstrate improved stability relative to the baselines across the tested conditions, while revealing sensitivity to fault ratio, group size, and influence threshold. Retrospective peer weighting assumes that evolving collec-tive opinions generally approach the ground truth, allowing later local estimates to serve as references for evaluating earlier peer information. The influence threshold thus re-quires configuration-dependent tuning to the expected com-munication activity within an update interval. Future work will investigate online threshold adaptation to reduce manual tuning across configurations.
