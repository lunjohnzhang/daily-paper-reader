# Spatially Conditioned Trajectory Diffusion 

## Yefim Nudelman, Dor Atzmon *, and Tom Tirer *

Faculty of Engineering, Bar-Ilan University Ramat Gan, Israel 

Abstract 

Multi-Robot Motion Planning (MRMP) requires trajectories that avoid environmental obstacles and collisions between robots in a shared workspace. Diffusion-based planners often address obstacle avoidance through sampling-time guidance, while some conditioned approaches aggregate map ge-ometry into a global representation. We present Spatially Conditioned Trajectory Diffusion (SCTD), a map-conditioned trajectory generator for multi-robot planning. SCTD retains multiple position-aware occupancy tokens accessed through cross-attention throughout a temporal U-Net, with hard endpoint inpainting enforcing start and goal states. One model trained on single-robot demonstrations across procedural environments generates obstacle-aware trajectories without sampling-time obstacle-cost guidance. High-level Conflict-Based Search (CBS) coordinates these trajectories through constrained replanning, retaining inter-agent guidance during sampling and reusing the model across workspace layouts and team sizes. Experiments assess single-robot success and trajectory quality, generalization to unfamiliar layouts with added obstacles, and coordinated multi-robot planning. On 15,000 held-out tasks, SCTD achieves higher success than the evaluated guided diffusion baseline, with the largest gains on hard-detour tasks, while trajectory-quality comparisons show smoother trajectories. Condi-tioning ablations show higher success with spatial tokens than with a global map embedding, and map-replacement tests demonstrate dependence on the supplied geometry. The resulting multi-robot system achieves higher team success than the compared diffusion baselines, with experiments covering teams of up to 15 robots. 

Keywords: diffusion models | motion planning | trajectory generation | map conditioning | multi-robot systems 

# 1 Introduction 

Multi-Robot Motion Planning (MRMP) is a fundamental challenge in robotics, requiring teams of robots to move through a shared continuous workspace while avoiding environmental obstacles and one an-other. Applications such as automated warehouses require individual routes to account for workspace geometry while remaining compatible with the motions of other robots. This coupling makes planning computationally challenging as the team size and complexity of the workspace increase. Obstacle lay-outs also vary across tasks, requiring planners to adapt their routes to changing workspace geometry. One approach to this complexity is to formulate a discrete abstraction of MRMP as Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF), representing configurations and transitions on a graph, typically in discrete timesteps [19]. MAPF methods such as Conflict-Based Search (CBS) separate coordination from individual path planning: a high-level search resolves conflicts by imposing constraints, while low-level planners re-vise affected agents’ paths [15]. Within this decomposition, coordination repeatedly requests individual paths under updated constraints. Extending this structure to continuous-space planning therefore mo-tivates studying low-level generators that account for the current workspace geometry while supplying trajectories for individual sampling and replanning [14]. Diffusion models [16, 17, 5, 18] offer a learned approach to low-level trajectory generation by mod-eling distributions over complete trajectories and generating samples through iterative denoising [8]. 

Motion Planning Diffusion [3] (MPD) develops this approach for robot motion planning, learning trajec-tory priors from demonstrations and adapting generated motions through sampling-time cost-gradient guidance. 

Multi-robot Multi-model planning Diffusion (MMD) [14] combines single-robot diffusion planners with constraint-based coordination, resolving conflicts through guided individual replanning. Simultaneous MRMP Diffusion (SMD) [9] instead plans trajectories for all robots simultaneously, using constrained op-timization during denoising to address obstacle and inter-robot collisions. The current map therefore influences sampling, but does not explicitly condition the learned denoiser. Building on this approach, we examine how trajectory features access the map during denoising. An aggregated map embedding can encode geometry, while spatially indexed features presented as separate attention tokens allow different workspace regions to be queried individually. Spatial map tokens also appear in Roken’s joint multi-robot diffusion transformer [1]; our focus is their use in a single-robot backbone learned across workspace layouts and evaluated within inherited multi-robot coordination. A convolutional occupancy encoder produces position-aware spatial features that trajectory features can query separately through cross-attention throughout a temporal U-Net. Start and goal states remain fixed through hard endpoint in-painting during denoising. For multi-robot planning, we integrate SCTD as the low-level trajectory generator in XECBS [14], retaining inter-agent guidance to address coordination constraints during sam-pling and replanning. Spatially Conditioned Trajectory Diffusion. Generalization and map-dependent generation in unseen workspaces. We show that an SCTD model generates feasible trajectories in held-out maps without static-obstacle-cost guidance, in-cluding obstacle-dependent and hard-detour tasks. Compared with MPD, SCTD achieves higher single-agent success. 3. Reusable low-level planning for multi-robot coordination. We integrate SCTD as the low-level trajectory generator in XECBS [14], retaining inter-agent guidance and reusing one map-conditioned model across robots and workspace layouts. On held-out maps, the resulting system achieves higher team success than MMD at every evaluated team size from 3 to 15 robots, and than SMD [9] at the shared sizes of 3, 6, and 9 robots, under a 180-second trial limit. 

# 2 Related Work 

Diffusion models for motion planning. Diffuser [8] formulates planning as trajectory denoising and combines guidance for planning objectives with inpainting for fixed trajectory states. Motion Planning Diffusion (MPD) [3] uses learned trajectory priors and adapts samples through task-specific cost gra-dients, while EDMP [11] combines multiple inference-time cost objectives. Sampling-time objectives are distinct from denoiser inputs and task conditions such as endpoint inpainting. Environment con-ditioning and sampling-time cost guidance are not mutually exclusive. SceneDiffuser [7] combines scene-conditioned generation with differentiable physical objectives and goal-directed planning during sampling. PRESTO [13] conditions its trajectory model on a key-configuration environment representa-tion and examines additional collision and smoothness cost guidance in a sampling ablation. Learned conditioning supplies an environment-dependent trajectory prior, while cost guidance adjusts samples according to further objectives/constraints. Within this trajectory-prior formulation, SCTD focuses on learning workspace-dependent routing through map conditioning. 

Environment-Conditioned Trajectory Diffusion. Environment-conditioned trajectory diffusion dif-fers in the geometric representations exposed to the denoiser. Carvalho et al. [2] pretrain an occupancy-image encoder through signed-distance regression, aggregate its features into a global map embedding, and combine this embedding with task features to condition a temporal U-Net through cross-attention. 2CAMPD [12] instead attends to a variable set of structured contextual elements, including obstacle pa-rameters, and uses classifier-free guidance to strengthen conditioning rather than applying obstacle-cost gradients. Observation-conditioned approaches include RobotDiffuse [20], which encodes obstacle point clouds for transformer-based trajectory generation, and DiffusionSeeder [6], which uses depth observations to generate trajectory seeds for subsequent motion optimization. Spatial map tokens are also used in Roken [1], where robot tokens cross-attend to position-aware map patches within a joint multi-robot diffusion transformer. These approaches distinguish aggregated map context, structured environmental elements, and separately addressable spatial features. SCTD uses occupancy-derived spatial features to learn a single-robot trajectory backbone across procedural environments, rather than a joint team-trajectory model. We assess this backbone both in single-agent generation and within inher-ited multi-robot coordination; the contribution is the resulting planning system and its evaluated use, not spatial tokens or cross-attention in isolation. 

Diffusion-based Multi-Robot Motion Planning. Diffusion-based multi-robot planners differ in how trajectory generation is coupled to coordination. MMD [14] pairs reusable single-robot diffusion plan-ners with MAPF-inspired coordination, including CBS [15], using only single-robot training data rather than learning a joint trajectory distribution over the team. Its planning framework translates spatiotem-poral constraints into sampling-time guidance and replans affected robots under those constraints. Other formulations include joint trajectory generation with constrained projection in Simultaneous MRMP Diffusion [9] and robot-token-based joint generation for safety and connectivity in Roken [1]. Discrete-Guided Diffusion [10] instead uses MAPF-derived spatiotemporal structure to guide continuous trajec-tory generation, followed by constraint repair. Within MMD’s inherited constraint-based coordination framework, we evaluate SCTD’s shared spatially map-conditioned backbone on held-out maps, retain-ing inter-agent guidance while assessing its effectiveness as the low-level generator across workspace layouts. 

# 3 Preliminaries and Problem Formulation 

## 3.1 Multi-Robot Motion Planning 

Multi-Robot Motion Planning (MRMP) concerns the coordinated motion of multiple robots in a shared continuous workspace. An environment E consists of a bounded planar workspace Ω ⊂ R2 and an obstacle region OE ⊂ Ω.The environment is represented by a binary occupancy grid ME , with occupied cells marked by one. We consider N disk robots of radius r > 0. At waypoint h, robot i has state si,h = ( qi,h , v i,h ) ∈ R4,where qi,h ∈ R2 denotes its planar position and vi,h ∈ R2 contains its velocity channels. ## 3.2 MAPF and Conflict-Based Coordination 

Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) formulates coordinated routing on a graph, typically using discrete timesteps [19]. Conflict-Based Search (CBS) separates this problem into high-level coordination and low-level individual planning [15]. A high-level search node contains individual paths and their constraints. When two paths conflict, the search branches by imposing an additional constraint on either agent and replanning the affected path. 3This decomposition provides a framework for coordinating continuous trajectory generators: indi-vidual planning produces candidate motions, while the coordination layer identifies incompatibilities and requests replanning under additional conditions [14]. The decomposition can be retained while replacing graph-based individual planning with continuous-space trajectory generation. The proposed method specifies how SCTD serves as the individual trajectory generator within the selected coordination procedure. 

## 3.3 Diffusion Models 

A denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) [5] learns to generate data by reversing a gradual noising process. For trajectory generation, let u0 denote a clean trajectory representation [8]. At a sam-pled diffusion step k, the forward process adds Gaussian noise: 

uk = √¯αk u0 + √1 − ¯αk ϵ, ϵ ∼ N (0 , I ), (3) where ¯αk is determined by the noise schedule. The denoiser learns to predict the added noise from the corrupted trajectory and the diffusion step. At inference, generation starts from Gaussian noise and applies successive reverse updates, uK → · · · → u0, where K is the number of diffusion steps. Each update revises the complete trajectory representation rather than generating waypoints sequentially. 

## 3.4 Conditional Trajectory Diffusion 

In conditional diffusion, the denoiser also receives context c:

bϵ k = ϵθ (uk, k, c ). (4) The context guides the denoising process toward samples compatible with the specified task or envi-ronment. In SCTD, the environmental context is the encoded occupancy map, c = ZE = Eϕ(ME ). Start and goal states are handled separately: endpoint inpainting fixes them before denoising and restores them after reverse updates. Thus, map conditioning informs generation of the trajectory interior, whereas inpainting enforces its boundary states. Section 4 describes the spatial conditioning interface and its integration with multi-robot coordination. 

# 4 Spatially Conditioned Trajectory Diffusion 

In this section, we present SCTD from architecture to multi-robot integration. We first present the spatial-conditioning architecture, then the training and single-robot inference procedures, and finally how the shared generator provides per-robot trajectories within MRMP coordination. 

## 4.1 SCTD Architecture

It encodes the map as spatial tokens and predicts noise for a trajectory conditioned on those tokens; endpoint inpainting fixes the boundary states, and reverse dif-fusion produces the complete trajectory.

## 4.4 SCTD-MRMP Integration 

For multi-robot planning, we reuse the same SCTD model to generate trajectories for every robot, both during initial planning and when conflicts require replanning. Each planning request supplies the workspace occupancy map and the requesting robot’s prescribed endpoints. We coordinate these tra-jectories using XECBS [14], a variant of CBS [15] which detects inter-agent conflicts at synchronized waypoints, branches on additional constraints, and replans the affected robot. Search continues until the selected trajectories have no detected inter-agent conflicts, or the planner fails or reaches its runtime limit. Coordination constraints influence trajectory generation through sampling-time guidance. # 5 Experimental Results 

Our experiments address three questions: how reliably SCTD generates feasible trajectories on unseen maps, whether its outputs genuinely depend on the supplied map, and how effectively the same gen-erator supports multi-robot coordination. Bold values indicate the best result within each comparison, including ties at the reported precision. 

## 5.1 Experimental Setup 

Environment and data. Experiments use disk robots of radius r = 0 .05 in Ω = [ −1, 1] 2, with disk and axis-aligned rectangular obstacles. SCTD observes a 64 × 64 binary occupancy grid, while task construction and feasibility evaluation use analytic geometry. The principal single-agent benchmark contains 15,000 tasks on 500 maps held out from training. 

Metrics. A single-agent trajectory is feasible if the robot avoids obstacles and remains within the workspace at every waypoint and interpolated check point. Success@ J (or simply @ J) is the percentage of tasks with at least one feasible trajectory among J samples, computed separately for each map and averaged equally across maps. Team success is the percentage of multi-agent tasks solved within the trial time limit, requiring feasible trajectories for all robots and no detected inter-agent conflicts. 

## 5.2 Single-Agent Performance on Unseen Maps 

Single-agent baselines. We compare SCTD with MPD using MMD’s public implementation [14]. MPD applies SDF-based obstacle guidance and post-sampling smoothing, whereas SCTD uses occupancy conditioning without obstacle-cost guidance or a separate smoother. Both methods use the same tasks and feasibility criterion. Table 1 reports single-agent success overall and on obstacle-dependent tasks, split into mild and hard-detour tasks (defined in Appendix D). SCTD achieves higher success than MPD overall, both with one sample (90.5% versus 82.3%) and with 64 samples (99.2% versus 89.4%). The largest differences occur on obstacle-dependent tasks, particularly hard detours, while both diffusion planners perform near ceiling on straight-clear tasks (not shown). 

Single-agent trajectory quality. We measure path length and geometric smoothness on feasible trajec-tories. Our smoothness measure is the maximum local turn angle, which captures the sharpest change in direction over a path distance of one robot diameter ( w = 0 .1). Smaller angles indicate gentler local turns. Principal quality comparisons use tasks for which every compared method produces at least one feasible trajectory. We average over feasible samples within each task, then over tasks within each map, and finally equally across maps. MPD’s large turn angles fre-quently occur near the trajectory end, as examined further in Appendix E.2. 

## 5.3 Map-Pathway Ablation 

We compare SCTD’s spatial map tokens with the global map embedding of Carvalho et al. [2]: an SDF-pretrained CNN encodes the map as a 128-dimensional vector, which is combined with the normalized start and goal into one cross-attention token. Both designs use the same diffusion-training data and budget, as described in Appendix A. For each design, we evaluate a frozen encoder, as in Carvalho et al., and a jointly fine-tuned encoder, as in SCTD. All four configurations use the same 500 evaluation maps and 15,000 tasks; this population is held out from training and separate from the sealed test set. For this ablation, single-sample success (Suc-cess@1) is estimated from the fraction of feasible trajectories among the 64 samples per task; Success@64 records whether at least one is feasible. Task-level values are averaged within each map and then equally across maps. Table 3 shows that SCTD outperforms the global embedding across all reported task groups with both frozen and fine-tuned encoders. The advantage is especially pronounced on tasks requiring ob-stacle avoidance: on hard-detour tasks, the fine-tuned SCTD achieves 73.7% Success@1 and 97.5% Suc-cess@64, compared with 2.6% and 38.6% for the fine-tuned global model. Fine-tuning improves SCTD’s success across all groups, whereas it reduces success for the global model. SCTD’s advantage also per-sists when both encoders are frozen, supporting the spatial conditioning design beyond the benefit of encoder fine-tuning alone.

# 6 Conclusion 

We presented SCTD, a spatially map-conditioned trajectory generator. Multiple position-aware occu-pancy tokens allow trajectory features to access different workspace regions through cross-attention throughout denoising. On held-out maps, SCTD achieves higher single-agent success than the MPD baseline without sampling time obstacle cost guidance, with the largest gains on hard-detour tasks. The conditioning-design ablation further shows higher success with spatial tokens than with a global map embedding. Integrating SCTD into XECBS supports multi-robot motion planning. This combination of spatial map conditioning and high-level conflict-based coordination enables one model trained on single-robot demonstrations to serve different robots, workspace layouts, and team sizes, while inter-agent guidance addresses coordination constraints during sampling and replanning. This integration of SCTD into XECBS achieves higher team success than MMD at all evaluated team sizes and than SMD at the shared sizes of 3, 6, and 9 robots.

# B Dataset Generation 

Maps. Each map is a 2 × 2 workspace containing four to six obstacles: disks with radius in [0 .20 , 0.32] 

and axis-aligned rectangles with half-extents in [0 .16 , 0.30] . A fraction of obstacles is placed adjacent to a neighbour to form compound shapes that force detours; the others keep a gap of at least 0.11 , wider than the robot diameter. Maps are generated deterministically from their index and rasterized to a 64 × 64 

occupancy grid. 

Tasks. Start and goal positions are sampled with obstacle clearance at least 0.16 , at least 0.15 apart, and in the same connected free region of a 128 × 128 grid. 

Expert trajectories. For each of 30 candidate tasks per training map, a single RRTConnect–GPMP2 run produces a 64-waypoint trajectory (RRTConnect initialization followed by 500 GPMP2 optimization it-erations). We discard trajectories that collide, and for straight-clear tasks those whose arc length exceeds 

1.10 times the start–goal distance. From the remaining trajectories, each map keeps at most ten, aiming for one straight-clear and nine obstacle-dependent examples while preserving the available ratio of mild to hard-detour tasks. The resulting diffusion-training set contains 44,984 demonstrations on 4,500 maps. 

Encoder-pretraining maps. SDF pretraining uses 45,450 maps from the same generator: 450 of the diffusion-training maps and 45,000 additional maps. Pretraining needs no demonstrations. 

Evaluation maps. Training, held-out evaluation, and test maps are disjoint by index. The single-agent benchmark consists of 500 test maps with 30 tasks each, taken in sampling order among candidates that pass a 512 × 512 four-connected reachability check for the robot. Algorithm 3 summarizes how SCTD is used within XECBS [14]. Each node stores conflict constraints, a batch of candidate trajectories for each robot, and one selected trajectory per robot. Plan( i, n, Bprev ) sam-ples a new batch for robot i from SCTD, conditioned on the occupancy map and the robot’s endpoints and guided by its conflict constraints and by soft constraints derived from the other robots’ selected trajectories in n. With an empty previous batch, sampling starts from Gaussian noise; otherwise, the previous batch is re-noised for 3 steps and denoised for 3 steps. A call fails if no candidate is free of ob-stacle collisions. At the root, each robot selects its lowest-cost collision-free candidate; after replanning, the collision-free candidate with the fewest inter-robot conflicts. 

Parameters. Each call samples 64 candidates; initial planning uses 25 denoising steps. As in MMD, guidance is applied during the final half of the reverse process with 20 gradient steps per denoising step. A* plans on a 4-connected grid with spacing 0.1, using exact obstacle geometry; each endpoint is connected by straight segments to the free corners of its grid cell. ## E.1 Map-dependence control 

To test whether SCTD uses its map input, we replace the supplied occupancy map with a different map while keeping the start and goal fixed, and score the result against the original obstacles. We use obstacle-dependent tasks solved by the expert planner on the 500 evaluation maps held out from training. For each task, we select up to 15 replacement maps with clear, connected endpoints using a fixed seed. We then keep only those that block the straight segment between the start and goal. We exclude 39 tasks with no remaining replacement. For each task, we generate 64 samples per replacement map, sharing sampling noise across its replacements. Over 7,336 tasks and 84,373 replacement comparisons, the collision-free rate is 87.3% with the cor-rect map and 7.6% with a replacement map. Within each original map, we average success with the correct map over tasks and success with replacement maps over replacement comparisons. We then average both rates equally across original maps. The difference is 79.7 percentage points, with a 95% bootstrap interval of [78 .4, 80 .9] , computed from 10,000 resamples over both original and replacement maps. Figures 6 and 7 show selected qualitative examples from a separate pool of replacement maps.
