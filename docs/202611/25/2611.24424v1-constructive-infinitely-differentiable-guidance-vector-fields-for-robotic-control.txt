# Constructive Infinitely Differentiable Guidance Vector Fields for Robotic Control 

Vinicius Mariano Gonc ¸alves, Peilin Wu, Adriano Rezende, and Weijia Yao 

Abstract — Guidance vector fields (GVFs) are a popular framework for tasks such as robotic path-following and multi-robot motion coordination, but existing approaches may be non-differentiable or have large derivatives, limiting their use in controllers that require derivatives of the guidance signal, which is common when the GVF is used as an input in a cascaded controller. This paper proposes an infinitely differentiable constructive GVF. The resulting vector field depends on asmoothing parameter and is a generalization of [1], recovering the original GVF in the limit as this parameter approaches zero. It also admits a simple and efficient implementation that preserves smoothness under discretization and can reduce computation time by using substantially fewer curve samples while maintaining small path-following errors. We establish the main theoretical properties and demonstrate its practical advantages through numerical and real-world experiments. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Guidance vector fields (GVFs) have been widely used for robot control, particularly for path following, owing to their conceptual simplicity and strong theoretical foundations [1]– [4]. More recent developments include nonsingular construc-tions, multi-robot coordination, inverse-kinematics formula-tions, data-based representations, extensions to Lie groups, and safety-aware formulations [5]–[10]. Given a static target curve in the configuration space, a GVF usually assigns to each configuration in its domain a desired velocity vector. Following this velocity field causes the system to approach and traverse the target curve. Among these approaches, [1] proposed a practical constructive GVF for generic curves with formal convergence guarantees. Its construction is based on the Euclidean distance and the closest-point map between the current configuration and the target curve. In many robotic systems, however, the velocity generated by the GVF is not the physical control input. Instead, this desired velocity must be realized by a lower-level controller acting through accelerations, forces, or torques. A common solution is to employ a cascade architecture, for example through a backstepping design [11], in which the velocity generated by the GVF is supplied as a reference to adynamics-level control layer. In a quadrotor, for instance, the propeller thrusts and the resulting torques can be con-trolled so that the vehicle tracks the desired linear velocity prescribed by the GVF. Implementing such a cascade architecture often requires time derivatives of the GVF output [11]. In some designs,

These derivatives appear in feedforward acceleration, force, or torque terms that are important both in formal stability analyses and in improving practical tracking performance [11]–[13]. The required derivatives, however, may fail to exist. The construction in [1], for example, relies on the Euclidean distance between the configuration and the curve and its derivative, which is related to the closest point between the current point and the curve. Although the Euclidean distance is continuous everywhere, it is generally non-differentiable at points having multiple closest points on the curve, a set called the medial axis . At the center of a circle, for instance, every point on the circle is a closest point. At such configurations, the GVF is discontinuous. See Figure 1 for an illustration of this phenomenon. These singular configurations form a set of measure zero and may not be reachable by trajectories initialized outside it. Nevertheless, they can still create practical difficulties. Near the medial axis, the vector field can change very rapidly. Consequently, a small perturbation in the robot configuration may produce a large change in the desired velocity and, therefore, in the feedforward control input generated from its derivatives. A second source of non-smoothness, perhaps even more important in practice, arises from the numerical implementa-tion of the closest-point computation. Except for very simple arXiv:2611.24424v1 [cs.RO] 18 Nov 2026 curves, the Euclidean distance and closest point cannot be obtained analytically. One possible implementation is to discretize the curve into a finite collection of samples and select the sample closest to the current configuration. This procedure introduces a hard nearest-neighbor decision. As the configuration crosses a boundary between the Voronoi regions of two samples, the selected sample may change abruptly, producing a non-smooth approximation of the GVF even in regions where the original GVF was smooth. Nu-merical differentiation of this approximate field can then generate very large values. Unlike the intrinsic medial axis singularity, this phenomenon may occur even arbitrarily close to the target curve, which is particularly concerning because a path-following robot is expected to operate close to the curve during most of its motion. In this paper, we propose a construction which is ageneralization of [1] when we consider static curves. The resulting GVF is parameterized by a positive scalar r, which controls the trade-off between tracking precisely the original target curve and having smoother control inputs. For every 

r > 0, the proposed vector field is infinitely differentiable at every point of the configuration space. This property is preserved when the defining integrals are replaced by a fixed numerical quadrature over sampled points of the curve, which is straightforward to implement. As r → +0 , the proposed vector field converges to the original vector field of [1] at configurations for which the closest-point evaluation operation is well defined. The construction is based on what we call the r-Differentiable Evaluation Operator (r-DEO). The operator replaces evaluation of a function at a closest point on the curve with a normalized Gaussian-weighted average over the entire curve. It also admits a probabilistic interpre-tation. The normalized exponential weights used by the DEO are closely related to those arising from log-sum-exp and soft-min approximations of hard maximum and minimum operators [14]. Log-sum-exp regularization has been used in several control applications to replace non-smooth objects with differentiable approximations. In model predictive control, smooth maximum approximations have been employed to obtain differentiable soft penalties [15]. Smooth approximations of geometric distances have also been used together with control barrier functions to produce smoother distance-dependent safety constraints and control signals [16]. Similarly, soft-minimum and soft-maximum operators have been used to regularize minimum-based bar-rier functions, allowing them to be represented by a single continuously differentiable control barrier function [17]. In the more specific context of GVFs, [9] employs a smooth approximation of the robot-to-obstacle distance to augment the path-following vector field of [1] with obstacle-avoidance capabilities. This regularization is necessary be-cause the Euclidean distance to an obstacle may be non-differentiable, whereas its derivative is required by the safety controller. Nevertheless, the path-following component re-mains based on the original closest-point construction for the target curve and therefore retains the regularity and numerical implementation issues discussed above. Further-more, smoothing only a scalar distance function would not, by itself, regularize the selection of the closest point and of its corresponding tangent vector. In contrast, the DEO regularizes the complete closest-point-evaluation operation and can therefore be applied simultaneously to both the normal and tangential components of the GVF in [1]. Other approaches can also generate sufficiently smooth GVFs, although under different formulations. In [7], for ex-ample, a closed path represented by discrete data is approx-imated through a finite Fourier series. The path parameter is then introduced as an additional virtual state θ, and a GVF is constructed in the resulting augmented space. The vector field can be smooth in this augmented state space, but the physical velocity command depends on both the physical configuration and the additional virtual variable. Hence, it is not an autonomous vector field defined solely on the original physical configuration space. The present method, by contrast, constructs an everywhere infinitely differentiable static vector field directly in the original configuration space, without introducing a path-parameter state. A related probabilistic approach is proposed in [18], where a score-based diffusion model learns an attracting vector field from discrete path samples, while a second neural network learns a tangential field that induces motion along the path. This formulation accommodates unordered and branching path representations. Our normal component also admits a score interpretation through Gaussian smoothing. Here, however, we consider a prescribed regular curve and its tan-gent vectors, and compute both components directly through normalized Gaussian-weighted integrals, without training. We establish the differentiability and limiting properties of the proposed operator and analyze the dynamics generated by the resulting GVF. In particular, for sufficiently small r, we show that almost all trajectories converge to a tube around the target curve that shrinks with r, and that as r → +0 ,we recover the prescribed target curve. We also present a numerical implementation that preserves the smoothness of the construction and experimentally validate the method on a real robotic platform. In summary, the contributions of this paper are:  

> •

Practical : our approach offers two main benefits. First, the GVF is infinitely differentiable, so cascaded con-trollers that require its derivatives are not subject to disturbances caused by points of nondifferentiability. Second, implementing the method in [1] typically re-quires sampling points on the target curve. To have a good performance (i.e., guaranteeing convergence and tracking and not having control inputs that are too noisy), a large number of sample points may be neces-sary, especially when computing derivatives, which can increase the computational cost. Our approach allows the GVF to be implemented using fewer sampled points at the cost of modifying the target curve, with this trade-off controlled by the parameter r. Although the computational cost per sample of our approach is higher than in [1], we show through extensive simulations that this trade-off can be very favorable: the computational time can drop sharply while keeping a very reasonable tracking error. Furthermore, as we will show, we obtain very competitive results even when, instead of imple-menting [1] using discrete points, we use polynomial segments obtained through regression.  

> •

Theoretical : for static curves, our GVF generalizes the formulation in [1], which is recovered in the limit 

r → 0+. The stability analysis in [1] must account for discontinuities in the vector field, which requires a cumbersome mathematical analysis. We establish sta-bility for every r > 0 without this complication, since the resulting vector field is infinitely differentiable. In essence, our formulation smooths out the discontinuities in [1] through an adjustable smoothing parameter, en-abling a more straightforward stability analysis. Finally, interestingly, we found that our proof follows more directly if instead of the classical Lyapunov arguments, the dual Lyapunov argument of [19] is applied.

B. Choosing the Parameters of the GVF 

The main design choice considered in this paper is the smoothing parameter r, including whether to use r = 0 ,corresponding to the original approach in [1], or r > 0,corresponding to our proposed approach. Additional choices arise in the numerical implementation. For r > 0, we use the sampled-point approximation described in Subsection II-C and must select the number of samples N . For r = 0 , al-ternatives to searching through sampled points are available. Here, we also consider piecewise polynomial approximation :we use regression to approximate C with N polynomial segments of degree m, matching derivatives through order 

m − 1 at the joins, and search for the closest point on each segment. For a unified notation, we use m = 0 to denote the sampled-point representation and m > 0 to denote polynomial segments. For m ≥ 1, finding the closest point on a polynomial segment requires finding the real roots of a polynomial of degree at most 2m − 1. Numerical root-finding algorithms can be used. For m = 1 and m = 2 , the stationary-point equations are linear and cubic, respectively, and can be solved analytically, using Cardano’s formula in the cubic case. These choices depend on several factors, including the target curve, the GVF parameters, and the low-level dynam-ics. We must balance steady-state tracking error, measured relative to the original curve, against computational time. Dimensional analysis of the ideal GVF provides another useful starting point. For geometrically similar curves, scal-ing all spatial coordinates by a factor a and scaling both r

and H/G by the same factor preserves the relative geometry of the ideal first-order trajectories, with correspondingly scaled initial conditions. For example, if a particular choice produces an error equal to 5% of the curve length, doubling the curve size suggests doubling r and H/G to retain the same relative behavior. Thus: 

Practical Observation 2 : The value of r should be com-mensurate with the “size” of the curve. Larger curves require larger values of r.

With discretization, computational time must also be balanced against performance. Small values of N , or low polynomial degrees when r = 0 , reduce evaluation time but may produce poor tracking or failures, such as becoming stuck. Larger values can improve the approximation, at the cost of additional computation. For the smoothed field, the interaction between r and the sample spacing is particularly important: 

Practical Observation 3 : The smoothing parameter r and the number of samples N should be chosen together. For approximately uniform arc-length sampling and being ℓC

the length of the curve, the sample spacing is h ≈ ℓC /N .Near the curve, a useful heuristic is to make this spacing small compared with r, so that several nearby samples contribute appreciably to the Gaussian-weighted average. If the sampling is too sparse relative to r, the weights can become concentrated on individual samples and change rapidly as the robot moves, even though the implemented field remains infinitely differentiable. Smaller values of r therefore generally require denser sampling. The required sampling density depends on the curve geometry and the derivatives required by the controller.

For time budgets tb > 200 μs , the best error for r > 0 becomes stable at this value, whereas the polynomial approach can potentially obtain smaller error values such as 0.0001 m . However, these results are unlikely to be useful in practice, since such an error is very unlikely to be obtained in a practical situation in the quadrotor platform considered here.

D. Experiment with Drone 

We implemented the vector field on a custom-built quadro-tor in an indoor motion-capture arena measuring approx-imately 13.8 m × 30.0 m. The overhead motion-capture system consisted of 54 Swift120A/AW cameras arranged in a regular rectangular layout. Each camera provides 12 MP resolution and operates at up to 320 fps, with a specified positioning accuracy of ±0.03 mm. The FZMotion system was operated using FZMotion’s Moca software, which was configured to publish pose data at 120 Hz. The pose data were forwarded through VRPN to the onboard ROS 2/PX4 control stack, with Micro XRCE-DDS providing the ROS 2-PX4 transport. The drone computes everything in a Rasp-berry Pi 4B with 2 GB RAM. The system model and controller parameters are as described in Subsection III-A and Table I, respectively. Our target curve is shown in Figure 5. It is important to mention that this curve did not appear as one of the five curves used in the analysis in Subsection III-B . Furthermore, it is significantly more complex topology-wise and also larger, being contained in a radius of 3m instead of 1m. These are interesting, because we can then try to extrapolate from the analysis in Subsection III-B. The experiments in Subsection III-B were performed on a 11th Gen Intel Core i7-11800H CPU at 2.30 GHz using a single core, whereas the drone has a Raspberry Pi 4B with 2 GB RAM. We estimate that the latter is roughly 8 times slower than the former. This implies that, if we have to adapt the x axis of Figure 3 to the drone, also considering that each evaluation of the full controller requires 9 evaluations of the GVF, we arrive to the consideration that the time scale in Figure 3 should be multiplied by 100 . If we want to operate at 1KHz, this implies looking at tb = 1 ms /100 = 10 μs in Figure 3. This suggests r = 0 .02 m and that we would have an error 0.8cm. But our curve is roughly three times the size of the curves considered in Subsection III-B. “Practical Observation 2” implies that we should roughly triple r = 0 .02 m then and expect roughly three times the average error. In the real experiment, considering real life behaviors not modeled by the model in Subsection III-A, we decided to have r = 0 .18 m. The time budget of 1ms in the drone allow us to use roughly N = 800 points. We decided to use N = 300 .The tracking performance (distance to the curve) can be seen in Figure 6, and it is at most 0.09 m and 0.03 m on average. Figure 5 shows the agreement between the trajectory (green line) and the target curve (black). Figure 7 shows the measured physical states. Figure 8 shows the control inputs in high level Ψr

In this paper, we proposed a generalization of the GVF in [1] that allows a smoothing parameter r. For r > 0 the GVF is infinitely differentiable, which is beneficial for control loops that require derivatives. Perhaps even more important in practice, this smoothing allows the implementation of the GVF with much less sampled points, and thus much faster, at a modest expense of tracking error.
