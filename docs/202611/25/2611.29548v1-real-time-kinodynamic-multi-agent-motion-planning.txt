# Real-Time Kinodynamic Multi-Agent Motion Planning 

Jiarui Li, Runyu Zhang, and Gioele Zardini 

Abstract —Coordinating large robot fleets, from warehouses to quadrotor swarms, requires collision-free trajectories that satisfy dynamic constraints and stay within speed and acceleration bounds. Existing kinodynamic multi-agent planners enforce these bounds during the search or through trajectory optimization 

after it, which limits fleet size and speed. We present Tickwise Actions to Kinodynamic Trajectories (TAKT), a framework that accounts for the dynamics before the search and reduces planning to a lattice search for double-integrator robots. TAKT analytically bounds the clearance lost when a discrete plan is smoothed and reserves this margin on the lattice. A closed-form convolution provably converts any valid plan into dynamically feasible trajectories with continuous-time collision avoidance. For robots spanning many lattice vertices, we introduce a diamond formulation that permits one-tick sideways yielding and adapt the Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) solvers PIBT and LaCAM to it. With 50 robots, our planners run over 2,000 times faster than the strongest baseline in 2D halls and over 100 times in 3D, with comparable or lower makespan and cost. They plan about a thousand robots in seconds in 2D, and tens of seconds in 3D, and replan up to a thousand robots in real time. 

Index Terms —Multi-robot systems, kinodynamic motion plan-ning, Multi-Agent Path Finding, trajectory smoothing, collision avoidance, real-time planning 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# LARGE teams of robots that share a workspace are central to applications ranging from automated warehouse ful-fillment [1], [2] and autonomous intersection management [3] to coordinated quadrotor swarms [4], [5], with commercial facilities deploying hundreds of robots in a single shared workspace [1]. In these settings, each robot must navigate from its start position to its goal without colliding with static obstacles or other robots. Physical execution further requires that the motions obey the robot dynamics and actuation limits, which defines the Kinodynamic Multi-Agent Motion Planning Problem (KMAMPP). A solution assigns one time-parametrized trajectory to each robot. The trajectories must be conflict-free , so that robot bodies remain disjoint from obstacles and one another at every instant, and dynamically feasible , so that the robot dynamics and actuation limits are satisfied [6]. First, in offline planning, i.e., before execution begins, the computational burden grows with the number of robots, so the available planning time caps the fleet size that can be coordinated. Second, a sufficiently fast planner can update the trajectories while the robots keep moving (Fig. 1), provided that each update returns within the lead time before it is executed. Such real-time planning [7], [8] tightens the time budget further as the fleet grows. Existing kinodynamic multi-agent planners remain several orders of magnitude slower than such time budgets for large fleets. Joint methods explore the combined state space of the fleet through sampling [9], [10] or optimize continuous trajectories under pairwise collision constraints [11]. Both couple inter-robot coordination with dynamics-constrained motion synthesis, so the state dimension and the number of collision constraints grow with every robot, which restricts these planners to relatively small teams [12]. By contrast, solvers for the related problem of MAPF scale to thousands of agents. Standard MAPF coordinates point agents on a graph through synchronized moves and waits, avoiding simultaneous vertex occupancy and opposing edge arXiv:2611.29548v1 [cs.RO] 22 Nov 2026 2

traversals [13]. Within this abstraction, PIBT constructs joint moves through priority inheritance and backtracking [14], and LaCAM embeds this efficient successor generation in a complete configuration search that has solved instances with thousands of agents in tens of seconds [15], [16]. Its solutions, however, are discrete schedules that ab-stract away robot-body geometry and dynamics, and not the continuous, dynamically feasible trajectories required by the KMAMPP. Several planners build on MAPF to solve the KMAMPP and differ in where they enforce dynamic feasibility. One class incorporates the dynamics during the search: db-CBS and db-LaCAM [12], [17] run MAPF search over kinodynamic motion primitives, while K-CBS [18] co-ordinates single-robot kinodynamic planners within Conflict-Based Search (CBS). Searching over higher-dimensional kin-odynamic states and primitives is expensive, however, which limits scalability to tens of robots [12]. A second class enforces the dynamics after the discrete search through continuous post-processing. MAPF/C+POST [4] constructs a discrete schedule and then solves constrained trajectory optimization problems within safe corridors, and Park et al. [19] additionally guar-antee that these problems are feasible, reporting an average planning time of 6.4 s for 64 robots. Because postprocessing can reduce obstacle clearance and inter-robot separation, these pipelines must enforce continuous-time collision constraints during trajectory optimization, which takes several minutes for hundreds of robots [4]. In both classes, executable motion thus requires additional computation, either to generate and evaluate kinodynamic motions during the search or to solve trajectory optimization problems after it. As the fleet grows, this computation limits how much of the speed and scalability of MAPF carries over to kinodynamic planning, particularly within the tighter time budgets of real-time planning. We resolve this bottleneck by accounting for the robot dy-namics before the discrete search. Specifically, we analytically bound the clearance lost when a discrete plan is reconstructed into continuous trajectories under the prescribed speed and acceleration caps, and we reserve the corresponding margin in the discrete search. A closed-form reconstruction, applied independently to each robot, then converts any valid discrete plan into smooth, dynamically feasible trajectories that are provably conflict-free in continuous time. This approach needs neither kinodynamic search nor trajectory optimization. The discrete search still ranges over synchronized moves and waits only, so the speed of MAPF solvers carries over, and any discrete solver that returns valid plans becomes a certified kinodynamic planner. 

A. Statement of Contribution 

This paper presents Tickwise Actions to Kinodynamic Tra-jectories (TAKT), a framework that certifies dynamic feasibil-ity and continuous-time collision avoidance through discrete coordination, together with two fast planners built on it. TAKT brings kinodynamic multi-agent motion planning to real-time speeds for fleets of hundreds of robots and scales to more than a thousand robots, more than an order of magnitude beyond the largest fleets solved by existing baselines. We model the robots as equal-radius closed balls in 2D and 3D with double-integrator dynamics and common speed and acceleration caps. Our contributions are threefold. First, we formulate TAKT as a pipeline of three stages, namely Discretization , Discrete Coordination , and Continuous Reconstruction (Section IV). Discretization embeds a lattice in the workspace, reserves a certified clearance radius around each robot, and reduces the KMAMPP to a discrete search problem (Problem IV.1). Discrete Coordination solves this problem with synchronized moves and waits, and Continu-ous Reconstruction filters the motion of each robot into a smooth trajectory. We prove that every valid discrete plan reconstructs into conflict-free trajectories that satisfy the speed and acceleration caps, independently of the discrete solver (Theorem VII.1). We also derive sufficient clearance con-ditions under which a discrete plan exists, both at a fixed lattice resolution and under lattice refinement (Theorem VII.2 and Corollaries VII.2 and VII.3). Second, we introduce a diamond formulation of the discrete problem and two planners that solve it. On a fine lattice, a robot spans many vertices, and a Euclidean separation rule can prevent it from yielding sideways in the same tick in which another robot advances. The diamond formulation restores this one-tick sideways yielding, and we derive a sharp condition on the diamond size that guarantees the Euclidean separa-tion required by reconstruction (Section V). We then adapt PIBT and LaCAM to this formulation through backtracking over simultaneous blockers, priority reshuffling in stationary groups, Euclidean candidate ordering, and memory-efficient goal-distance queries, which yields TAKT-PIBT and TAKT-LaCAM (Section VI). With 50 robots, TAKT-LaCAM is 2,200 to 2,600 times faster than MAPF/C+POST [4] in four 2D halls and 105 to 415 times faster on three 3D maps, with comparable or lower makespan and cost (Section IX-B). Both planners compute trajectories for about a thousand robots in seconds in 2D and in tens of seconds in 3D (Section IX-C), and ablation studies isolate the effect of each design ele-ment (Section IX-D). In physics simulation, fleets of up to 1,082 differential-drive robots and 1,010 quadrotors track the planned trajectories without collisions (Section IX-E). Third, we develop two paradigms for real-time replanning under online changes to goals and to the environment (Sec-tion VIII). Whole-fleet replanning computes a complete new plan and, in our experiments, returns within 500 ms for up to 763 robots in an empty hall and 335 quadrotors around a central pillar. Incremental replanning extends the plan with TAKT-PIBT by one configuration per tick, faster than the robots execute it, for up to 1,018 robots and 671 quadrotors on the same maps (Section IX-F). 

Organization: Section II reviews related work, and Sec-tion III states the problem. Section IV presents the TAKT framework, Section V the diamond formulation, and Sec-tion VI the two planners. Section VII establishes the guar-antees, Section VIII presents real-time planning, Section IX reports the experiments, and Section X concludes the paper. 3

II. R ELATED WORK 

We review kinodynamic motion planning, MAPF, and meth-ods that connect them. Here we use path to denote a geometric route without timestamps, and trajectory to denote a time-parameterized motion. 

A. Kinodynamic Motion Planning 

Kinodynamic planning searches for trajectories that sat-isfy dynamic constraints. Sampling-based methods such as kinodynamic rapidly exploring random trees (RRT) [20] and stable sparse RRT (SST) [10] generate candidate motions by integrating sampled controls through the robot dynamics. Optimization-based methods optimize a parameterized trajec-tory subject to dynamic constraints, as in polynomial and minimum-snap trajectory generation [21]. Conflict-free way-points alone do not ensure continuous-time safety. Collision avoidance must be enforced along the trajectory or established by a post-hoc separate verification. Multi-robot extensions either plan jointly or coordinate separate robot plans. Joint methods explore the combined state space through cooperative sampling [9] or optimize all trajectories under pairwise separation constraints, using methods such as sequential convex programming [11]. These approaches couple the robots directly, so the dimension of the search space or the number of separation constraints grows with every added robot, which restricts these planners to relatively small teams [12]. Prioritized planning coordinates separate plans by planning robots sequentially, with each avoiding the trajectories of higher-priority robots [22], [23]. Dynamic feasibility then depends on the single-robot planner. While avoiding direct coupling, the trajectories fixed for higher-priority robots can leave a later robot without a feasible trajectory [23]. Real-time planners replan while the robot moves. Each replan starts from the state that the robot will reach after a lead time and must return within that lead time, resulting in higher runtime requirements [7], [8]. Reported planning times of centralized kinodynamic planners range from seconds for tens of robots to minutes for hundreds [4], [19]. Centralized kinodynamic planners account for dynamics and collision avoidance through the states, controls, or trajectory constraints of every robot. The resulting computational burden limits both the fleet size and the speed of replanning, which motivates solving the coordination problem on a simplified motion model. 

B. Multi-Agent Path Finding on Graphs 

MAPF serves as an abstraction of such a coordination problem on graphs. In standard discrete-time MAPF, agents move along an edge or wait at a vertex in each synchronized step. Plans avoid simultaneous occupancy of a vertex and opposite traversal of an edge [13]. This model abstracts away robot-body geometry and differential constraints. CBS resolves conflicts through a high-level tree of con-strained single-agent searches [24], while M* expands the joint search where agents interact [25]. PIBT constructs one joint move at a time, in which a blocked agent passes its priority to the agent in its way [14]. LaCAM embeds such successor generation in a complete search over configurations and has solved instances with ten thousand agents [15]. Closed-loop approaches repeatedly plan from the observed configuration and commit the next joint move [26]–[28]. Large-agent MAPF models footprints spanning multiple vertices [29], while multi-dependency PIBT handles moves blocked by several agents [30]. 

C. From Discrete Coordination to KMAMPP 

Standard MAPF discarded robot-body geometry, inter-step motion, and dynamic constraints, which are inevitable for solving KMAMPP, for better scalability. Two lines of work bridge the two fields. One line retains kinodynamic reasoning during the search. K-CBS coordinates single-robot kinodynamic planners within CBS [18]. db-CBS and db-ECBS search precomputed prim-itives with bounded discontinuities and remove those dis-continuities through joint trajectory optimization [17], [31]. db-LaCAM propagates controls from applicable primitives and coordinates the resulting motions through a kinodynamic extension of PIBT within LaCAM, with reported experiments reaching 50 robots [12]. Precomputed primitives encode local dynamics in advance, but the search still carries kinodynamic states and validates candidate motions. Because dynamics enter the search directly through the state and transition representations, coordinating these higher-dimensional kino-dynamic states limits scalability to tens of robots. A second line constructs dynamically feasible trajectories 

after discrete coordination. MAPF/C+POST constructs safe corridors from a discrete schedule and optimizes one trajectory per robot within them [4]. Park et al. [19] additionally guar-antee feasibility of the resulting optimization problems. These pipelines preserve obstacle clearance and inter-robot separa-tion through continuous trajectory constraints, so executable motion still requires solving expensive post-hoc constrained optimization problems, limiting scalability. 

D. Summary and Positioning 

The challenge addressed here is to retain simple discrete co-ordination while obtaining smooth, conflict-free motion under given dynamic limits through direct reconstruction. TAKT framework establishes the reconstruction guarantee 

before search and makes continuous feasibility a property of any discrete plan. At a chosen lattice resolution, the prescribed speed and acceleration caps and reconstruction kernel fix the tick duration, filter window, and clearance certificate. The search then coordinates only synchronized axial moves and waits. Every valid plan reconstructs into smooth, collision-free trajectories satisfying the dynamic constraints through independent closed-form convolutions. This guarantee is in-dependent of the discrete solver, and reconstruction requires neither trajectory optimization nor collision repair. Building on this framework, we develop two fast discrete planners whose efficiency enables real-time planning for fleets of up to a thousand robots. 4

A. Pipeline Overview 

In this section, we present the TAKT framework, which co-ordinates the robots on a lattice and obtains dynamic feasibility afterwards from a fixed filter whose effect on the clearance is bounded before the search. Its pipeline comprises three stages (Fig. 2). Discretization places a lattice in the workspace and sets the timing and clearance requirements according to the speed and acceleration limits and the chosen smoothing filter. 

Discrete Coordination plans synchronized moves and waits on this lattice, maintaining the required obstacle clearance and inter-robot separation at every step. Continuous Reconstruction 

filters each robot’s motion independently, using a common kernel, to produce smooth, conflict-free trajectories within the speed and acceleration caps. The guarantee holds for every valid discrete plan, allowing PIBT [14], LaCAM [15], or another discrete solver.

E. Computational Cost 

Diamond conflicts can be checked using nearby robots only. The following bound limits the number of immediate blockers of one target. 

Lemma VI.2 (Number of blocking robots) . In a valid diamond configuration, at most 3d robots have planning diamonds that intersect the planning diamond at a given target. 

Proof sketch. The centers of the blocking robots lie within Manhattan distance 2h of the target and are pairwise more than 2h apart, where h = mε . The open Manhattan balls of radius h around them are disjoint and lie in the Manhattan ball of radius 3h around the target, whose volume is 3d times theirs. Recursive dependencies may still involve many robots, so the budget B remains necessary to bound the search effort. Two targets can conflict only if their current robot positions are within Euclidean distance (2 m+2) ε. A spatial hash rebuilt at each tick collects these neighbors. The packing argument also bounds each neighbor list by a constant γ depending only on the dimension d. Given the priority order and goal distances, one generator call costs 

O(N + N γ + N Bγ ) (36) for the hash, the neighbor lists, and at most N B candidate attempts, each checking O(γ) neighbors. Algorithm 1 returns a reference trajectory for every robot, with position, velocity, and acceleration at all times, which a low-level controller on the robot tracks at high frequency. When goals or workspace conditions change during execution, the planner must update these trajectories, and we call the time that an update takes its computation time . Because planning takes time, a replan starts from the state of the fleet at a lead time in the future, and it must return within this lead time [7], [8]. In this section, we present two paradigms for real-time planning with TAKT. The lead time that the fleet can afford differs between the two paradigms, so each paradigm has its own criterion for real-time planning. 

Definition VIII.1 (Real-time planning) . Replanning is real-time when its computation time is at most 500 ms (2 Hz) under whole-fleet replanning (Section VIII-A) or one tick ∆t per decision cycle under incremental replanning (Section VIII-B). 14 

The whole-fleet criterion lies within the 0.1–1 s cycles of existing single-robot planners [35]–[39]. Robots track the com-mitted motion while a replacement is computed. Incremental replanning has no trajectory beyond the supplied configura-tions and must append one configuration per tick: 101 ms in our 2D experiments and 43 ms in 3D. 

A. Whole-Fleet Replanning 

This paradigm is the default setting for the planner-controller interaction [7], [8], [35]. When an update is needed at time t, the planner computes a complete replacement plan from a future configuration on the current discrete plan. The existing and replacement sequences are joined at that configuration and reconstructed with the same filter, while the robots continue executing the committed motion. Figure 1 shows an example with 24 quadrotors. After the no-fly zone appears, TAKT computes new reference trajectories for all robots within 30 ms, and the controllers then switch to the new trajectories. Figures 9 and 11 show that in the 2D experiments, TAKT-PIBT produces complete trajectories within 500 ms, in the median over 20 instances, for up to around 750 robots in the empty hall and up to 200 robots in the hall with three racks. The incremental paradigm extends real-time planning to larger fleets by using the one-step structure of PIBT. 

B. Incremental Replanning 

This paradigm rests on two observations. First, by Eq. (19), the reference position ˜xi(t), velocity ˙˜xi(t), and acceleration 

¨˜xi(t) that a controller needs at time t depend on the discrete motion only up to t + Tf /2, so the reference trajectory can be constructed on demand as execution advances. Second, unlike LaCAM, PIBT has a closed-loop structure and constructs its solution one configuration at a time [26], [27]. In every tick, the paradigm therefore runs one decision cycle , which reads the changes that arrived since the last cycle, calls Algorithm 2 once, and appends the returned configuration to the discrete motion, which stays at least Tf /2 ahead of the execution. A change in the problem simply enters the next decision cycle. Since each cycle computes one configuration in place of the entire plan, the paradigm meets the one-tick criterion for fleets for which a complete replan takes longer than 500 ms (Section IX-F). Moreover, every appended configuration is valid by Lemma VI.1, so the executed motion remains the reconstruction of a valid plan, and the guarantee of Theorem VII.1 holds. IX. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

In this paper we presented TAKT, a framework that certifies continuous dynamic feasibility through discrete coordination for the KMAMPP. TAKT bounds the clearance lost during continuous trajectory reconstruction before the search begins, and reserves that clearance margin on the workspace lattice. A closed-form convolution, applied independently to each robot, then converts synchronized lattice moves and waits into smooth trajectories that avoid collisions and satisfy the speed and acceleration caps. This soundness guarantee holds for every valid discrete plan, so any discrete solver that returns valid plans becomes a certified kinodynamic planner. Our analysis also establishes sufficient clearance conditions under which a valid discrete plan exists at a fixed lattice resolution and under lattice refinement. To coordinate robots that span multiple lattice vertices, we introduced a diamond formulation whose conflict rule supports sideways yielding in a single tick while guaranteeing the Euclidean separation required by reconstruction. We adapted PIBT and LaCAM to this dia-mond formulation by backtracking over simultaneous blockers, reshuffling priorities in stationary groups, ordering candidate moves by Euclidean distance to the goal, and querying exact goal distances with retained backward A* searches. With 50 robots, the resulting planners ran more than three orders of magnitude faster than the strongest baseline in the 2D halls and more than two orders of magnitude faster on the 3D maps, with comparable or lower makespan and cost. The planners computed complete trajectories for about a thousand robots in seconds in 2D and in tens of seconds in 3D, and simulated differential-drive robots and quadrotors tracked these trajectories without collisions. TAKT also replanned in real time under online changes to goals and keep-out regions. Replanning the complete fleet returned within 500 ms for hundreds of robots, and incremental replanning extended real-time planning to fleets of up to a thousand robots by computing one configuration per tick. Future work includes tighter certificates and conflict rules for narrower passages and denser fleets; incorporating track-ing uncertainty and communication delays, with hardware validation; and co-designing lattice resolution, reconstruction kernels, and dynamic caps to optimize clearance, runtime, and solution quality [45]. C. Response Search and Rollback 

RESPOND in Algorithm 2 keeps an agenda of the robots that must respond to the tentative targets of the current branch. The blocking robots of a target are placed at the front of the agenda in the priority order, each is resolved fully before the next, and a robot that another branch has decided in the meantime is skipped, with its target constraining every later choice. The candidate orders, the distances, and the priority order are frozen for the tick. A call of R ESPOND that returns failure has undone every tentative assignment it made, so the branch of its caller is restored to the state before that call, and the caller tries its next candidate. When the budget is 24 

exhausted, the interruption propagates to the root without local cleanup, and the root then discards every tentative assignment of its search and marks those robots undecided. The same budget counts the attempts of all branches of the search for one root, so trying another root target does not refresh the budget. 

D. Neighbor Queries and Direct Commitment 

One call of Algorithm 2 tests every candidate target against the positions and the targets of the other robots, and testing all pairs of robots would cost O(N 2) per tick. Conflicts are local, because a target lies within one pitch of its robot, so only robots within a fixed distance of each other can conflict. The generator uses this locality in two ways. Neighbor queries restrict every conflict test to a list of nearby robots, whose length is bounded by a constant (Lemma A.4). Direct commitment assigns robots that have no other robot near their preferred move before any search starts, so the response search of Appendix B-C runs only where robots interact. Together they give the cost Eq. (36) of one generator call, which is linear in N apart from the sort of the priorities. Two vertices are in diamond conflict when their planning diamonds intersect, as defined in Problem V.1. A pair in diamond conflict has ∥p − q∥1 ≤ 2h by Eq. (23), and 

∥p − q∥2 ≤ ∥ p − q∥1, so the pair lies within the Euclidean conflict radius R = 2 h = 2 m∗ε. Under the Euclidean rule of the ablation in Section IX-D, the conflict radius is R = 2 rε.A target lies within ε of its robot. The targets of two robots can therefore conflict only when the robots are within R + 2 ε

of each other, and the current position or a target of another robot can conflict with a candidate t only when that robot lies within R + ε of t. The neighbor list of a robot holds the robots within R + 2 ε of its position, and the blocking robots of a target are found in the neighbor list of the robot that proposes it. A spatial hash with cells of side R + 4 ε is rebuilt at every tick, and a query for the robots within a radius scans the 3d cells around the query point. Before the searches of a tick, a robot with no other robot within R + 4 ε of its first candidate is committed to that candidate directly. The query radius covers R + ε, so the committed target is separated from the current position and from every possible target of every other robot. Fixed targets are committed before this step and remain fixed throughout the call, so the direct commitment considers undecided robots only.
