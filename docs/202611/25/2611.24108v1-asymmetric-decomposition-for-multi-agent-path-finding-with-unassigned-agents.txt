Abstract—Robots without assigned tasks may obstruct active
robots, while routing them to designated parking locations
can cause unnecessary travel. This article proposes flow-oracle
conflict-based search (FOCBS) for Multi-Agent Path Finding
with unassigned agents (MAPF-UA). FOCBS searches over
assigned-agent paths using conflict-based search (CBS) and
routes targetless unassigned agents through a min-cost flow
oracle. When the selected assigned-agent paths leave unassigned
agents no collision-free paths, flow-conflict branching converts
min-cut blockers into assigned-agent constraints, which guide the
search without excluding optimal solutions. A batched zero-cost
warm start accelerates the oracle, and start-avoiding tie-breaking
reduces unassigned-agent movement. For feasible instances on
finite graphs, we prove termination and global minimization
of assigned-agent sum of service time, measured by last-arrival
times. Unassigned-agent movement is minimized conditional on
the selected assigned-agent paths, without a global lexicographic
optimality guarantee. Benchmark experiments show that FOCBS
scales well with the number of unassigned agents, and ablation
studies show that, compared with discarding non-completable
assigned-agent paths, flow-conflict branching solves substan-
tially more instances while retaining optimality. Simulation and
physical-robot experiments demonstrate plan executability, with
laboratory tests reducing fleet travel by 40.8% relative to the
parking-based coordination baseline. The source code and related
resources are available at https://github.com/Chin-hsi/focbs.
Index Terms—Conflict-based search, min-cost flow, Multi-
Agent Path Finding, multi-robot systems, unassigned agents.

Multi-Agent Path Finding with unassigned agents (MAPF-
UA) [6] captures this setting. Assigned agents have designated
targets, whereas unassigned agents have only starts and may
terminate at any vertex; all agents must remain collision-free.
Minimizing assigned-agent service time alone may move unas-
signed agents unnecessarily. Subject to collision-free paths for
all agents, we therefore first minimize the sum of service
time (SST) for assigned agents and then minimize the sum of
moves by unassigned agents (SMU) conditional on the selected
assigned-agent paths.
Because MAPF-UA generalizes MAPF, solving it optimally

is NP-hard [7]. Unassigned agents add two difficulties. Their
terminal vertices are decision variables, and permuting them
over the same vertices yields equivalent states. Moreover, static
unassigned agents may greatly increase service time or render
dense instances unsolvable [6]. In dense instances, scarce free
vertices leave unassigned agents little space to yield.
Conflict-based search (CBS) [8] uses a two-level search.
The high level resolves conflicts by creating child nodes
in a constraint tree, each imposing a constraint on one of
the conflicting agents; the low level replans that agent’s
path under the updated constraints. Applying CBS jointly
to both agent types places assigned agents and unassigned
agents in the same high-level constraint tree (Fig. 1(c), left).
This direct application does not fully exploit the targetless,
interchangeable nature of unassigned agents. As unassigned-
agent density grows, branching on conflicts involving them
substantially increases high-level search overhead, creating a
scalability bottleneck.
Optimal CBS extensions reduce search effort via stronger
reasoning and splitting, including improved CBS (ICBS) [9],
disjoint splitting [10], dependency-graph heuristics [11], and
symmetry reasoning [12]. In-depth cluster reasoning strength-
ens CBS heuristics by identifying conflicts involving more
than two agents [13]. Enhanced CBS (ECBS) [14] and ex-
plicit estimation CBS (EECBS) [15] improve efficiency with
bounded-suboptimality guarantees. For multi-agent combina-
torial path finding, CBSS-A and CBSS-AF combine approx-
imate target sequencing with collision-free routing [16]. The
cited formulations consider agents with prescribed targets.
Other methods emphasize scalable coordination or feasible-
path search. Priority inheritance with backtracking (PIBT) [17]
provides fast online coordination, the LaCAM family [18]
uses lazy constraint addition, and the multi-agent corridor
generating algorithm (MACGA) [19] constructs target-directed
corridors and evacuates blocking agents. The acceptance-
rejection sampling-guided discrete rapidly-exploring random
tree (adRRT) [20] searches for feasible paths through random-
ized expansion. Learning-based methods, including pathfind-
ing via reinforcement and imitation multi-agent learning (PRI-
MAL) [21] and transformer-based approaches [22], train de-
centralized policies for online decisions. Chen et al. [23]
combine deep reinforcement learning with hot supervision
contrastive loss to learn coordination without interagent com-
munication. These methods provide efficient coordination
mechanisms, but their original formulations do not explicitly
minimize unassigned-agent movement.
Network-flow and decomposition methods offer another
way to organize multi-agent planning. Time-expanded net-
works (TENs) encode routing as network flow [24], and
conflict-based min-cost flow (CBM) [25] combines CBS be-
tween teams with min-cost flow within teams having pre-
scribed target sets. For labeled robots, multicommodity-flow
encodings support integer-programming algorithms for several
path-cost objectives [26]. Branch-and-cut-and-price also uses
decomposition [27], while anonymous MAPF exploits agent
interchangeability with respect to goals [28]. Layered decom-
position for large agents partitions the planning problem and
merges subproblems when needed to restore solvability [29].

Lexicographic bi-objective MAPF [30] and multi-objective
CBS [31] address ordered objectives and Pareto-optimal so-
lution sets, respectively. These formulations assign each agent
a target or each group a target set, whereas unassigned agents
may stop at any free vertex.
MAPF-UA is closely related to MAPF with the reachability
objective (MAPF-RO) [32], in which each agent must visit its
target at least once but may subsequently leave it. Gabay et
al. [32] reduce the reachability-based MAPF-UA formulation
to MAPF-RO by assigning each unassigned agent its start ver-
tex as its target; this reduction preserves soundness, optimality,
and completeness under their first-arrival-based SST objective.
The resulting MAPF-RO instance treats both groups as agents
with targets, and the originally unassigned agents satisfy their
targets at the initial time. Their subsequent movement there-
fore contributes nothing to the standard MAPF-RO objective,
which neither penalizes that movement nor optimizes SMU.
In MAPF-UA, unassigned agents are commonly treated as
static obstacles, assigned surrogate targets such as their starts
or parking locations, or planned individually within a joint
search. These treatments do not fully exploit the targetless,
interchangeable nature of unassigned agents. Decomposition is
a natural approach: with assigned-agent paths fixed, targetless
unassigned agents can be routed by single-commodity min-
cost flow in polynomial time. However, SST-optimal assigned-
agent paths computed without unassigned agents may occupy
the vertices and time steps they need to yield, leaving no
collision-free paths for unassigned agents, and the failure alone
does not identify which paths to revise. The central challenge
is to exploit the targetless structure for scalability while using
such failures to guide the assigned-agent search.
Feeding subproblem infeasibility back to a higher-level
search follows the decomposition principle of Benders [33].
Logic-based Benders decomposition derives constraints from
subproblem inference [34], and combinatorial Benders cuts
exclude combinations of decisions that make a subproblem
infeasible [35]. FOCBS adopts a similar feedback idea for
MAPF-UA: a minimum cut [36] identifies assigned-agent
reservations whose joint retention prevents unassigned-agent
routing, and their negations form a CBS branching disjunc-
tion. Standard CBS splitting enforces such a disjunction for
a pairwise conflict [8]; for MAPF-UA, the key step is to
show that the flow-derived disjunction covers every feasible

longer horizons.
Our contributions are as follows.
1) We formulate sequential MAPF-UA and establish its
asymmetric decomposition: finding SST-optimal com-
pletable assigned-agent paths is NP-hard, whereas
unassigned-agent routing under fixed assigned-agent
paths is solvable by min-cost flow in time polynomial
in TEN size.
2) We propose flow-oracle conflict-based search (FOCBS),
using CBS for assigned agents and flow-conflict branch-
ing to convert min-cut blockers into a CBS disjunction.
We prove that this disjunction preserves every feasible
completion, including those with longer horizons, which
yields termination and sequential optimality for feasible
instances on finite graphs.
3) We develop a batched zero-cost warm start to accelerate
the flow oracle, and a start-avoiding tie-breaking rule to
reduce SMU without compromising SST optimality.
4) We evaluate FOCBS on standard benchmarks and val-
idate path executability in warehouse simulation and
physical-robot experiments.
The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Sec-
tion II formulates MAPF-UA and the asymmetric decomposi-
tion. Section III presents the FOCBS algorithm and the flow
oracle. Section IV evaluates FOCBS on standard benchmarks,
reports ablation experiments and runtime analysis, and val-
idates path executability through simulation and real-world
experiments. Section V concludes this article.

We formulate sequential MAPF-UA, which minimizes SST
globally and then SMU for the selected assigned-agent paths.
Finding SST-optimal completable assigned-agent paths is NP-
hard, whereas unassigned-agent routing under fixed ones is
polynomial-time solvable by min-cost flow.

III. FOCBS ALGORITHM
FOCBS solves sequential MAPF-UA with a CBS search
over assigned agents and a flow oracle for unassigned-agent
routing (Fig. 3). Following Proposition 1, it exploits the
absence of individual targets: a single-commodity flow de-
cides the terminal vertices of unassigned agents implicitly;
hence, the high-level search constrains only A-paths. Each
constraint-tree (CT) node N stores only constraints and A-
paths πA
N, whose horizon is TN := T(πA
N); the open list OPEN
is ordered by SST. FOCBS first resolves conflicts among

assigned agents with standard CBS splits. Once the A-paths
of a node are mutually conflict-free, the oracle treats them
as reservations and computes minimum-SMU U-paths in a
time-expanded network (TEN). If such U-paths exist, FOCBS
returns the complete plan; otherwise, flow-conflict branching
converts min-cut blockers into constraints on assigned agents
and replans their paths. The two groups thus remain coupled:
unassigned agents determine the flow value, the TEN size, and
whether the selected A-paths are completable.

The construction gives a two-way correspondence between
paths and flow. Sending one unit from each unassigned-agent
start along its vertex, wait, and gadget arcs maps every U-
path set in R(πA

cost. Vertex-capacity and central gadget arcs exclude vertex
and edge conflicts, respectively, while reservations exclude
conflicts with πA

Conversely, the acyclic TEN allows every value-m integral
flow to decompose into m unit s+–s−paths, which project
onto G after deleting split and gadget nodes. Call an integral
flow canonical if every gadget traversal exits at the endpoint
opposite to its entry. The merge–split gadget also admits a
same-endpoint traversal, or turnback, which projects to a wait.
If a flow contains a turnback at v, the direct wait arc vout
t
→
vin
t+1 is unused; otherwise the unit vertex capacity at (v, t)
would be exceeded. Replacing the turnback by this wait arc
preserves feasibility and reduces its cost from 1 to 0. Repeating
this replacement yields a canonical flow, whose cost equals
the SMU of its projected paths. Every minimum-cost integral
flow is therefore canonical, and the minimum flow cost equals
the minimum SMU. Because unassigned agents may stop at
any unreserved vertex, the horizon TN suffices for fixed A-
paths: any U-path set over 0, . . . , TN can be extended by zero-
cost waiting, and any longer conflict-free U-path set can be
restricted to 0, . . . , TN without increasing SMU.
The oracle uses successive shortest path (SSP) [37] on
a TEN of O(LN) vertices and arcs, where LN = (|V | +
|E|)(TN + 1). One oracle call takes O(LN + mLN log LN)
time using at most m reduced-cost Dijkstra searches with
binary heaps.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
The scalability experiment increases unassigned-agent load,
and the fixed-density experiment varies the proportions of as-
signed agents and unassigned agents. Ablation studies evaluate
flow-conflict branching, BZ-SSP, and tie-breaking, followed by
simulation and physical-robot validation.
A. Experimental Settings
1) Instances: Fig. 6 shows the four standard MAPF maps
used in the scalability experiment [4] (an open grid, random
obstacles, bottleneck rooms, and warehouse aisles) and the
smaller open grid and custom warehouse used in the fixed-
density experiment. Assigned-agent start–target pairs come
from the standard even and random scenarios, and unassigned-
agent starts are sampled without replacement from the remain-
ing traversable vertices. All solvers receive the same seeded
instances.
2) Methods: The scalability experiment compares FOCBS
with CBS-UA and the fast yielding baselines PIBT, MACGA,
and LaCAM [8], [17]–[19]. CBS-UA runs CBS on all agents,
and each unassigned agent may stop at any vertex that sat-
isfies its constraints. CBS-UA minimizes SST but does not
optimize SMU. For PIBT, MACGA, and LaCAM, unassigned
agents wait by default and yield with the lowest priority. The
yielding baselines do not guarantee sequential optimality. The
fixed-density experiment additionally distinguishes CBS-static,
which fixes unassigned agents as obstacles; LCBS, our joint
lexicographic CBS baseline for MAPF-UA, which adapts the
lexicographic bi-objective framework of [30], places all agents
in one constraint tree, and globally minimizes (SST, SMU);
CBS-SSP, a two-stage baseline with the same sequential ob-
jective as FOCBS; and FOCBS-noTB, which removes only the
start-avoiding tie breaker. CBS-SSP first obtains SST-optimal
A-paths with CBS0, which runs CBS on assigned agents only,
and then calls an SSP oracle once to minimize SMU. CBS-
SSP stops if no U-paths exist.

B. Scalability with Increasing Unassigned-Agent Load
We fix the assigned-agent count n and add unassigned
agents to increase ALR from 0.05 to 0.95 on the four standard
maps. Optimal CBS becomes rapidly more expensive as n
increases. To keep assigned-agent search tractable and isolate
the effect of the unassigned-agent count m, we select four
values of n per map and scenario type for which CBS0 solves
all 25 seeds with m = 0 within a 10-s per-instance limit. Each
map–ALR setting combines the four assigned-agent counts
with 25 even and 25 random seeds, giving 200 instances per
method and 76,000 runs in total.
CBS-UA places both agent groups in one constraint tree,
so adding unassigned agents increases the burden of conflict
resolution. Aggregated over all tested loads, the success rate
of CBS-UA is at most 43.9% on any map (Fig. 7). FOCBS
confines CBS search to assigned agents and routes unassigned
agents through the flow oracle, achieving overall success rates
of 94.1–100% across the four maps. The median runtime of
FOCBS over solved instances also grows more gradually with
unassigned-agent count.
PIBT, MACGA, and LaCAM return solutions faster than
FOCBS, but achieve lower overall success rates on the three
obstacle maps (73.4–91.5% across the tested loads; Figs. 7
and 8(b)). On instances solved by both methods, their SST
exceeds that of FOCBS by 2.0–27.0%, and their mean SMU is
2.9–5.9 times that of FOCBS. Under the tested assigned-agent
loads, FOCBS finds globally SST-optimal solutions within
1 s for the vast majority of instances on the empty, random,
and room maps, whereas the warehouse map requires longer
runtimes.
Fig. 8(a) shows that the success rate of CBS-UA de-
clines with increasing unassigned-agent count at every tested
assigned-agent count. FOCBS maintains higher success rates
under the corresponding settings, showing that the advan-
tage over joint search holds across different assigned-agent
loads. In the random warehouse scenarios, FOCBS solves all
25 instances with 10 assigned agents and 5,404 unassigned
agents, indicating that FOCBS scales well with the number of
unassigned agents.
On the warehouse map, the success rate of FOCBS begins
to decline at lower densities as the assigned-agent count
increases. Assigned-agent paths reserve space-time resources
in the TEN, restricting the space available for unassigned

3) Start-Avoiding Tie-Breaking: FOCBS and FOCBS-noTB
differ only in the start-avoiding tie breaker. TB reduces mean
SMU by 35.1–71.4% across the four common sets in Table I,
with unchanged SST and similar success rates and runtimes
in Fig. 9. An assigned-agent path that visits an unassigned
agent’s start forces that unassigned agent to move. Among
equal-cost choices, TB favors paths with fewer such visits,
reducing the need for yielding before the flow oracle plans
unassigned-agent paths.
4) Computational Cost: The near-one mean oracle-call
counts on three maps in Table III indicate that FOCBS usually
completes unassigned-agent routing with a single call. FOCBS
invokes the oracle only after finding mutually conflict-free
assigned-agent paths, avoiding repeated flow computations
during most of the CBS search. Consequently, even on the
random map, where assigned-agent search requires more CT
expansions, only 0.64% of expansions invoke the oracle.
The additional cost in difficult instances is concentrated
in flow computation and diagnosis. On room-32-32-4, the 28
timed-out runs account for 64.0% of oracle calls because infea-
sibility repeatedly triggers assigned-agent replanning and TEN
reconstruction. On the warehouse map, the timed-out runs
contribute only 9.8% of oracle calls but 93.2% of diagnostic
time, indicating expensive diagnoses rather than frequent calls.

area. The mean batch completion time, measured until all
assigned robots reach their goals, also decreases by 14.7%.
V. CONCLUSION
This article studied MAPF-UA, which arises when robots
with and without assigned tasks share a workspace. We estab-
lished an asymmetric decomposition and proposed FOCBS,


13
which searches over assigned agents with CBS, routes unas-
signed agents with a min-cost flow oracle, and converts routing
failures into assigned-agent constraints through flow-conflict
branching. For feasible instances on finite graphs, FOCBS
terminates with a sequentially optimal solution. Benchmark
results show that FOCBS scales well with the number of
unassigned agents at fixed assigned-agent load, and ablation
studies confirm the benefits of flow-conflict branching, BZ-
SSP, and start-avoiding tie-breaking. Laboratory experiments
show that yielding as needed reduces fleet travel by 40.8%
relative to parking-based coordination.
Several directions merit further study. FOCBS minimizes
SMU only for the selected SST-optimal assigned-agent paths.
Efficiently exploring alternative SST-optimal A-path sets may
further reduce SMU. Combinatorial search over assigned
agents limits scalability as tasks increase, and repeated di-
agnosis and TEN recomputation dominate hard instances on
bottleneck maps; bounded-suboptimal search and incremental
TEN updates may reduce these costs. Finally, the episode-level
objective ignores how terminal locations affect future tasks,
which motivates terminal-aware planning in lifelong settings.
