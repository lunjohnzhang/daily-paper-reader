# Byzantine Resilience of Robot Gathering under Round-Robin Scheduling

Abstract 

We study deterministic gathering of n anonymous, oblivious mobile robots in the plane under round-robin scheduling with rigid movement, in the presence of f Byzantine robots. Robots observe all robot positions with their multiplicities, while their local coordinate sys-tems may change adversarially between activations. The goal is for all correct robots to gather at a single point in finite time and remain there forever. We analyze ToMax , a simple algorithm that moves to a point of maximum multiplicity and stays still if and only if its current position is the unique maximum. We prove that ToMax achieves gathering whenever f ≤ ⌊ √n/ 2⌋, establishing an Ω( √n) lower bound on the achievable Byzantine resilience. arXiv:2611.22631v1 [cs.DC] 17 Nov 2026 1 Introduction 

We study the Byzantine resilience of deterministic gathering by Look–Compute–Move (LCM) robots [SY99] under round-robin scheduling. The gathering problem requires all correct (non-Byzantine) robots to reach a common point in finite time and remain there forever, regardless of the adversarial behavior of the Byzantine robots. Our focus is on how many Byzantine robots can be tolerated as the number of robots grows. Prior work has established a range of impossibility results for Byzantine gathering [AP06, DGMR06, IBTW11, DPR20], while positive guarantees remain scarce. Agmon and Peleg [AP06] showed that gathering is possible under fully synchronous scheduling (FSYNCH) whenever n ≥

3f + 1 , where n is the total number of robots and f is the number of Byzantine robots. This permits a constant fraction of the robots to be Byzantine. Défago, Gradinariu, Messika, and Raipin-Parvédy [DGMR06] noted that the maximum-multiplicity strategy achieves gathering under round-robin scheduling (RR) for n = 3 and f = 1 . It is straightforward to verify that the same strategy achieves gathering for every n ≥ 3 with f = 1 . However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous guarantee for deterministic gathering under RR permits the number of Byzantine faults to grow with n.Both FSYNCH and RR impose strong regularity on robot activations. Under FSYNCH, all robots are activated simultaneously; under RR, they are activated one at a time in a fixed cyclic order. Despite this regularity in both settings, the available guarantees differ substantially: a linear number of Byzantine faults under FSYNCH, but only a single fault under RR. This paper advances the resilience guarantee under RR from one Byzantine robot to Θ( √n)

Byzantine robots. Specifically, we prove that gathering is possible whenever n ≥ 4f 2, giving the resilience lower bound ψ(n) = Ω( √n). Here, ψ(n) denotes the largest number of Byzantine faults that can be tolerated by a gathering algorithm for n robots. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first superconstant resilience guarantee for deterministic Byzantine gathering under a scheduler other than FSYNCH. A substantial gap remains between our sufficient condition n ≥ 4f 2 and the general necessary condition n > 2f for tolerating up to f Byzantine robots. In particular, whether RR can support gathering despite a constant fraction of Byzantine robots, as FSYNCH does, remains open. Our result provides a starting point for determining how Byzantine resilience scales with the number of robots beyond fully synchronous activation. 

1.1 Related Work 

LCM robots and model assumptions. The LCM model, introduced by Suzuki and Ya-mashita [SY99], provides a formal framework for studying distributed algorithms for mobile robots. The model has since been extensively studied, particularly in the context of funda-mental coordination problems such as gathering and pattern formation. In each LCM cycle, a robot observes its surroundings ( Look ), computes a destination ( Compute ), and moves toward it ( Move ). Variants of the model differ in the robots’ capabilities and the assumptions on their environment. Oblivious robots retain no information between cycles, whereas non-oblivious robots can retain internal state. In the luminous model [DFP +12, DFP +16], each robot has a persistent light whose color is drawn from a finite set and is visible to itself and other robots. The solvability of various problems, including gathering, depends on the scheduling model, which dictates the timing and order of robot activations. Under fully synchronous (FSYNCH) and semi-synchronous (SSYNCH) scheduling, execution proceeds in rounds: FSYNCH activates every robot in each round, whereas SSYNCH activates an arbitrary nonempty subset, whose members execute their cycle phases synchronously. Under asynchronous scheduling (ASYNCH), phases may overlap across robots, and observations may become outdated before they are acted upon. Centralized (or sequential) schedulers allow only one robot to execute a complete cycle at a time; RR further fixes a cyclic activation order. A k-bounded scheduler allows each other 1robot at most k activations between consecutive activations of any given robot. The capabilities of robots in terms of movement and sensing provide further distinctions. With rigid movement, a robot always reaches its computed destination; with non-rigid move-ment, a move may be interrupted, subject to a fixed positive minimum progress guarantee, with closer destinations reached. For sensing, robots may or may not have the ability to detect multiplicity at a location. Without multiplicity detection, a snapshot reveals only occupied loca-tions. Weak multiplicity detection distinguishes one robot from multiple robots at each occupied location, while strong multiplicity detection reveals the exact number. This paper assumes rigid movement and strong multiplicity detection with RR scheduling, with all robots, including Byzantine robots, subject to the same cyclic activation order. 

Byzantine gathering in the plane. For non-rigid, oblivious robots, [AP06] proves that no deterministic algorithm can achieve gathering or even convergence under SSYNCH when n = 3 

and f = 1 . Under FSYNCH, the same work gives gathering algorithms for n ≥ 3f + 1 , as well as for the special case n = 3 and f = 1 . These results establish that fully synchronous activation can support gathering despite a linear number of Byzantine faults. The maximum-multiplicity strategy of [DGMR06] is the closest predecessor of our algorithm 

ToMax , presented in Section 3. The paper notes that, with strong multiplicity detection, this strategy tolerates one Byzantine robot among three robots under RR. For rigid moves and the tie rule used in ToMax , the extension to every n ≥ 3 with f = 1 is immediate: between two consecutive activations of the Byzantine robot, all n−1 correct robots are activated consecutively. Consider the n − 1 consecutive correct activations immediately following an activation of the Byzantine robot. After the first correct activation, there is a unique maximum-multiplicity point: if the robot moves, its destination gains one robot and becomes uniquely maximal; otherwise, its current position was already uniquely maximal. Every subsequent correct robot moves to that point or remains there. Since the Byzantine robot is not activated during this interval, all 

n − 1 correct robots gather at that point. As n − 1 > 1, the point remains uniquely maximal regardless of the Byzantine robot’s subsequent behavior, so all correct robots remain stationary. Note 5.2 of [DGMR06] also claims that the same strategy tolerates one Byzantine robot for every odd n ≥ 5 under any fair centralized scheduler. However, this claim is false, as shown by the following counterexample with n = 5 . Place two correct robots a1, a 2 at p and two correct robots c1, c 2 at a distinct point q. Under the fair schedule obtained by repeating 

(b, a 1, a 2, b, c 1, c 2) forever, let the Byzantine robot b move to p at its first activation in each period and to q at its second. Every correct robot is activated at the unique maximum-multiplicity point and therefore remains stationary, so gathering never occurs. The journal version by Défago, Potop-Butucaru, and Raipin-Parvédy [DPR20] establishes only impossibility results for Byzantine gathering; the positive guarantees claimed in [DGMR06] are not retained. In particular, Lemmas 16–18 rule out deterministic gathering for oblivious robots under centralized k-bounded schedulers, even with multiplicity detection, in the following cases: (i) n = 3 , f = 1 , and k ≥ 2; (ii) n is even, f ≥ 1, and k ≥ ⌈ (n − f )/f ⌉; and (iii) n is odd, f ≥ 2, and k ≥ ⌈ (n − f )/(f − 1) ⌉. Without multiplicity detection, Lemma 15 also rules out both deterministic and randomized weak gathering under RR for f ≥ 1 and n > f + 1 .Izumi, Bouzid, Tixeuil, and Wada [IBTW11] use a reduction based on BG-simulation to establish another impossibility result for deterministic Byzantine gathering. Translated into our notation, their result applies to n ≥ 4 robots with one Byzantine robot under an (n−1) -bounded centralized scheduler, even with multiplicity detection and internal memory. Note that this does not imply impossibility under the more restricted RR scheduler. As reviewed above, most known results on Byzantine gathering by LCM robots are impossi-bility results. To the best of our knowledge, prior positive guarantees for deterministic gathering are limited to FSYNCH with n ≥ 3f + 1 or (n, f ) = (3 , 1) [AP06], and RR with n ≥ 3 and 

f = 1 [DGMR06]. 2Round-robin and sequential scheduling. RR and more general sequential schedulers have also been studied for gathering and pattern formation without faults. Frei and Wada [FW26] study gathering from distinct initial positions under RR without multiplicity detection. They show that four or more oblivious robots can gather even with non-rigid movement, whereas three robots require additional self-consistency of their local coordinate systems. For three robots, maintaining either the local unit of distance or the compass across activations suffices, without requiring agreement between robots. These results isolate the effects of activation order and coordinate consistency even in the absence of faults. Flocchini, Navarra, Pattanayak, Piselli, and Santoro [FNP +26] consider arbitrary pattern formation under general fair sequential schedulers. For oblivious non-rigid robots, they give algorithms for every target pattern with between two and n distinct points without multiplicity detection, and show that weak multiplicity detection is necessary and sufficient for gathering when n > 2. Thus, the distinction between gathering and other patterns remains consequential even when simultaneous activations are excluded. Navarra and Piselli [NP25] study RR gathering on anonymous unoriented rings without multiplicity detection, characterize solvability from both arbitrary and distinct initial positions, and give asymptotically optimal algorithms. Terai, Wada, and Katayama [TWK23] investigate gathering with lights under several schedulers, including RR, relating scheduling assumptions to the robots’ available persistent state. Together, these works examine how sequential activation interacts with geometry, multiplicity detection, and memory; our focus is its interaction with Byzantine faults. 

Other gathering models and objectives. Bramas and Tixeuil [BT15] study wait-free gath-ering without chirality in the presence of crash faults. Crash failures permanently stop robots, whereas Byzantine robots may continue moving adversarially, so crash-tolerance guarantees do not directly imply Byzantine tolerance. Byzantine convergence , which only requires the distances between correct robots to tend to zero, admits stronger resilience guarantees than those currently known for RR gathering. For robots on a line, Bouzid, Potop-Butucaru, and Tixeuil [BPT10] establish tight thresholds 

n > 2f under FSYNCH and n > 3f under bounded asynchronous scheduling. Without a bound on scheduling, [BPT09] gives an asynchronous convergence algorithm for n > 5f and a matching lower bound for cautious algorithms. Related convergence impossibilities have also been verified in Coq [ABC +13]. Location functions for Byzantine-tolerant swarms support tasks such as convergence, marching, and exploration under FSYNCH with shared coordinates [ADK +23]. These results concern objectives or capabilities different from finite-time gathering by oblivious robots without coordinate agreement. Byzantine gathering has also been studied for mobile agents in graphs. In this setting, agents typically have labels and memory, communicate when colocated, and move in synchronous rounds. With a known Byzantine bound f , Dieudonné, Pelc, and Peleg [DPP14] achieve gather-ing with termination even when Byzantine agents can forge labels, requiring at least 2f +1 correct agents if the graph size is known and 4f + 2 when no upper bound on it is known. Bouchard, Dieudonné, and Ducourthial [BDD16] reduce these requirements to the optimal thresholds of 

f + 1 and f +2 , respectively. [DPP14] also establishes tight bounds for weakly Byzantine agents, which cannot forge labels: any positive number of correct agents suffices when the graph size is known, whereas f + 2 are necessary and sufficient when no upper bound on the graph size is known. Hirose, Nakamura, Ooshita, and Inoue [HNOI22] study the tradeoff between resilience and gathering time for weakly Byzantine agents. They give polynomial-time algorithms as-suming an upper bound on the graph size and at least 4f 2 + 8 f + 4 correct agents; [HNOI24] reduces this requirement to 7f + 7 while retaining polynomial running time and simultaneous termination. Hirose, Eguchi, and Sudo [HES25] study self-stabilizing perpetual gathering under weak Byzantine faults: from arbitrary initial states, correct agents must eventually remain to-gether, although they may keep moving together. These graph-based results concern a different 3computational model and do not directly yield algorithms for anonymous oblivious LCM robots. 

1.2 Our Contribution 

We consider deterministic gathering by anonymous, oblivious robots under RR scheduling, as-suming rigid movement and strong multiplicity detection. We prove that our algorithm ToMax 

achieves gathering whenever f ≤ ⌊ √n/ 2⌋, without knowing f . This raises the previously known resilience guarantee from one Byzantine robot to Θ( √n) Byzantine robots and estab-lishes ψ(n) = Ω( √n).We briefly outline our analysis, which exploits the fixed cyclic activation order. After its first full activation, each maximal block of consecutive correct robots behaves as a single group. If such a block contains more than 2f robots and moves, it creates a multiplicity advantage that the Byzantine robots cannot erase before all correct robots gather. Thus, every block that moves infinitely often has size at most 2f . A separate potential argument shows that all correct robots that eventually stop moving must share the same final position. Combining these observations with a counting argument, we show that, when n ≥ 4f 2, any execution in which some correct robots move infinitely often would have a strict majority of robots eventually stationary at a common point. This majority forces all remaining correct robots to gather there, yielding a contradiction. Hence, all correct robots eventually stop moving at a common point. We also give a self-contained proof of the general upper bound ψ(n) ≤ ⌊ (n − 1) /2⌋ and construct a repeating execution showing that the resilience of ToMax is at most ⌊(n + 1) /3⌋.A substantial gap remains between the square-root lower bound and these linear upper bounds. Determining the asymptotic order of ψ(n), and in particular whether RR can tolerate a constant fraction of Byzantine robots, remains open. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines the model and resilience. Section 3 presents ToMax and proves the lower bound, while Section 4 proves the upper bounds. Section 5 concludes the paper. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

For integers a and b, we write [a, b ] = {a, a + 1 , . . . , b }. For a multiset B and an element b ∈ B,let mB (b) denote the multiplicity of b in B, that is, the number of occurrences of b in B.We consider n robots r0, r 1, . . . , r n−1 moving in the Euclidean plane R2. The robots are oblivious, identical and anonymous. Throughout the paper, robot indices are taken modulo n

whenever this is clear from the context; for example, rt = rt mod n. Exactly f ∈ [0 , n − 1] robots are Byzantine , and the remaining n − f robots are correct .We assume a round-robin scheduler: at each time t = 0 , 1, 2, . . . , only robot rt is activated and executes the Look–Compute–Move cycle described below. Let pt(r) denote the position of robot r immediately before the Look phase at time t, expressed in a global coordinate system used only for the analysis. The robots have no direct access to this global coordinate system. We adopt the variable disorientation model: the robots do not share a coordinate system, and even the local coordinate system of an individual robot may change adversarially between activations through rotation, reflection, and uniform scaling. Definition 1 (Gathering) . Let n ≥ 2 and 0 ≤ f < n be integers. An algorithm A solves the (n, f )-gathering problem if, for every initial configuration, every choice of the f Byzantine robots, every behavior of those robots, and every choice of the coordinate transformations Lt,all correct robots executing A gather at a single point in finite time and remain there forever. 

Definition 2 (Resilience) . For an integer n ≥ 2, let ψ(n, A) be the largest integer k ∈ { 0, . . . , n −

1} such that A solves the (n, f )-gathering problem for every integer 0 ≤ f ≤ k. If no such k

exists, set ψ(n, A) = −1. We define ψ(n) = max A ψ(n, A), where the maximum ranges over all possible algorithms.

# 5 Conclusion 

We showed that ToMax solves deterministic gathering under RR scheduling whenever f ≤⌊√n/ 2⌋, assuming oblivious robots, rigid movement, and strong multiplicity detection. This extends the previously known resilience guarantee from a single Byzantine robot to a number that grows with the system size. A substantial gap remains between our square-root lower bound and the linear upper bounds, both for ToMax and for the optimal resilience ψ(n). In particular, whether RR can tolerate a constant fraction of Byzantine robots remains open.
