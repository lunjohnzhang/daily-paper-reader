# A heuristic for optimal coordination of autonomous vehicles: real-time algorithms 

Saugat Shahi, Jonathan Frey, Filippo Fabiani, Moritz Diehl and Mario Zanon 

Abstract —When coordinating connected and automated vehi-cles at an unsignalized intersection, the crossing order and the vehicle trajectories must be decided jointly. For a fixed crossing order, the problem can be formulated conveniently in a bi-level form where each vehicle solves its own optimal control problem, whose optimal value is a function of its entry and exit times, and a coordinator optimizes these times subject to precedence constraints. We compute the first and second order sensitivities of lower-level problems, which are required to solve the upper-level problem, by means of algorithmic differentiation using the real-time software package acados . This implementation exploits tailored algorithms to obtain a computationally efficient imple-mentation of the optimal coordination algorithm. We validate our implementation in numerical simulations that demonstrate both real-time feasibility and effectiveness of our algorithm. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

At an unsignalized intersection, connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) travel through the conflict zone directly, which can reduce delay, energy consumption and collision risk [1], [2]. Coordinating them requires two decisions: a discrete crossing order and the continuous trajectories opti-mization. Together they form a mixed-integer nonlinear pro-gram (MINLP) [3], [4], and deciding optimal crossing order is combinatorially hard [5]. Existing work therefore fix the schedule, such as first-come-first-served reservation [6] or static priorities [7], but their solutions far from optimal for heterogeneous fleets [8]. For a fixed crossing order, the problem can be decomposed into an upper-level nonlinear program (NLP) that assigns occupancy slots of the intersection and independent optimal control problems (OCPs), one per vehicle [3], [9], [10]. The semi-distributed sequential quadratic programming (SQP) and interior-point schemes of [10], [11] build on this structure and solve only a small quadratic program (QP) centrally. Similar formulations for a fixed schedule are found in [12]–[22]. Choosing the crossing order remains the primary challenge. Exact methods formulate it with integer variables and use mixed-integer programming [4], [23], [24], tree search [25], branch-and-bound [26] or dedicated sequencing algorithms [27], but scales poorly. Heuristics instead improve a given order by swapping adjacent vehicles [24], [28]. In our previous study, the Lagrange multipliers associated with the precedence constraints quantify the cost related to the crossing order. They rank the critical vehicle pairs discussed in [8], which retrieves a significant portion of the optimality gap found in first-in-first-out. The extension in [29] incorporates this prioritization into a model predictive control (MPC) framework [30] that retains a history of performed swaps. Nevertheless, each swap is verified through a complete re-solving with a general-purpose NLP solver [31], [32], which prevents realtime operation as the number of vehicles increases. Parametric sensitivity analysis is an alternative to handle these re-solves. Under standard assumptions, the solution of a parametric NLP is differentiable in the parameter, and its derivatives follow from the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) system [33], [34]. A second-order solver factorizes this system anyway, so first- and second-order derivatives of the optimal value come at little extra cost [34], [35]. The same idea un-derlies real-time iteration schemes [30], [36] and the solution sensitivities of acados [37], [38], which builds on HPIPM 

[39] and CasADi [32]. This paper cast the coordination problem of [8], [29] in bi-level form, in which each vehicle solves its own acados 

OCP and its optimal value is a function of its crossing times. Our contributions are fourfold. First, we compute the gradient of these value functions from the multipliers of the crossing conditions and their Hessian from the acados 

solution sensitivities. Second, we use them in a prediction QP that estimates the cost change of a candidate swap. Only swaps predicted to improve are confirmed by a re-solve, which replaces most of the full re-solves of [8], [29]. Third, we solve the upper level by SQP with a dense or a structured HPIPM 

solver. Fourth, we compare the solutions of centralized and decentralized formulation and evaluate real time feasibility in closed loop simulation with computation delay against controllers in section IV-D. Section II presents the centralized formulation and the multiplier-guided schedule refinement, Section III the decen-tralized formulation and the sensitivity-based method, Sec-tion IV the numerical results, and Section V concludes the paper.

D ECENTRALIZED FORMULATION 

We now discuss why the centralized problem (4) is reformu-lated, by stating the three bottlenecks of Algorithm 1. First, the structure of the problem is not exploited: the cost and the constraints are separable across vehicles, only coupling between the vehicles is precedence constraints, yet (4) is solved as a single NLP. Second, the computation is centralized: the trajectories of all vehicles are optimized centrally, which requires the full state information of every vehicle. Third, every candidate swap is evaluated at line 12 by a complete re-solve of (4), and the memory T bounds the number of such evaluations but not their cost. We address these bottlenecks by reformulating the problem in a bi-level form first proposed in [10], in which each vehicle solves its own optimal control problem and only its crossing times are exchanged with a coordinator, and by exploiting the sensitivities of the value functions to replace expensive re-solves at line 12 by the solution of a QP.

The expression (11) requires the sensitivities dwi/dti and

constraint qualification, strict complementarity and the second-order sufficient conditions [33], [40]. Then, by the implicit function theorem applied to the KKT conditions of (5), the primal-dual solution is locally a differentiable function of ti,and its derivative is the solution of a linear system whose matrix is the KKT matrix at the solution and whose right-hand side is the derivative of the KKT residual with respect to ti [33], [34], [38]. F. Real-time scheduling iteration 

Algorithm 2, though computationally cheaper than Algo-rithm 1, still relies on complete SQP solves: one for the replanning and one for every confirmation. For closed-loop simulation with a fixed sampling time we also consider a real-time scheduling variant based on the idea of the real-time iteration [36]. At every sampling instant, the derivatives (10) and (11) are evaluated at the current lower-level solutions, which are available from the vehicles’ own MPC controllers. Only a single full SQP step (8) is taken for the current schedule, and one prediction QP (14) is solved for the most promising pair not in T . If this pair is predicted to improve, the swapped schedule is accepted together with the step of its prediction QP. A swap is accepted based on the prediction alone, and the confirmation of Algorithm 2 is replaced by the next sampling instant, which linearizes the problem again at the new schedule and updates the multiplier ranking. The computation per sampling instant consists of one derivative evaluation and two QPs, independently of the number of 7

candidate swaps, so its computation time varies little between sampling instants. IV. N UMERICAL RESULTS

C. Dense versus structured upper-level QP 

Fig. 6 compares the two HPIPM formulations of Sec-tion III-B on upper-level QPs with M = 2 to 512 vehicles. The structured formulation scales as M 1.03 , in line with the linear cost of the Riccati recursion, while the dense one scales as M 2.85 . The dense formulation is faster for few vehicles. In each interior-point iteration, it factorizes a single matrix of size 2M , which is cheap when M is small. The structured formulation instead runs a Riccati recursion over M stages, and each stage requires its own operations on matrices of size at most 2. For few vehicles, this fixed work per stage costs more than the single dense factorization, whose cost grows as 

M 3. The two curves cross at ¯M ≈ 39 , and at M = 512 the structured formulation is 121 times faster. The implementation therefore uses the dense formulation for M < ¯M and the structured one otherwise.

We have presented a sensitivity-based SQP method for the coordination of CAVs at an intersection, built on the bi-level formulation of the coordination problem. The gradient and the Hessian of each vehicle’s value function are ob-tained analytically from the acados solution sensitivities. With these derivatives, the bi-level fromulation yields the same crossing orders as the centralized formulation in 100 

randomized scenarios, and a single prediction QP predicts the cost change of a candidate swap with the correct sign in every confirmed case. The structured HPIPM formulation makes the upper-level QP scale linearly with the number of vehicles. In closed loop with a fixed computation delay, the real-time variant is the only controller that decides within the 

100 ms sampling period, and its cost is 19 .7% above that of the ideal controller without delay, whereas the controllers based on full re-solves lose 156 .3% and 197 .2% because of their latency. This work completes the line of research of [8], [29]: it retains the multiplier guided swapping heuristic, replaces the expensive re-solves with sensitivity-based QPs, and shows that, once the computation time is accounted for, a fast approximate decision is worth more than an exact but late one.
