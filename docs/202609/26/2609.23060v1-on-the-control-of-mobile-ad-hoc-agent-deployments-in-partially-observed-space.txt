Abstract. We study the online deployment of mobile ad hoc networks in unknown orthogonal environments, formalized as the Partially Ob-servable Cooperative Guard Art Gallery Problem. We give a full proof that CADENCE algorithms achieve full coverage while maintaining a connected visibility graph using at most n 

> 2

+ h − 2 agents in orthogonal worlds with n corners and h holes. We further evaluate deployment-order heuristics that reduce agent count and deployment time in practice. 

Keywords: Mobile ad hoc networks · Partially Observable Cooperative Guard Art Gallery Problem · Multi-agent deployment. 

## 1 Introduction 

Mobile ad hoc networks [1] (MANETs) are teams of mobile agents that estab-lish communication and sensing coverage without pre-existing infrastructure or knowledge of the environment. They arise in indoor, underground, and confined spaces such as disaster-stricken buildings, tunnels, and mines, where commu-nication infrastructure is unavailable, unreliable, or prohibitively expensive to deploy [1,6,12,13,16], and where obstacles and irregular geometry force agents to organize under limited range and line-of-sight conditions [10]. The combinato-rial core of such deployments, covering an environment with line-of-sight agents, is the Cooperative Guard Art Gallery Problem [17]. When the environment is dis-covered online rather than known in advance, it becomes the Partially Observable Cooperative Guard Art Gallery Problem (POCGAGP), introduced in [9]: agents enter an unknown environment, expand coverage monotonically, and preserve connectivity throughout. Rather than a single algorithm, we present World Coverage Algorithms (WCA), which for a given world W (see Definition 3), solves the POCGAGP (see Re-quirements 1,2,3,4) with its requirements of constant connectivity between the deployed agents, monotonically increasing coverage, and limiting its usage of resources (the number of agents and time to completion). This represents the deployment of a MANET to form a connected network over the whole space. arXiv:2609.23060v1 [cs.RO] 19 Sep 2026 2 E. Meriaux, L.-R. Langevin et al. 

WCA deploy agents onto points visible to the current network and may relocate them to adjacent visible points; both preserve connectivity, so on completion the agents cover W as one connected network. Members differ in their rules, and may reach different covering configurations. A key subclass of this algorithm class is the CADENCE (Centralized Agent Directed Exploration for Network Coverage) [9] Algorithm which deploys agents to valid 270 ◦ corners of an orthogonal world, yielding the worst-case bound  

> n
> 2

+ h − 2 for n corners and h holes, matching the best known bound for the Cooperative Guard Art Gallery Problem [17] under partial observability. We make three contributions. First, we introduce the class of World Coverage Algorithms, an exact classification of the algorithms that solve the POCGAGP. Second, we present a full proof of the n 

> 2

+ h − 2 bound for CADENCE, achieved despite the partial observability constraint (formalizing a proof already sketched in a previous work [9]). Third, we report an empirical study of over 10 ,000 tests comparing members of CADENCE with different deployment-order heuristics.

## 3 The Partially Observable Cooperative Guard Art Gallery Problem (POCGAGP) 

Consider the real-world deployment of a continuously connected MANET in an environment containing walls and obstacles that is initially unknown and incre-mentally being discovered. As the coverage expands, with a bounded number of agents and after a bounded amount of time, the objective is that every part of the environment is observed by some agent. Furthermore, for any time stamp 

t ≥ 0, if a part of the environment is seen at time t, then it must also be seen at time t + 1 . In this setting, there exists a deployment point from which agents spawn, which acts as a node in the MANET and enables communication with the outside world. The MANET satisfies the following assumption: 

Assumption 1 (Mutual visibility). The members of the MANET are equipped with sensors such that any two members x, y can directly communicate with each other if x observes y (and equivalently y observes x). 

The objective of the POCGAGP is to create a geometric representation of this MANET deployment and design efficient algorithms to fully discover the environment in which they are deployed. We represent such environments as subsets W of R2 (corresponding to worlds defined in the previous section), where the agents become points in W and the deployment point is denoted as xd ∈

W . If A ⊂ W represents our set of agents, then G(A ∪ { xd}) represents the MANET, where two members of the MANET communicating with each other are represented by points x, y ∈ W being in each other’s field of view ( x ∈ FW ({y}),or equivalently y ∈ FW ({x})), corresponding to Assumption 1. To properly represent the real-world MANET deployment and its constraints, the following requirements are added to the POCGAGP: 

Requirement 1 (Constant connectivity). At any point of the algorithm, the visibility graph G(A ∪ { xd}) formed by the deployed agents together with the deployment point must be connected. 

Requirement 2 (Increasing coverage). Coverage must evolve monotonically. That is, if A and A+ denote the positions of agents at consecutive time steps, then F (A ∪ { xd}) ⊆ F (A+ ∪ { xd}).

Requirement 3 (Agent budget). Given a world W , the number of deployed agents never exceeds some number Nmax (dependent on W ), representing the resource constraints of the real-world deployment. 

Requirement 4 (Time budget). Given a world W , the deployment must ter-minate within a finite time horizon Tmax (dependent on W ), representing the time constraints of the real-world deployment. Control of Mobile Ad-Hoc Agent Deployments 5

## 4 World Coverage Algorithms and CADENCE 

4.1 The Class of World Coverage Algorithms 

To approach the POCGAGP, we make use of a certain type of algorithm that we call World Coverage Algorithms (WCA) 3. Every such algorithm takes a world 

W and a deployment point xd ∈ W as inputs, and can be uniquely defined by its transition rule τ .This rule τ takes three arguments: the deployment point xd, a (finite) set 

A ⊆ W representing the agents currently allocated, and a (non-finite) set F ⊂ W

representing the field of view of A ∪ { xd}. Then, A+ := τ (xd, A, F) will be the (finite) set of allocated agents in the next step of the algorithm. It must always hold that A+ ⊆ F to ensure that no agent is ever allocated to a location that has not been discovered yet. To satisfy the four requirements stated in the previous section, τ must obey certain conditions. First, we require that G(A+ ∪ { xd}) is always connected (1), and that F (A ∪ { xd}) ⊆ F (A+ ∪ { xd}) always holds (2). Furthermore, given an input W , we require that |A +| ≤ Nmax (3), and that τ (Tmax )(xd, ∅, F (xd)) = W ,i.e. that after using the transition rule τ exactly Tmax times starting from the initial state (xd, ∅, F (xd)) , the world must be fully covered (4). In order to properly model the fact that the agents do not know the shape of their environment before observing it, this function τ has to act independently from the input W . This way, all the decisions that are made by the algorithms of WCA are based uniquely on the location of the deployment point xd, the current allocation A of the agents, and the set F of what is visible of W , and therefore cannot depend on parts of W that have not been observed yet. To help us understand how such algorithms are executed, we provide the following general template.

Setup. We use randomly generated discrete orthogonal worlds (grid worlds) at three sizes (as in [9]), 50 × 50 , 100 × 100 , and 250 × 250 , each with holes and a fixed deployment point xd. In total, 300 orthogonal worlds were generated, each repeated with 5 seeds (each world being run 5 times by each algorithm). Each run is capped by an agent budget Nmax = n 

> 2

+ h − 2 (the worst-case CADENCE bound) and a step budget Tmax , set to 5,000 , 10 ,000 , and 30 ,000 (as in [9]) selection steps for the three resolutions respectively; a run that reaches either cap before full coverage is recorded as non-converged. 

Process. To simulate real-life MANETs, we run CADENCE by deploying agents from xd, they walk one square at a time (step by step) towards their allocated valid corner, and the total number of steps they take is denoted "Steps" (see Table 1). Throughout this process, we call an agent de-allocated [9] if its removal would not affect coverage or connectivity. Then, we may re-allocate it in subsequent iterations instead of deploying a new agent. Once W is finally covered (i.e. F (A) = W ), the nb. of agents in W is denoted "Max", and this number minus the nb. of de-allocated agents is denoted "Final" (see Table 1). 

Selection rules. rand_point picks a uniformly random visible valid corner. 

max_dist picks the visible valid corner that maximizes the minimal distance to the deployment point and to any de-allocated agent, while min_dist minimizes that minimal distance. most_edge picks the visible valid corner that sees the most frontier cells (the visible cells with a non-visible neighbor), while least_edge 

picks the one that sees the least. On the other hand, ISDA [4] could deploy agents anywhere in the current field of view, not only at corners. There are two variants: 

ISDA edge randomly selects a frontier cell, while ISDA any randomly selects any visible cell. 

Results. The CADENCE variants trade off along minimizing the aforemen-tioned metrics Final, Max, and Steps, and no single selection rule wins on all of them. See Table 1 for full details of the results. First, ISDA any does worse than least_edge on every metric, and least_edge 

is never the best performing rule on any metric. This is what we expected, since these rules do not pick points according to any minimizing strategy. Next, min_dist and ISDA edge have similar performances on Steps and Max and are the two best selection rules for these two metrics, but min_dist overtakes 

ISDA edge on every metric as the world size grows. Indeed, for 50 2, 100 2, and 

250 2 sized worlds, min_dist performs better for Final by 15 .5% , 19 .8% , and 

24 .3% and for Max by 0.6% , 9.5% , and 15 .2% , respectively. Meanwhile, ISDA edge performs better on Steps by 18 .7% , 7.6% , and −0.4% , respectively, meaning that min_dist eventually overtakes ISDA edge on every metric for bigger worlds. Finally, most_edge , max_dist , and rand_point have very similar perfor-mances on every metric, and are the best at minimizing Final. These results were surprising to us, since such rules tend to try expanding the field of view of the agents as fast as possible, therefore leaving less room for de-allocation. ## 6 Conclusion 

This paper studies the WCA that solve the POCGAGP. We give a full proof that CADENCE, a subclass of WCA, achieves full coverage of orthogonal worlds with at most n 

> 2

+ h − 2 agents despite the partial observability constraint, matching the best known bound for the fully observable setting. Our empirical evaluation of deployment-order heuristics shows that the strongest CADENCE variants outperform the ISDA baseline on every metric as world sizes grow.
